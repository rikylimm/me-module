Heterogeneous reaction, immobilisation and reactor choice

Week content

The week where a chemical engineering degree needs no translation at all, and the one place in this course where the biology is the easy part. A catalyst you can retain is a catalyst you can starve, and the whole content is the arithmetic that decides how big the particle may be. Three texts give the same problem three different ways and it is worth seeing why: Doran's generalised Thiele modulus divides by three for a sphere, Plawsky's divides by three inside the square root, and Shuler's does not divide at all, so the same bead can have a modulus of 0.7, 0.7 or 2.1 depending on whose definition you picked up. The number that matters is not the modulus but the effectiveness factor, and the safe route — the one that works when you do not know the intrinsic kinetics, which is almost always — is Weisz's observable modulus, which is built from the rate you measured rather than the rate you assumed.

Core ideas

1. Immobilisation is five chemistries and one trade, stated as what each costs

Shuler §3.4.1 lists the methods and Doran §12.1 the reasons; put them in the order of what they do to activity. Adsorption is cheap, reversible and leaks — the enzyme comes off with ionic strength or shear, so the catalyst has a half-life rather than a lifetime. Covalent attachment does not leak but reacts with residues that may be in the active site, so activity retention is typically a fraction rather than all of it, and the fraction has to be measured per enzyme. Entrapment in a gel — alginate, carrageenan, polyacrylamide — retains activity well because the protein is never chemically touched, and introduces the diffusion problem that is the rest of this week; it also leaks anything smaller than the mesh, which for an enzyme means slow loss and for a whole cell means none. Encapsulation behind a membrane is entrapment with a harder cut-off and a worse mass-transfer coefficient. Cross-linked enzyme aggregates dispense with the carrier entirely, so the catalyst is all catalyst rather than 95% support, which is the single largest gain available on a cost-per-kilogram basis and the reason they exist. Whole-cell flocculation and biofilm are the free versions, and the biofilm in particular is immobilisation you get whether you designed it or not. The trade across all five is the same one: everything that makes the catalyst easier to retain makes the substrate harder to deliver.

2. The shell balance, done once, in the geometry that matters

Take a sphere of radius R, effective diffusivity 𝒟_Ae, and a reaction consuming A at rate r_A per unit catalyst volume. A balance on a shell between r and r + Δr at steady state gives 𝒟_Ae (d²C_A/dr² + (2/r) dC_A/dr) = r_A, with dC_A/dr = 0 at r = 0 by symmetry and C_A = C_As at r = R. Doran §12.3 carries this through analytically for first and zero order; Plawsky §5.5.1 does the same balance in slab geometry for a first-order reaction, with the coupled energy balance alongside it, which is the better place to see the method because nothing is hidden in the Laplacian. For first order and a sphere the solution is the familiar hyperbolic-sine profile; the substitution u = r·C_A turns the equation into a constant-coefficient one and that is the whole trick. Do it by hand once. The reason to do it by hand is that the boundary condition at r = R is the one every real system breaks — C_As is not the bulk concentration, it is the surface concentration, and Doran says plainly that surface concentrations are very difficult to measure accurately, which is why §12.5 exists and why the external film cannot be dismissed as a refinement.

3. Three Thiele moduli for one bead, reconciled

Doran's generalised modulus is φ = (V_p/S_x)·r_A|C_As / √(2∫𝒟_Ae r_A dC_A), with V_p/S_x = R/3 for a sphere and b for a flat plate, which for first order collapses to φ₁ = (R/3)√(k₁/𝒟_Ae). Plawsky's Table 8.1 gives the spherical pellet modulus as √(k″r_o²/9𝒟_eff), which is the same number written differently — √(r_o²/9) is r_o/3 — and the planar one as √(k″L²/𝒟_eff) with L = V_p/A, also the same. The two books agree exactly. Shuler §3.4.2 defines it as φ = R√(V_m″/K_m 𝒟_e), with no division by three, so Shuler's modulus is three times Doran's and Plawsky's for the same sphere; Shuler's effectiveness-factor expression, η = (3/φ)(1/tanh φ − 1/φ), carries the compensating factor of three, so the physics is identical and only the bookkeeping differs. This is worth stating because the two conventions produce different-looking rules of thumb for the same bead: 'limitation begins above φ = 0.3' in Doran's convention is 'above φ ≈ 1' in Shuler's. Plawsky's Eq. (8.74) as printed in this PDF reads φ_p = k″L²/𝒟_eff, which would be the square of the modulus; the radical does not extract from the file, and Table 8.1 shows it, so take the table.

4. The effectiveness factor, and how much of the curve you actually need

η_i is the observed rate over the rate that would occur at C_As everywhere, so η_i = 1 means no internal limitation and η_i < 1 measures it. For first order and a sphere, η_i1 = (1/φ₁²)(3φ₁ coth 3φ₁ − 1); for a flat plate, tanh φ₁/φ₁; Plawsky's cylindrical case is I₁(2φ)/[φ I₀(2φ)]. Doran's Figure 12.8 and Plawsky's Figure 8.17 plot all three and they coincide exactly in both asymptotes and fall within 10-15% between, which is the practical result: geometry barely matters once you use V_p/S_x as the length scale. Two asymptotes carry most of the engineering. Below φ₁ = 0.3, η_i1 = 1 and the catalyst is working at full rate. Above φ₁ = 10, η_i1 ≈ 1/φ₁ for all geometries, which means the observed rate scales as 1/R — halve the bead and you double the rate, exactly, with no curve-reading required. Zero order is different in kind rather than degree: η_i0 = 1 for all φ₀ ≤ 0.577 in a sphere and ≤ 1 in a flat plate, because the rate does not depend on concentration until the concentration hits zero, at which point part of the particle switches off entirely. Doran's own warning is worth carrying: because of the errors in estimating the parameters that define φ, the effectiveness-factor curves should be viewed as diffuse bands rather than precise functions.

5. Weisz's observable modulus, which is the one to reach for first

The problem with everything above is that φ needs k₀, k₁ or V_max and K_m, and Doran §12.9 is explicit that those are hard to get for biological systems — and that an immobilised catalyst reports apparent values, not intrinsic ones, so measuring them on the bead is circular. Weisz's modulus removes the circularity: Φ = (V_p/S_x)²·r_A,obs/(𝒟_Ae C_As), which for a sphere is (R/3)²·r_A,obs/(𝒟_Ae C_As), and every quantity in it is measured. The criteria are flat and apply to all geometries and all kinetics: below Φ = 0.3, η_i = 1 and internal limitation is insignificant; above Φ = 3, η_i is substantially below 1; between 0.3 and 3 you have to look closer. The relation back to the conventional modulus is Φ = φ₁²η_i1 for first order and Φ = 2φ₀²η_i0 for zero order, which is how Doran's Figure 12.11 is constructed. What Φ cannot do is decide a Michaelis-Menten case cleanly, because the curve depends on β = K_m/C_As and that needs K_m — but the first- and zero-order curves bracket it, so you can always bound η_im from above and below without knowing K_m at all. That bracketing is the single most useful operational fact in the chapter.

6. The external film, which adds as a resistance and is checked the same way

Doran §12.5 defines the matching observable modulus for the film, Ω = (V_p/S_x)·r_A,obs/(k_s C_Ab), sphere (R/3)·r_A,obs/(k_s C_Ab), and the result that makes it usable: C_As/C_Ab = 1 − Ω. So Ω is literally the fractional drop across the boundary layer, Ω ≪ 1 means the film is irrelevant, and Ω approaching 1 means the film is delivering nothing. External and internal effectiveness multiply, η_T = η_i η_e, with η_e1 = C_As/C_Ab for first order and η_e0 = 1 for zero order — the zero-order case being the trap, because η_e0 = 1 does not mean there is no gradient across the film, only that the rate does not care about it until the surface concentration reaches zero. Plawsky §8.4.1 gives the same physics in series-resistance form, 1/η_g = 1/η_p + φ_p²/Bi_m, with the mass-transfer Biot number Bi_m = k_c λ_v/𝒟_eff, which is the more transferable way to see it: internal and external resistances add as reciprocals, so the larger one dominates and improving the smaller one does nothing. The order of operations in practice is therefore Ω first, then C_As, then Φ, then η_i — which is exactly the sequence of Doran's Example 12.5 and of this week's anchor.

7. Apparent kinetics: why the bead reports a K_m that is not its K_m

Shuler §3.4.2 states it plainly: under internal diffusion limitation, V_m,app and K_m,app are not true intrinsic constants. The mechanism is the shape of the curve rather than any change to the enzyme. At low bulk substrate the interior is depleted and the reaction is close to first order, so the observed rate is depressed by η_i; at high bulk substrate the interior is saturated, η_i rises toward 1, and the observed rate approaches the intrinsic V_max. Fitting Michaelis-Menten to that titration therefore returns a K_m larger than the true one — the apparent half-saturation point moves right because low concentrations are penalised more than high ones — with V_m,app roughly unchanged if saturation is reached. The same mechanism explains why diffusion masks substrate inhibition: if the interior never sees the bulk concentration, it never sees the inhibitory concentration either, so an immobilised preparation can look well-behaved over a range where the free enzyme is inhibited. That is a real process advantage and it is routinely mistaken for the immobilisation having stabilised the enzyme. Shuler's remedy is the right one and the only one: to get intrinsic constants, eliminate the resistances — small particles, high turbulence, high substrate — and measure there.

8. Oxygen in a pellet, and the √3 result

The fungal pellet is the same problem with the substrate changed to oxygen, and the numbers come out somewhere surprising. For zero-order consumption, the slab penetration depth before the concentration reaches zero is δ = √(2𝒟_Ae C_As/k₀). For a sphere, Doran's zero-order criterion says the core first goes anaerobic when the observable modulus reaches 0.667, i.e. at R_crit = 3√(0.667 𝒟_Ae C_As/k₀). Divide one by the other and the kinetics cancel completely: R_crit = √3 · δ. The critical radius of a sphere is 1.73 times the penetration depth of a slab, for any diffusivity, any surface concentration and any zero-order rate. That is worth having because it means one slab calculation sizes any pellet. Put numbers on it with Doran's measured alginate figure 𝒟_Ae = 1.88 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹, air-saturated water at 7.0 g O₂ m⁻³, a specific uptake of 3 mmol O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ and 100 kg dry weight per m³ of pellet: δ = 99 µm and R_crit = 172 µm. Lee §20.7's 600 µm A. niger pellet has a radius of 300 µm, so it is already well past the threshold — its core is anaerobic, and that is not a defect of that pellet but a property of every pellet anyone has ever grown. Which reframes §20.7's morphology engineering: chitin-synthase downregulation raising citric acid and penicillin titres 40%, cell-cycle genes raising malate about 50%, and a hyperbranching Tet-on glaA strain secreting 400% more glucoamylase are not fixes for the diffusion problem. They are ways of changing the ratio of active shell to dead core, which is the only variable the organism offers.

9. Reactor configuration follows from the particle, not the other way round

Doran §13.2 is the chapter's configuration section and §13.5 the comparison of ideal operation; the choice is decided by three numbers you now have. Retention: a stirred tank with a settler or screen retains the catalyst but subjects it to the full impeller shear, and gel beads attrit; a packed bed retains perfectly and shears not at all, but the pressure drop goes as 1/d_p² in the Blake-Kozeny regime, so the bead size that the effectiveness factor wants is the one the pump bill does not. A fluidised bed splits the difference and buys back the channelling risk. A membrane reactor retains the catalyst absolutely and gives you the worst mass-transfer coefficient of the four. Oxygen: a packed bed cannot be aerated along its length, so an aerobic immobilised system in a packed bed is a sequence of progressively more anoxic zones — which is why aerobic immobilised processes are either fluidised, trickle beds, or not immobilised. Compressibility: Stanbury's filtration chapter makes the point for filter cakes and it applies identically here — a soft particle under its own bed weight closes the voidage it needs, so the pressure drop of a tall gel-bead column is not the one the correlation predicts at zero load.

Worked anchor

How big may the bead be, and what does immobilisation cost at that size? An oxygen-limited whole-cell system, costed in the order Ω → C_As → Φ → η → loading → productivity.

Basis. Spherical gel beads of immobilised aerobic cells. Effective diffusivity of oxygen 𝒟_Ae = 1.88 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹, which is Doran's measured value for alginate. Bulk dissolved oxygen at air saturation and 30 °C, C_Ab = 7.0 g m⁻³. Liquid-solid mass-transfer coefficient k_s = 1 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹, Doran's value for a stirred vessel with neutrally buoyant beads. Specific oxygen uptake 3 mmol O₂ per g dry weight per hour, which is 0.096 g O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹.

Step 1 — the design as stated fails, and the failure is in the film. Try a 1 mm radius bead at the free-suspension cell density of 100 kg dry weight per m³. The volumetric uptake is k₀ = 0.096 × 100,000 / 3600 / 1000 = 2.67 × 10⁻³ kg O₂ m⁻³ s⁻¹. Then Ω = (R/3)·k₀/(k_s C_Ab) = (3.33 × 10⁻⁴)(2.67 × 10⁻³)/((1 × 10⁻⁵)(7 × 10⁻³)) = 12.7.

Ω = 12.7 means C_As/C_Ab = 1 − 12.7, which is negative, which means the film cannot deliver the oxygen at all. The bead cannot consume at that rate, so 'r_A,obs' was never an observable — the design is not slow, it is impossible. Setting Ω = 1 gives the radius at which the film saturates: R = 3 k_s C_Ab/k₀ = 3(1 × 10⁻⁵)(7 × 10⁻³)/(2.67 × 10⁻³) = 7.9 × 10⁻⁵ m, i.e. 79 µm. At full cell density the film alone caps the bead at 80 µm of radius, before any internal gradient is considered.

Step 2 — so fix the bead size and solve for the loading instead. Take R = 1.5 mm, a bead you can actually make, screen and retain. Require that the core stay aerobic: Doran's zero-order sphere criterion is Φ ≤ 0.667, so

k₀ ≤ 0.667 𝒟_Ae C_As/(R/3)² = 0.667(1.88 × 10⁻⁹)(7.0 × 10⁻³)/(5 × 10⁻⁴)² = 3.51 × 10⁻⁵ kg m⁻³ s⁻¹ = 126 g O₂ m⁻³ h⁻¹.

At 0.096 g O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ that is a loading of 126/0.096 = 1,320 g dry weight per m³ of bead, i.e. 1.3 g L⁻¹.

Step 3 — the film takes another quarter. Check Ω at that loading: Ω = (5 × 10⁻⁴)(3.51 × 10⁻⁵)/((1 × 10⁻⁵)(7 × 10⁻³)) = 0.25. So C_As = 0.75 C_Ab = 5.25 g m⁻³, not 7.0. Repeat step 2 with the correct surface concentration and the allowable loading falls to 0.99 g L⁻¹. Call it 1 g per litre of bead.

Step 4 — what that costs, stated as the comparison the week asks for. A free-cell fed-batch of the same organism runs at 30 to 50 g dry weight per litre. The immobilised bead at 1.5 mm runs at 1 g per litre of bead, and the bed is at best 60% beads, so 0.6 g per litre of reactor. That is a factor of 50 to 80 in catalyst density, paid for one thing: retention.

Step 5 — does retention pay it back? Retention buys dilution rates above µ_max, because the catalyst does not wash out. Compare volumetric rates. A chemostat at D = 0.4 h⁻¹ and 30 g L⁻¹ gives a biomass-hour product of 12 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹. A packed or fluidised bed at D = 5 h⁻¹ and 0.6 g L⁻¹ gives 3.0 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹. The ratio is 0.25. Retention does not pay it back — it recovers a quarter.

Step 6 — which is the answer, and it is a negative one with three named exceptions. For an aerobic whole-cell conversion, immobilisation in beads of a size you can handle loses a factor of four on volumetric productivity, and the loss is caused by oxygen, not by the substrate. It becomes the right answer when any of three things is true. (i) The reaction is anaerobic or the acceptor is not oxygen, in which case C_As is the substrate concentration — grams per litre rather than milligrams — and the same arithmetic with C_As = 10 g m⁻³ × 1,000 allows a loading three orders higher and the whole objection evaporates. This is why immobilised-cell processes in industry are overwhelmingly anaerobic. (ii) The catalyst is expensive and the retention is about catalyst cost rather than productivity, which is the enzyme case and the reason Artifact 31 is scoped around an enzyme. (iii) The product inhibits and continuous removal at high dilution is worth more than volumetric rate, which is the week 51 argument arriving early.

Step 7 — the sensitivity that decides whether to believe any of it. Every number above moves with 𝒟_Ae, which appears linearly in the loading limit, and 𝒟_Ae in a gel is the least well known quantity in the calculation. A liquid correlation from Plawsky §3.8 gives the free-solution diffusivity; the gel value is that multiplied by porosity and divided by tortuosity, and for alginate the published ratio ranges from about 0.5 to nearly 1. Taking the extremes, the allowable loading at R = 1.5 mm is between 0.5 and 1.0 g L⁻¹ — a factor of two, which is smaller than the factor of 50 the comparison turns on, so the conclusion survives the uncertainty. That is the right way round for a design calculation, and it is worth checking explicitly rather than assuming: the honest version of this anchor is not '1 g per litre' but 'between 0.5 and 1, and the decision does not depend on which'.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • effective diffusivity of oxygen in the gel taken as Doran's measured alginate value, 1.88 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹, for a gel that is not necessarily alginate
  • bulk dissolved oxygen 7.0 g m⁻³, water under air at 30 °C, with no correction for medium solutes
  • liquid-solid mass-transfer coefficient 1 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹, Doran's Example 12.5 value, with the chapter's own caveat that k_s is hard to determine for neutrally buoyant particles
  • specific oxygen uptake 3 mmol O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹, taken as independent of position inside the bead and of local oxygen concentration down to zero
  • oxygen consumption treated as strictly zero order, which is what makes Doran's Φ = 0.667 the anaerobic-core criterion; a Michaelis–Menten treatment would give a softer threshold
  • beads taken as uniform spheres of uniform cell loading, with no cell growth, no gradient in loading and no bead-size distribution
  • bed voidage 40%, so 60% of reactor volume is bead, when the volumetric comparison is made
  • the retention comparison assumes a packed or fluidised bed can be run at D = 5 h⁻¹ without channelling or attrition, which is the optimistic case for the immobilised side
  • porosity-and-tortuosity correction on 𝒟_Ae taken as between 0.5 and 1.0 of the free-solution value, which is the range the sensitivity step uses

Common misconceptions

  • That the Thiele modulus is a defined quantity. Three of the texts on this reading list define it three ways for the same sphere — Doran and Plawsky as (R/3)√(k/𝒟), Shuler as R√(V_m/K_m𝒟) — so a modulus quoted without its convention is a number without units. Quote the effectiveness factor instead, or quote Weisz's observable modulus, whose criteria (0.3 and 3) hold for every geometry and every kinetic order.
  • That a diffusion-limited bead reports a damaged enzyme. It reports a shifted curve: low bulk concentrations are penalised more than high ones, so the fitted K_m rises while V_max is roughly unchanged. The enzyme is intact. The same mechanism makes an immobilised preparation look resistant to substrate inhibition, because the interior never reaches the inhibitory concentration — a real process benefit routinely misattributed to stabilisation.
  • That zero-order kinetics means diffusion does not matter. It means the effectiveness factor is exactly 1 right up to the point where the concentration hits zero somewhere inside, and then part of the particle switches off completely — η_i0 = 1 for φ₀ ≤ 0.577 in a sphere and falls steeply after. For oxygen in a cell-loaded bead that threshold is at a radius of under 200 µm, which is smaller than any pellet or bead anyone works with, so the zero-order case is the one where the limitation is total rather than absent.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. At what bead radius does your system cross from reaction-limited to diffusion-limited, and which criterion did you use to decide?

A. Use Weisz's observable modulus, because it needs only measured quantities: Φ = (R/3)²r_A,obs/(𝒟_Ae C_As) for a sphere, with the crossing taken at Φ = 0.3 for the onset and Φ = 3 for substantial limitation, criteria that hold for all geometries and all kinetic orders. Solving for R at Φ = 0.3 gives R = 3√(0.3 𝒟_Ae C_As/r_A,obs). For the anchor's oxygen case — 𝒟_Ae = 1.88 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹, C_As = 7 g m⁻³, r_A,obs at 100 g dry weight per litre of bead — that is about 115 µm, and the stricter zero-order criterion for an anaerobic core, Φ = 0.667, gives 172 µm. Do not use the conventional Thiele modulus for this unless you have intrinsic kinetics measured on a particle small enough to be unlimited, because the constants fitted on a limited particle are apparent, not intrinsic, and using them is circular. And check Ω = (R/3)r_A,obs/(k_s C_Ab) before any of it: if Ω is not small, C_As is not C_Ab and the Φ you computed is wrong in the direction that flatters the design.

Q. Your immobilised preparation shows a K_m four times the free enzyme's and no substrate inhibition where the free enzyme is strongly inhibited. Two reviewers give two explanations. Which is right and how would you settle it?

A. Both observations have the same cause and it is not a change in the protein. Internal diffusion depresses the observed rate most at low bulk concentration, where the interior is most depleted relative to the surface, and least at high bulk concentration, where the interior saturates and η_i approaches 1; fitting Michaelis-Menten to that titration moves the apparent half-saturation point to the right. The same gradient means the interior never experiences the bulk concentration, so it never experiences the inhibitory concentration either, which is why the inhibition disappears. Settle it as Shuler §3.4.2 says: remove the resistances and measure again — crush or make much smaller particles, raise the turbulence to collapse the film, and work at high substrate — and if K_m returns to the free value, it was diffusion. A second and cheaper check is to vary only the particle radius: an intrinsic change in K_m is independent of R, an apparent one scales with it, and the direction of that dependence is unambiguous.

Q. A proposal immobilises an aerobic organism in 2 mm alginate beads at 30 g dry weight per litre of bead, to run a packed bed at high dilution. Give the quantitative objection.

A. Three numbers, in order. First the film: at 30 g L⁻¹ and 3 mmol O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ the volumetric demand is 8 × 10⁻⁴ kg m⁻³ s⁻¹, so Ω = (R/3)r/(k_s C_Ab) = (3.3 × 10⁻⁴)(8 × 10⁻⁴)/(10⁻⁵ × 7 × 10⁻³) = 3.8, which exceeds 1, so the boundary layer cannot deliver the stated rate at all and the design is not slow but impossible. Second the core: even if the film were removed, Φ for the interior at that loading is three orders above the 0.667 threshold, so all but an outer shell of about 100 µm is anaerobic — a 2 mm bead is 90% dead volume by that measure. Third the bed: a packed bed cannot be aerated along its length, so the oxygen that does arrive is consumed in the first centimetres and the rest of the column is an anoxic plug. The constructive version of the objection is to ask what the loading could be: at R = 1 mm, the aerobic-core criterion allows about 3 g per litre of bead, so the proposal is a factor of ten out, and the honest options are an anaerobic conversion, a fluidised or trickle bed, or free cells.
