Fermentation routes and redox balancing

Week content

Case thread: Which product mix is consistent with the reported pH, and what is missing? Open the case thread.

Anaerobic metabolism is a redox-balancing exercise with ATP as the prize. By Friday you should be able to write a product spectrum that balances electrons, detect what is missing from one that does not, and explain why fermentation persists in the presence of oxygen. The week closes phase 2, so budget two sessions for consolidating the pathway atlas into something you will actually reuse in phase 4.

Core ideas

1. The anaerobic pyruvate node, and the choice the cell makes there

Without a terminal electron acceptor, glycolytic NADH must be reoxidised by reducing something the cell made itself, and the options at the pyruvate node determine the product spectrum. Lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate directly, consuming one NADH and making no ATP beyond glycolysis. Pyruvate formate lyase cleaves pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and formate without redox chemistry, and the acetyl-CoA can then go to ethanol, consuming two NADH, or to acetate via phosphotransacetylase and acetate kinase, consuming none but making an extra ATP. So the cell trades ATP against redox: acetate is the ATP-rich, electron-poor option and ethanol the reverse, and the ratio it chooses is set by how much NADH needs disposing of. That is the whole logic of mixed-acid fermentation.

2. Mixed-acid fermentation, and why hydrogen matters to your balance

E. coli anaerobically produces a mixture: ethanol, acetate, formate, lactate, succinate, and from formate via formate hydrogen lyase, CO₂ and H₂. The hydrogen is the trap for a careless balance: it carries electrons and no carbon, so omitting it leaves carbon closing while electrons do not. Succinate is the other subtlety, since its formation fixes CO₂ and consumes NADH, making it an electron sink that also conserves carbon. When you see a published anaerobic product spectrum, check first whether gas was measured; a great many are reported without it and cannot be balanced as published.

3. Homolactic and heterolactic fermentation, with the numbers

Homolactic: glucose through the EMP pathway to 2 pyruvate, both reduced to lactate by LDH. Net 2 ATP, 2 lactate, no CO₂, and a theoretical mass yield of 1.0 g/g since 180 g of glucose becomes 180 g of lactate. Heterolactic: glucose through the phosphoketolase pathway to lactate plus ethanol plus CO₂, with 1 ATP per glucose and roughly half the mass yield. Homolactic is therefore the chassis for lactate, which is why lactate production uses homofermentative lactobacilli. The limit in practice is not stoichiometry but pH: the undissociated acid mechanism from week 2 sets a titre ceiling well before the carbon runs out, which is why these processes run neutralised and pay for it downstream.

4. Overflow metabolism: fermentation in the presence of oxygen

Above a critical growth rate, E. coli excretes acetate and yeast excretes ethanol even when oxygen is abundant. The modern explanation is proteome allocation, not any deficiency: respiration yields more ATP per glucose but requires more protein per unit of ATP flux, because the respiratory chain and TCA enzymes are expensive. Above a threshold flux the cell gets more ATP per gram of protein invested by fermenting part of the carbon, so it does. This is the week-1 growth laws cashed out, it explains the Crabtree effect and the Warburg effect by the same mechanism, and it is why proteome-constrained models in week 31 reproduce the acetate switch without any ad hoc bound. The process consequence is direct: suppress overflow by holding growth below the critical rate with a controlled feed, which is week 50's exponential feeding.

5. Redox balancing as a design constraint and as a diagnostic

Two uses, and you should practise both. As a constraint: when designing an anaerobic pathway, the NADH produced by glycolysis must equal the NADH consumed by your route plus any remaining sinks, or the design cannot carry flux — which is the stoichiometric basis of growth coupling in week 26, where you delete every sink but the product. As a diagnostic: given a measured product spectrum, compute carbon and electron closure separately and read the pattern. Carbon short with electrons complete implicates a carbon-bearing, electron-poor stream, almost always CO₂. Both short by a similar factor implicates biomass under-reporting. Both high by a similar factor implicates one over-reported stream. Electrons short with carbon complete implicates H₂ or another reduced gas.

Worked anchor

Close a mixed-acid balance, and name what is missing.

Per 100 mol glucose: 60 ethanol, 60 formate, 50 acetate, 20 lactate, 30 succinate.

Carbon in: 600 C-mol. Out: ethanol 120, formate 60, acetate 100, lactate 60, succinate 120 = 460 C-mol, so 77% recovery.

Electrons, using γ per C-mol (glucose 4.00, ethanol 6.00, acetate 4.00, lactate 4.00, succinate 3.50, formate 2.00): in 2,400. Out: ethanol 60×2×6 = 720; acetate 50×2×4 = 400; lactate 20×3×4 = 240; succinate 30×4×3.5 = 420; formate 60×1×2 = 120. Total 1,900, so 79%.

Both balances short by a similar factor points at a stream carrying both carbon and electrons in roughly biomass proportion — and indeed biomass is entirely absent from this spectrum. Assigning the missing 140 C-mol to biomass at γ = 4.2 supplies 588 electrons, which brings electrons to 2,488, slightly over. The small excess sits within the uncertainty of the biomass formula, and the residual can be trimmed by noting that succinate formation fixes CO₂, so some of the carbon came from outside glucose.

The method is the lesson: read the pattern of the two closures before you guess at a missing stream. Both short in proportion means biomass; carbon short alone means CO₂; electrons short alone means H₂.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the listed products are everything the analytics measured
  • γ per C-mol as tabulated
  • no biomass term in the balance
  • the sample is representative of the whole fermentation

Common misconceptions

  • That overflow metabolism is oxygen limitation. It happens with oxygen in excess, and the explanation is proteome efficiency — more ATP per gram of protein from fermentation than from respiration above a threshold flux.
  • That a product spectrum without gas measurement can be balanced. Formate, CO₂ and H₂ carry carbon and electrons in different proportions, and omitting them makes the balance uninterpretable rather than merely incomplete.
  • That homolactic fermentation's 1.0 g/g ceiling is reachable. The undissociated acid poisons the cell long before the carbon runs out, which is why the real constraint is pH tolerance.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Write the homolactic and heterolactic equations and compute ATP and mass yield for each.

A. Homolactic: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 CH₃CHOHCOOH, via EMP, net 2 ATP, no CO₂, mass yield 180/180 = 1.0 g/g. Heterolactic: C₆H₁₂O₆ → lactate + ethanol + CO₂, via phosphoketolase, net 1 ATP, mass yield roughly 90/180 = 0.5 g/g for lactate alone. Homolactic is therefore the lactate chassis, with twice the ATP and twice the yield; the trade is that homofermentative lactobacilli are nutritionally fastidious, so complex medium cost partly offsets the yield advantage.

Q. A product spectrum closes to 95% on carbon and 101% on electrons. What is missing and how do you know?

A. A carbon-bearing, electron-poor stream — almost certainly CO₂, because it carries carbon and no available electrons, so omitting it depresses carbon closure while leaving electrons intact or slightly high. Measure it by off-gas integration rather than inferring it. Had both balances been short by a similar factor the diagnosis would instead be under-reported biomass; had electrons alone been short, H₂ or another reduced gas.

Q. Why does a cell ferment when oxygen is available, and what does that imply for process control?

A. Because respiration yields more ATP per glucose but requires more protein per unit of ATP flux; above a critical flux the cell obtains more ATP per gram of protein invested by fermenting part of the carbon. It is a proteome allocation optimum, not a defect. The process implication is that overflow is controllable by rate rather than by oxygen: hold the specific growth rate below the critical value with an exponential or feedback-controlled feed, and the carbon stops going to acetate — which is why fed-batch exists and why simply adding more air does not fix acetate.
