FBA extensions: variability, loops and alternatives

Week content

One FBA solution is a hypothesis, not a result. The extensions exist because the feasible region is large, the optimum is frequently degenerate, and mass balance alone permits flux distributions that thermodynamics forbids. This is the week that teaches scepticism about your own output, which is what an interviewer is probing when they ask what FBA assumes.

Core ideas

1. Degeneracy, and why it is the normal case

If the objective vector is parallel to an edge or face of the polytope, every point along it is optimal. In real models this happens constantly, because many reactions appear in alternative routes of identical stoichiometric cost — a redox shuttle that can run through either of two carriers, an isozyme pair, a cycle with no net exchange. The consequence is that the particular flux vector a solver returns is one arbitrary point among many, chosen by the solver's internal pivot order rather than by biology. Reporting it as the flux distribution is the commonest misuse of FBA, and flux variability analysis exists to measure how arbitrary it was.

2. Flux variability analysis, and how to read its output

FVA maximises and minimises each reaction's flux subject to holding the objective at or near its optimum, giving an interval per reaction. Read the pattern rather than individual numbers. Reactions with a zero-width interval are fixed by the constraints: those are the real conclusions. Reactions with a wide interval are degenerate, and a reaction whose interval includes zero may or may not carry flux in vivo — the model cannot say. Running FVA at 100% and again at 90% of optimum tells you how flat the landscape is; a large increase in variability for a 10% objective relaxation means the optimum is a plateau, and so is barely an optimum.

3. Parsimonious FBA and the enzyme-cost argument

pFBA selects, among all optimal solutions, the one minimising the sum of absolute fluxes. The justification is that total flux is a rough proxy for total enzyme investment, so the parsimonious solution is the one a proteome-limited cell would prefer. It is a reasonable heuristic with two useful side effects: it removes most internal loops, since a loop adds flux without objective value, and it returns a single reproducible solution rather than an arbitrary one. It is not a thermodynamic guarantee, and it should be described as a heuristic rather than as a correction.

4. Loops: why mass balance permits the impossible

A set of reactions forming a closed cycle with no net exchange satisfies S·v = 0 at any loop flux, because every metabolite is produced and consumed within the loop. It is nonetheless impossible: Gibbs energy is a state function, so the sum of ΔrG′ around a closed loop is exactly zero, and a set of strictly negative values cannot sum to zero. Therefore at least one reaction in the loop must be running against its own gradient. The remedies are loopless FBA, which adds integer constraints forcing a thermodynamically consistent set of directions at the cost of a mixed-integer program, and thermodynamics-based flux analysis, which carries concentration variables and ΔrG′ constraints explicitly at the cost of needing data that does not exist for every metabolite.

5. Flux coupling, which tells you about structure rather than state

Two reactions are fully coupled if a non-zero flux in one implies a fixed ratio in the other, directionally coupled if one implies the other is non-zero, and partially coupled if the ratio is bounded. Coupling is a property of the network and the bounds, not of a particular solution, which makes it more durable than a flux distribution. Practically it identifies correlated reaction sets, which behave as single units for engineering purposes: deleting one member of a fully coupled set has the same effect as deleting any other, and no intervention can change their ratio.

6. MOMA and ROOM: what a cell does immediately after a knockout

Growth maximisation is an evolutionary argument and applies to an organism that has had time to adapt. A freshly constructed knockout has not. MOMA instead finds the flux distribution closest, in Euclidean distance, to the wild-type one, on the argument that regulation has not been retuned; ROOM minimises the number of reactions whose flux changes significantly, on the argument that regulatory changes are discrete. Both predict knockout phenotypes better than growth maximisation for unevolved strains, and growth maximisation recovers its accuracy after adaptive evolution — a result worth knowing because it is one of the field's cleanest validations of the optimality assumption's domain.

Worked anchor

Diagnose a wide FVA interval.

FVA at 100% of optimum returns a range of 0 to 22 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ for a reaction. Work through it rather than concluding the reaction is unimportant.

First, is it a loop? Run loopless FVA. If the range collapses to a narrow interval, the variability was a thermodynamically infeasible cycle and never meant anything.

Second, is there a cheaper alternative? Run pFBA and see whether the reaction carries flux in the parsimonious solution. If it does not, an enzymatically cheaper route exists and the cell plausibly uses that one.

Third, what is it coupled to? Compute flux coupling. If the reaction is in a correlated set with an alternative, you have found the specific competing route rather than a vague alternative.

Fourth, how flat is the optimum? Rerun FVA at 90%. If the range widens substantially, the model has a plateau rather than a peak, and the objective is barely discriminating.

Fifth, and only now: resolve it experimentally. A genuinely variable split between two routes is exactly what ¹³C labelling measures, which is why week 29 follows this one. The output of this analysis should be a stated experiment, not a conclusion.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the model structure is correct and the bounds are as set
  • FVA run at 100% of the optimum
  • the objective is the one the cell is assumed to optimise

Common misconceptions

  • That the flux distribution a solver returns is the flux distribution. It is one arbitrary point among many whenever the optimum is degenerate, which is usually.
  • That pFBA corrects loops. It removes most of them as a side effect of minimising flux; it guarantees nothing thermodynamically.
  • That a wide FVA range means a reaction is dispensable. It means the model cannot distinguish two routes of equal cost; the cell may use one exclusively.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Why does S·v = 0 permit a thermodynamically impossible loop, and what are the two principled remedies?

A. Mass balance constrains stoichiometry, not direction: a closed cycle produces and consumes each of its metabolites internally, so every row of S·v = 0 is satisfied at any loop flux. It is impossible because Gibbs energy is a state function, so ΔrG′ around a closed loop sums to exactly zero and strictly negative values cannot. The remedies are loopless FBA, adding integer constraints that force a consistent direction assignment, and thermodynamics-based flux analysis, carrying metabolite concentrations and ΔrG′ explicitly. pFBA removes most loops incidentally but guarantees nothing.

Q. When is MOMA the appropriate objective rather than growth maximisation, and what evidence supports the distinction?

A. For a freshly constructed knockout that has not had time to adapt, because growth maximisation assumes an evolutionary optimisation the strain has not undergone, whereas MOMA assumes regulation has not been retuned and the flux distribution stays close to wild type. The supporting evidence is that MOMA predicts unevolved knockout phenotypes better, while growth maximisation recovers accuracy after adaptive laboratory evolution — which both validates the optimality assumption and delimits where it applies.

Q. FVA at 100% of optimum gives a reaction a range of 0 to 20. Give the four checks before drawing any conclusion.

A. Run loopless FVA to test whether the variability is a thermodynamically infeasible cycle; run pFBA to see whether the reaction carries flux in the enzymatically cheapest solution; compute flux coupling to identify the specific competing route rather than a vague alternative; and rerun FVA at 90% of optimum to see whether the landscape is a plateau rather than a peak. If the range survives all four, the split is genuinely unresolved by the model and the next step is a ¹³C labelling experiment, not a conclusion.
