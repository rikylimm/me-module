Media, sterilisation and inoculum development

Week content

Case thread: Which process changes alter the cell environment, not only handling? Open the case thread.

The half of a fermentation that no paper reports and every plant lives with. Three decisions sit in this week — what the medium is made of, how it is sterilised, and how the inoculum gets to the vessel — and each is a cost against a risk rather than a yield against a ceiling. The habit from weeks 44-46 carries over unchanged: cost every option against what it buys. What is different here is that the arithmetic is unusually clean, because sterilisation kinetics are first order with an Arrhenius constant and the numbers are published. The single most useful thing in the week is that the del factor delivered during heat-up and cool-down is usually larger than the del factor the holding period was designed for, which means most hand-computed cycles are over-specified and destroy nutrients for nothing.

Core ideas

1. What the medium is for, and the four things that decide it

Stanbury Ch 4 lists the medium's duties and they are worth stating as a specification rather than a recipe: supply every element in the biomass, supply the carbon and electrons for the product, supply the energy for maintenance, and buffer the pH excursion the process creates. The elemental demand is not a judgement call — it comes straight out of the biomass formula you built in week 16 and Shuler §7.3.1 is the method: write CH1.8O0.5N0.2 (or your own measured formula), multiply by the target biomass, and that is the nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur bill before anything else is decided. Stanbury's chapter is organised by component — water, energy sources, carbon sources, nitrogen sources, minerals with chelators, growth factors, nutrient recycle, buffers, precursors and regulators, oxygen requirements, antifoams, medium optimization — and the four decisions that actually move cost are: complex against defined, the carbon source, whether anything must be fed rather than batched, and how much of the nitrogen is there for biomass you do not want. On that last point the arithmetic is brutal and is done in the anchor of week 52: nitrogen in the medium that produces biomass rather than product is a cost twice, once as the nitrogen and once as the oxygen to burn the carbon that went with it.

2. Complex against defined, costed rather than argued

The argument is usually had on grounds of reproducibility and that is the wrong axis to start on, because the difference in cost per kilogram of product is often larger than the difference in yield. Take Stanbury's own framing — factors influencing the choice of carbon source, and the examples under carbohydrates, oils and fats, hydrocarbons — and convert it. A defined medium on glucose at the week 45 price of $400 per tonne, at a biomass yield of 0.45 g g⁻¹ and a product yield of 0.30 g g⁻¹, costs 3.33 kg of glucose per kilogram of product, $1.33. A complex medium replacing part of the nitrogen with corn steep liquor or yeast extract typically raises titre and rate but adds a nitrogen line costing dollars rather than cents per kilogram, and the test is whether the productivity gain pays for it. The second axis is variability: Stanbury is explicit that complex sources are batch-variable, which is a validation cost in a regulated product and a control cost in any product, because every analytical method you built in phase 5 now has a matrix that changes between deliveries. The third is downstream, and it is the one most often forgotten — a complex medium puts colour, protein and particulates into the broth that week 51's train has to remove, and the clarification burden is a real line in the cost of goods rather than an inconvenience.

3. Thermal death is first order, and that is the whole of the design

Stanbury Ch 5 sets it out: −dN/dt = kN, integrating to ln(N₀/N_t) = kt, with N the total number of viable organisms in the batch rather than a concentration, because the minimum number needed to spoil a batch is one regardless of volume. Deindoerfer and Humphrey's design criterion is the del factor, ∇ = ln(N₀/N_t), and the temperature dependence is Arrhenius: k = A e^(−E/RT), so ln k against 1/T is a straight line. Two consequences follow immediately and both are stated in the chapter. First, sterility is unattainable in finite time, so the target is a probability — the fermentation industry's usual figure, which Stanbury attributes to Deindoerfer and Humphrey, Richards, Banks and Corbett, is one contaminated batch in a thousand, so N_t = 10⁻³. Second, the del factor is scale-dependent: N₀ is proportional to volume, so going from 1,000 to 10,000 dm³ at fixed risk raises the required del from 34.5 to 36.8. That is a rise of exactly ln 10, which is the whole of the scale effect and is worth seeing as such — the del factor grows as the logarithm of volume, so a thousand-fold scale-up costs only 6.9 more del.

4. Verified kinetics, and what they say about 121 °C

Doran's Example 13.8 supplies parameters you can use rather than assume: for bacterial spores, E_d = 283 kJ gmol⁻¹ and A = 5.7 × 10³⁹ h⁻¹. Put those through k_d = A e^(−E_d/RT) and the temperature ladder falls out, with D (the time for a one-log reduction, 2.303/k_d) beside it: 100 °C gives k_d = 1.38 h⁻¹ and D = 100 min; 110 °C gives 15.0 h⁻¹ and 9.2 min; 115 °C gives 47.0 h⁻¹ and 2.9 min; 121 °C gives 179 h⁻¹ and 0.77 min; 130 °C gives 1,228 h⁻¹ and 0.11 min; 140 °C gives 9,474 h⁻¹ and 0.015 min. Two things are worth reading off that ladder. The activation energy is high, so the rate rises by roughly a factor of ten for every 10 °C — which is why 121 °C is the autoclave temperature and why nothing useful happens at 100 °C. And Doran says plainly that cell death below about 100 °C is minimal, which is the licence to start the heat-up integral at 100 °C rather than at ambient. Doran also records, for direct steam sparging, that condensate typically adds 10-20% to the liquid volume; if you size the vessel from the medium volume and sterilise by injection, the fill is 10-20% larger than you planned.

5. Heat-up and cool-down are not a correction, they are usually most of the cycle

Doran §13.6.1 is explicit that cell death occurs at all times during the cycle, that holding periods are of the order of minutes while heating and cooling of large volumes take hours, and that t_hd can be minimised by accounting for the ramps. Stanbury's Ch 5 names the same calculation — 'calculation of the Del factor during heating and cooling' is its own named subsection, ahead of the holding-time one. The integral is ∫k_d dt over the ramp, and it cannot be done until the temperature profile is known; Deindoerfer and Humphrey's generalised profiles, reproduced in Doran's Table 13.3, give the form — hyperbolic for direct steam sparging, linear for electrical heating, exponential for heat transfer from isothermal steam through a coil — and linear cooling against a constant-temperature coolant. The anchor does the integral numerically for a linear ramp, and the result is the point of the week: a 60-minute heat-up from 100 to 121 °C delivers del 37.7 on its own, and a 45-minute cool-down delivers 28.3, against a requirement of 40.1 for 50 m³. The ramps over-deliver by a factor of e²⁶. Every minute of holding time computed without them is a minute of nutrient destruction bought for nothing.

6. The thing you are trading against is the medium, and it has its own Arrhenius constant

Deindoerfer and Humphrey's second criterion, which Stanbury gives as equation (5.11) onward, is the Nutrient Quality Criterion Q = ln(x₀/x_t) = k·t, built on exactly the same first-order-with-Arrhenius logic as the del factor, with the nutrient's own activation energy. Superimpose the two families of lines on ln t against 1/T and you have the continuous sterilisation performance chart of their 1961 paper, reproduced as Stanbury's Fig. 5.12: lines of constant del, lines of constant Q, and a safe operating region between the nutrient-in-excess bound and the nutrient-exhausted bound. The reason the chart slopes the way it does is the only thing you need to remember from it. Spore destruction has an activation energy around 283 kJ mol⁻¹; nutrient destruction is far lower, of the order of 100 kJ mol⁻¹ for vitamins and sugars, which is not a figure from the assigned reading and is flagged as an assumption in the anchor. Because the spore reaction is the more temperature-sensitive of the two, hotter and shorter always buys more sterility per unit of nutrient destroyed. That single inequality is the entire case for high-temperature short-time continuous sterilisation, and the anchor puts a number on it. Stanbury's own caveat is worth keeping: the approach only works if the limiting heat-labile nutrient has been identified and its Arrhenius parameters measured, which Banks points out is rarely true of a complex medium — so in practice the chart tells you which direction to move, not where to stop.

7. What has to be sterilised separately, and why the list is short but non-negotiable

Three mechanisms, and they are chemical rather than kinetic, so no temperature-time regime avoids them. Maillard reaction between reducing sugars and amino groups browns the medium, destroys lysine and arginine preferentially, and generates compounds that inhibit growth — which is why glucose and the nitrogen source are routinely sterilised apart and combined cold. Phosphate precipitates with calcium and magnesium at sterilisation temperature, removing both the phosphate and the divalent cations from solution, which is why phosphate is a separate addition and why chelators appear in Stanbury's component list at all. Vitamins and some amino acids are simply destroyed, which is why they are filter-sterilised; Stanbury notes that filtration is the method of choice for animal-cell media precisely because they are completely soluble and heat-labile, and Ch 5's filter-sterilization section covers media as well as air. The decision rule is not 'sterilise everything separately' — every separate stream is another vessel, another transfer and another aseptic connection, which is a contamination risk of its own. It is: separate the reducing sugar from the amino nitrogen, separate the phosphate from the divalent cations, filter what heat destroys, and heat the rest together.

8. The seed train is a generation count, and the generation count is a stability calculation

Stanbury Ch 6 gives the structure — criteria for the transfer of inoculum, then the development of inocula for yeast, bacterial and mycelial processes, then the aseptic inoculation of plant fermenters — and the criterion matters more than the stages. The usual inoculum ratio is 5 to 10% v/v, so each stage multiplies volume by 10 to 20 and the train from a 1 mL vial to 50 m³ is about seven to eight transfers. The number that actually matters is not the number of stages but the number of generations, because that is what week 33's plasmid-stability model takes as its argument. Volume from 1 mL to 50,000 L is a factor of 5 × 10⁷, which is log₂(5 × 10⁷) = 25.6 generations of net growth, plus whatever is spent re-entering exponential phase after each transfer. Twenty-six generations is a long time for an unselected plasmid: at a segregational loss rate per generation of 1%, the plasmid-free fraction at inoculation is 1 − 0.99²⁶ = 23%, and the production vessel is seeded with a population that is already nearly a quarter wrong. Stanbury's transfer criterion — transfer in late exponential phase, on a defined physiological state rather than a clock — is what keeps the lag short and therefore keeps the generation count near its theoretical minimum, and it is the only lever in the train that does not cost a vessel.

9. The contamination risk you accept, stated as a number rather than a posture

The week's fourth objective asks you to state the risk you accept and why, and the honest version of that is an expected-cost calculation rather than a safety factor. One batch in a thousand is the industry figure; the question is whether it is right for your process. A contaminated batch costs the medium, the utilities, the vessel time, the clean-down, and the downstream capacity that was reserved for it — for the 50 m³ aerobic process costed in week 52 that is of the order of tens of thousands of dollars. Moving from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶ risk costs 6.9 more del, which at 121 °C is 2.3 more minutes of holding, which costs a little nutrient and nothing else. Moving the other way, to 10⁻², saves 2.3 minutes and buys a ten-fold higher failure rate. The asymmetry is the point: sterility is cheap at the margin and failure is expensive, so the usual error is not over-sterilising the broth but under-specifying everything else — the seals, the valves, the sample ports and the exhaust filter, which Stanbury Ch 7 covers at length and which is where contamination actually comes from. A del factor is a statement about the medium, not about the plant.

Worked anchor

A batch sterilisation cycle for 50 m³, with the ramps integrated, and what ignoring them would have cost.

Step 1 — the del factor required. Doran's Example 13.8 basis: spores at 5 × 10¹² m⁻³. In 50 m³, N₀ = 2.5 × 10¹⁴. Accepting one contaminated batch in a thousand, N_t = 10⁻³. So ∇ = ln(2.5 × 10¹⁴ / 10⁻³) = ln(2.5 × 10¹⁷) = 40.1.

Sanity check against Stanbury: their 1,000 dm³ figure of 34.5 corresponds to ln(10¹²/10⁻³) = 34.54, i.e. 10⁹ organisms per dm³. Doran's 5 × 10⁹ per dm³ is the same order, and the two sources agree to within one del factor on the same basis. Good.

Step 2 — the rate constant. k_d = A e^(−E_d/RT) with E_d = 283 kJ gmol⁻¹, A = 5.7 × 10³⁹ h⁻¹, E_d/R = 34,036 K. At 121 °C (394.15 K): E_d/RT = 86.35, k_d = 179 h⁻¹ = 2.98 min⁻¹, D = 0.77 min.

Step 3 — the naive answer. Ignoring the ramps, t_hd = 40.1 / 2.98 = 13.5 min at 121 °C.

Step 4 — the ramps, for a fast cycle. Direct steam injection to 121 °C in 20 min from 100 °C, flash cooling back to 100 °C in 15 min, both taken as linear in temperature, death below 100 °C neglected per Doran. Numerical integration of ∫k_d dt over each ramp gives ∇_heat = 12.6 and ∇_cool = 9.4. Together 22.0, which is 55% of the requirement.

The holding period therefore needs only 40.1 − 22.0 = 18.1 del, i.e. t_hd = 18.1 / 2.98 = 6.1 min. Accounting for the ramps more than halves the holding time: 6.1 min against the naive 13.5.

Step 5 — the ramps, for a slow cycle, which is the usual case at this scale. A 50 m³ vessel heated through a jacket does not reach 121 °C in 20 minutes. Take 60 min up and 45 min down: ∇_heat = 37.7, ∇_cool = 28.3, total 66.0 against a requirement of 40.1. The holding time required is negative. The cycle is already sterile before the hold begins, by a margin of e^(66.0−40.1) = e^25.9, which is a factor of 1.8 × 10¹¹.

Step 6 — what that over-delivery costs, in nutrient. Compare two regimes delivering the same del of 40.1: a 121 °C hold for 13.5 min, and a 140 °C continuous steriliser for 40.1/157.9 = 0.254 min = 15.2 s. Take the nutrient's activation energy as 100 kJ mol⁻¹ — this figure is not from the assigned reading and is the one assumption carrying the result. The ratio of nutrient destruction is Q₁₄₀/Q₁₂₁ = [k_n(140)/k_n(121)] × (t₁₄₀/t₁₂₁) = 4.07 × 0.0189 = 0.077. The hotter, shorter regime destroys about one-thirteenth as much nutrient for the same sterility.

Step 7 — what the arithmetic chooses. Three options, costed:

(i) Batch, slow ramps, no holding time. Free. Delivers del 66 against 40 needed, so the nutrient damage is whatever a 105-minute excursion above 100 °C does. This is what most plants actually run, and the reason it survives is that the damage is absorbed into the medium recipe during development rather than measured.

(ii) Batch, fast ramps by direct steam injection and flash cooling, 6.1 min hold. Costs clean steam and 10-20% condensate dilution, and Stanbury warns of foaming on heating and the need for steam free of anticorrosion additives. Cuts the above-100 °C excursion from 105 min to 41 min.

(iii) Continuous, 140 °C, 15 s hold. Costs a spiral or plate exchanger and its capital, and Stanbury notes plate exchangers cannot handle suspended solids while spirals can and are self-cleaning. Buys the factor of thirteen on nutrient destruction, and buys back the vessel time — 105 minutes of a 50 m³ vessel per batch, which at a 48-hour cycle is 3.6% of capacity.

The order of magnitude of the decision is set by option (iii)'s two credits, and the larger of them is not the nutrient. It is the vessel time, because vessel time is what week 52 will show carries the capital charge. The nutrient argument is the one in the textbooks; the capacity argument is the one that gets funded.

One caveat the arithmetic cannot carry: Stanbury, following Armenante and Li (1993), records that particles in the medium heat far more slowly than the bulk, that their cores may not reach temperature before leaving a continuous steriliser, and that the conclusion is to overdesign the steriliser and the first cooling exchanger rather than lengthen the holding coil — and that direct steam injection is unwise for a particulate medium for the same reason. A hydrolysate or a starch slurry is a particulate medium. The 15-second answer above is for a soluble one.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • spore load 5 × 10¹² m⁻³ of raw medium, from Doran's Example 13.8 rather than from a measurement on this medium
  • E_d = 283 kJ gmol⁻¹ and A = 5.7 × 10³⁹ h⁻¹ for the contaminating spores, also from Doran's Example 13.8 and treated as applying to the whole population
  • one contaminated batch in a thousand accepted, which is the figure Stanbury attributes to the fermentation industry and not a derivation
  • cell death below 100 °C neglected, so the ramp integrals start and finish there, per Doran §13.6.1
  • heat-up and cool-down taken as linear in temperature, which is Deindoerfer and Humphrey's electrical-heating profile rather than the jacket profile this vessel would actually have
  • nutrient destruction activation energy of 100 kJ mol⁻¹ — this figure is not from any assigned reading and is the single assumption carrying the hot-and-short conclusion
  • medium taken as fully soluble, so Armenante and Li's particle-core lag does not apply; a hydrolysate or starch slurry would invalidate the 15-second continuous answer
  • the seed train's generation count taken as the volume ratio alone, with no allowance for growth spent re-entering exponential phase after each transfer
  • segregational loss of 1% per generation assumed when converting the generation count into a plasmid-free fraction

Common misconceptions

  • That the holding time is the sterilisation cycle. For a vessel of this size it is usually the smallest of the three contributions: a 60-minute heat-up to 121 °C delivers del 37.7 on its own against a requirement of 40.1. Compute the ramps first and the hold last, which is the order Stanbury's own subsection headings use.
  • That higher temperature is harder on the medium. It is harder per minute and much easier per unit of sterility, because spore destruction has roughly three times the activation energy of nutrient destruction. At fixed del, 140 °C for 15 seconds destroys about a thirteenth of the nutrient that 121 °C for 13.5 minutes does. The whole of high-temperature short-time processing is that inequality.
  • That separate sterilisation is a precaution. It is a reaction-avoidance measure with named chemistry: Maillard between reducing sugars and amino groups, and phosphate precipitating with calcium and magnesium. No time-temperature regime prevents either, because neither is a thermal-death problem. Conversely, every extra stream is an extra aseptic connection, so the list of what to separate should be short and justified rather than long and cautious.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Why must heat-up and cool-down be integrated into the del calculation, and what happens if you ignore them?

A. Because cell death is first order with an Arrhenius rate constant and therefore proceeds at every temperature, not only at the holding temperature, and because at production scale the time spent above 100 °C during the ramps is in hours while the hold is in minutes. Ignoring them means solving ∇ = k_d·t_hd for the whole requirement and getting a holding time that is too long — in the worked case 13.5 min instead of 6.1 min with fast ramps, and with slow ramps a positive holding time where the correct answer is zero, because the ramps alone deliver del 66 against a requirement of 40. The cost is not safety, it is the medium: every surplus minute at 121 °C destroys nutrient, browns the broth and consumes vessel time, and none of it buys sterility that was not already there. The ramps have to be integrated numerically because k_d varies by two orders of magnitude over the ramp, so an average temperature does not work.

Q. Your seed train takes a 1 mL vial to a 50 m³ vessel. How many generations is that, and what do you do with the answer?

A. A volume ratio of 5 × 10⁷, so log₂(5 × 10⁷) = 25.6 generations of net growth as a floor, plus the growth spent re-entering exponential phase after each of the seven or eight transfers. Feed 26 generations into the week 33 segregational-loss model: at 1% loss per generation the production vessel is inoculated with 23% plasmid-free cells before the fermentation starts, and the loss continues through it. Three things follow. Use a chromosomally integrated construct if the product allows, because the arithmetic does not improve with vessel design. If a plasmid is unavoidable, keep selection on through the train and know what the selection costs in the production vessel, where antibiotic is usually not permitted. And transfer on Stanbury's criterion — a defined physiological state in late exponential phase — rather than on a clock, because that minimises the lag and therefore the generations spent outside net growth, which is the only part of the count that vessel design can touch.

Q. A reviewer says your decision to sterilise glucose separately is over-engineering. Answer them quantitatively.

A. The mechanism is not thermal death, so no cycle avoids it: reducing sugars condense with free amino groups in a Maillard reaction whose products brown the medium, consume lysine and arginine preferentially, and inhibit growth. The quantitative part is the excursion — with jacket heating, this medium spends about 105 minutes above 100 °C, and Maillard rate rises steeply through that range, so the exposure is not a few minutes at peak but most of two hours. The cost of separating is one extra vessel, one extra transfer and one extra aseptic connection, which raises contamination risk against the 10⁻³ target; the cost of not separating is a yield loss that has to be measured rather than argued, and a broth that is harder to clarify in week 51. The right answer is to run both and compare titres, and to say which way the comparison came out — and if it came out neutral, combine them, because the connection is a real risk and the chemistry is only a real cost when it is large.
