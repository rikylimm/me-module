Product classes II: amino acids, natural products and proteins

Week content

Where the economics inverts. For the products of week 45 the sugar bill decided everything; here the sugar is almost free and purification decides everything, and the strain parameter that matters is the one that makes the broth easier to work up. Shuler §11.1 states the relationship in a single comparison that is worth memorising: citric acid enters recovery at around 100 g L⁻¹ and sells for order 1 to 2 dollars a kilogram, while a therapeutic protein can enter at 0.00001 g L⁻¹ and sell for order 100 million dollars a kilogram — seven orders of magnitude in price against seven in dilution. Everything in this week follows from that line, including the phase synthesis at the end of it, because the host you defend in Artifact 26 is the one whose failure modes your product's price can absorb.

Core ideas

1. The economics of a high-value product, stated as two ratios

Shuler §11.1 gives the first: recovery and purification often exceed 50% of total manufacturing cost, especially for an intracellular product, and the difficulty of recovery is tightly coupled to how dilute the product is when it leaves the fermenter. Stanbury's recovery costs section gives the second, and it is the sharper one: cost analyses of older fermentation processes split roughly 1:1 between fermentation and isolation, but the shift to recombinant organisms making exotic compounds at extremely low titres has moved that split to 1:8 or 1:10 — the fermentation becoming 10% of the cost and the recovery 90%. Read those two together and the design consequence is immediate. A yield point is worth almost nothing here. A titre point is worth a great deal, because it shortens the concentration train. A change that moves the product from inside the cell to outside it is worth more than either, because it deletes unit operations rather than shrinking them.

2. Amino acids are the bridge case, and they behave like bulk

Keep them in the matrix as the calibration point. Lysine is a 2.5 million tonne a year market at commodity price, made by C. glutamicum LYS-12 at 120 g L⁻¹ in 30 hours and 55% yield — 4.0 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹ and 79% of the electron ceiling derived in week 42 — with glutamate in §12.4.1.1 at a comparable industrial scale. At that price and that titre every term in the week 45 skeleton still applies: the sugar bill is the largest line, the recovery allowance is generous because the broth is concentrated, and yield earns research. Lee §12.4.1 also lists the specialty amino acids where the economics has already inverted — pipecolic acid, trans-4-hydroxyproline, shinorine, ectoine, theanine, 4-hydroxyisoleucine — at titres orders below lysine and prices orders above it. The same organism, the same tools, and a completely different set of design priorities: that contrast is the cleanest teaching device in the week.

3. Terpenoids: the mevalonate route as Lee gives it

Lee §18.2.5.3 is precise and worth following step by step, because the anchor needs it. More than 68 000 terpenoids are known, more than the flavonoids, polyketides and alkaloids combined, and all of them come from the C5 units isopentenyl pyrophosphate and its isomer dimethylallyl pyrophosphate. In S. cerevisiae those come from the cytosolic mevalonate pathway: Erg10 and Erg13 condense three acetyl-CoA, Hmg1 or Hmg2 reduces, Erg12 and Erg8 add two phosphates, Erg19 decarboxylates to give IPP, and Idi1 isomerises. Erg20 then condenses IPP with DMAPP to geranyl pyrophosphate and adds a second IPP to give the C15 farnesyl pyrophosphate, with Bts1 extending to C20 and Erg9 drawing FPP off to squalene and ergosterol. The chapter's five engineering targets for sesquiterpenoids follow from that map: cytosolic acetyl-CoA supply, flux to FPP, preventing FPP drainage to ergosterol, efficient turnover by the heterologous enzyme, and sufficient NADPH. And it states the host argument in one sentence — yeast harbours native cytochrome P450s and their redox partners, which is why artemisinic acid, originally planned for E. coli, moved to yeast when a P450 oxidation proved difficult in the bacterium.

4. The MEP route is not in the chapter, and the difference is a yield

Lee Ch 18 is a yeast chapter and describes only the mevalonate route; the methylerythritol phosphate route of bacteria and plastids appears nowhere in it, and the session asking for both routes with their cofactor demands cannot be answered from the assigned reading alone. What can be done exactly, and is done in the anchor, is the carbon bookkeeping, which is fixed by the precursors each route takes. Mevalonate starts from three acetyl-CoA, and every acetyl-CoA costs a pyruvate decarboxylation before the pathway begins, so four CO₂ are lost per C5 unit. MEP starts from one pyruvate and one glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, exactly what one glucose yields, and loses one CO₂ per C5. That single difference is worth about 29% in mass yield. The cofactor demands differ too — the mevalonate route needs two NADPH and three ATP per C5, while MEP needs of order three reducing equivalents and two nucleotide triphosphates, with its last two steps requiring reduced ferredoxin in bacteria — but take those from a pathway database rather than from these notes before they enter a proposal, and note that the ferredoxin requirement is itself a reason the MEP route is hard to move into yeast.

5. Polyketides and nonribosomal peptides: the four obstacles, now as a product problem

Week 44 read Lee Ch 17 as a host question; read it here as a product question and the four obstacles §17.5.1 names become a design checklist. Regulatory incompatibility: the cluster's own promoters and regulators may not function in the new host, which is what full pathway refactoring in §17.5.4.4 exists to solve and what the bottromycin work did by replacing every native promoter with synthetic constitutive ones, for a 5- to 50-fold titre increase. Missing triggering signals or receptors: a silent cluster in its native host often stays silent elsewhere. Precursor supply: the extender units are malonyl-CoA and methylmalonyl-CoA and the carrier proteins need phosphopantetheinylation, so a host without the full machinery has to be given it. Toxicity. Over those sits the base rate — about 10% success for non-Streptomyces clusters and 30% for Streptomyces ones in a Streptomyces host — and the mechanics of 20 to 210 kb of 65–75% GC DNA. The titres that result are modest in absolute terms: pristinamycin II at 2.2 g L⁻¹ in flask and 2 g L⁻¹ in batch is reported as the highest for that compound. A product class where a gram per litre is a result is a product class whose price must be high.

6. Malonyl-CoA is the recurring precursor limit, and the numbers show it

Lee §18.2.5.1 gives the flavonoid case with enough detail to generalise. Chalcone synthase is a type III polyketide synthase that takes p-coumaroyl-CoA as the starter and runs three malonyl-CoA-dependent elongations to a tetraketide, which becomes a chalcone and then, through chalcone isomerase, the flavanone naringenin — the core from which almost all flavonoids derive. So three malonyl-CoA per molecule, before any decoration. The titres: naringenin at about 55 mg L⁻¹ from an Arabidopsis pathway in an optimised chassis in shake flasks, raised to 90 mg L⁻¹ by dividing the pathway into three modules — tyrosine supply, malonyl-CoA supply, and naringenin formation — and balancing them stepwise; then kaempferol at 26.6 mg L⁻¹ and quercetin at 20.4 mg L⁻¹ downstream. Set those milligrams beside the 140 g L⁻¹ itaconate and 100 g L⁻¹ cellulase of week 44 and the gap is four orders of magnitude, with the same organism types and better tools. The cause is that malonyl-CoA is a tightly regulated node feeding fatty acid synthesis, so pulling flux from it fights the cell's own control; Lee §18.2.4.1 reports the same problem for 3-HP made from malonyl-CoA, solved by upregulating pyruvate to malonyl-CoA flux and by swapping in enzymes with better kinetics. The chapter gives no intracellular malonyl-CoA concentration, so quantify the pool from a measurement database rather than from the chapter if the dossier needs it.

7. Modular balance, in week 31's language

Ajikumar et al. 2010 and the naringenin result above are the same experiment done twice in different organisms, and week 31 gives the vocabulary for why the approach works. Partition a long pathway into an upstream module supplying precursor and a downstream module consuming it, vary the expression of each over a grid, and measure product. In control-analysis terms the grid is a direct search for where the flux control coefficient sits, without having to measure elasticities: if raising upstream expression alone accumulates intermediate and does not raise product, control is downstream; if it raises product proportionally, control is upstream; and the characteristic result in these papers — that the optimum is an interior point and that overexpressing both modules is worse than balancing them — is the summation theorem made visible, control being shared and the sum fixed. The practical rule to carry into the dossier is that the intermediate pool is the readout, and a pathway that accumulates its intermediate is telling you which module to tune.

8. When the product is a protein, the design question moves downstream

Shuler §11.1 reduces recovery to four functions — separate insoluble solids, concentrate and remove most of the water, remove contaminating chemicals, and prepare the product — and insists the water comes out early so that everything after it is smaller equipment. Apply that to the three locations a protein can be made in. Intracellular: lysis, debris removal, and for E. coli the inclusion-body and endotoxin problems of week 40, with 25–50% of total protein as product but every gram of it behind a cell wall. Periplasmic: an osmotic-shock step releases a fraction of the proteome rather than all of it, so the first purification begins far cleaner, at the cost of the translocation capacity — about 20% of E. coli protein crosses the inner membrane. Secreted: no lysis, no debris, no endotoxin from the cytoplasm, and the fungal numbers of week 44 show how far that can go at 100 g L⁻¹ cellulase against the milligrams-to-grams per litre of bacterial, yeast and mammalian hosts. The downstream consequence is not marginal: it is the difference between Stanbury's 1:1 split and its 1:8. Then the branch Shuler §14.3.1 puts first still binds — if the product needs post-translational modification, none of this is available in a bacterium at all.

9. Defending a choice, which is what Artifact 26 is

The defence has a shape, and it is the negative rule of week 42 followed by the arithmetic of weeks 45 and 46. Name the one property the product cannot do without and eliminate every host lacking it — modification, secretion, tolerance, anaerobiosis, food-grade status, a P450 redox partner. Among the survivors, compute the ceiling, place the best published strain against it as a fraction, and state the basis of that number. Then cost the skeleton: substrate at the achievable yield, utilities at 8.2 kW m⁻³ if the route is aerobic, and the recovery allowance implied by the titre and the price. Then state the failure mode you are accepting and what it costs. Finally, and this is the part that makes it a defence rather than a preference, state the result that would reverse it: a measured secretion titre, a solvent tolerance figure, a sugar price, a modification requirement discovered in assay. A choice with no such condition attached has not been defended, it has been asserted — and the three places in this phase where the assigned reading could not supply a number (Bacillus secretion capacity, Pseudomonas solvent-tolerance mechanisms, the regulatory path for an engineered food-grade strain) are exactly the conditions most likely to decide a real project.

Worked anchor

The theoretical yield of a C15 terpenoid by each route, computed, and the gap between them explained.

Take β-farnesene, C₁₅H₂₄, 204.36 g mol⁻¹, degree of reduction 15(4) + 24(1) = 84. Glucose is 180.16 g mol⁻¹, six carbons, degree of reduction 24.

Step 1, the two absolute ceilings, before any pathway is named. Carbon permits 15/6 = 2.5 glucose per farnesene, that is 204.36/(2.5 × 180.16) = 0.454 g g⁻¹. Electrons permit 84/24 = 3.5 glucose per farnesene, that is 204.36/(3.5 × 180.16) = 0.324 g g⁻¹. Electrons bind, as they do for every terpene, because a terpene is a hydrocarbon and carries 5.6 electrons per carbon against glucose's 4.

Step 2, the mevalonate route's own carbon bookkeeping. Per C5 unit the route condenses three acetyl-CoA. Each acetyl-CoA comes from a pyruvate, losing one CO₂, so three acetyl-CoA consume 1.5 glucose and release 3 CO₂. The pathway then decarboxylates once more at Erg19, C6 mevalonate pyrophosphate to C5 IPP, for a fourth CO₂. So 9 carbons in, 5 carbons out, 4 lost. Farnesene is three C5 units: 4.5 glucose, 27 carbons in, 15 out, 12 CO₂ released. Yield = 204.36/(4.5 × 180.16) = 204.36/810.7 = 0.252 g g⁻¹.

Step 3, the MEP route's bookkeeping. Per C5 unit the route takes one pyruvate and one glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and loses one CO₂ in forming 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate. One glucose yields exactly two trioses, one of which becomes the pyruvate and one of which is the GAP, so one glucose supplies one C5 unit: 6 carbons in, 5 out, 1 lost. Farnesene: 3 glucose, 18 carbons in, 15 out, 3 CO₂. Carbon-wise that is 204.36/(3 × 180.16) = 0.378 g g⁻¹.

Step 4, the comparison, with the constraint check that matters. MEP's carbon figure of 0.378 g g⁻¹ exceeds the electron ceiling of 0.324, which means carbon is no longer what binds the MEP route — electrons are, and the route's real ceiling is 0.324 g g⁻¹, reached by spending some of the surplus carbon on reducing power. The mevalonate route's 0.252 g g⁻¹ is below the electron ceiling, so carbon still binds there, and the strain can never reach 0.324 no matter how much NADPH it is given. In fractions of the absolute limit: MEP 100%, mevalonate 78%. In mass terms MEP is worth 0.324/0.252 = 1.29, about 29% more product per tonne of sugar.

Step 5, where the difference comes from, in one sentence. Three pyruvate decarboxylations paid before the mevalonate pathway even starts — 3 of the 12 CO₂ per farnesene, with a fourth from Erg19 — against one decarboxylation in MEP, because MEP builds its C5 from a C3 and a C3 rather than from three C2 units that each cost a C1.

Step 6, why yeast is nonetheless the host that made the product. Lee §18.2.5.3 is explicit: S. cerevisiae carries native cytochrome P450s and their redox partners, and artemisinic acid moved to yeast from E. coli precisely because a P450 oxidation was difficult in the bacterium. A 29% yield advantage is decisive for a bulk chemical and secondary for a product whose cost is dominated by purification — and the reported performance confirms it, with amorphadiene above 40 g L⁻¹ and β-farnesene at 130 g L⁻¹ in yeast, the latter also reported as needing 75% less oxygen than the alternative. Against the 0.324 g g⁻¹ ceiling, 130 g L⁻¹ is a titre and not a yield, and the chapter does not give the sugar consumed, so the matrix entry carries the titre and marks the yield as unstated.

So the answer to the session is not that one route is better. It is that MEP has the higher ceiling by a calculable 29%, that the ceiling is irrelevant to whether a terpenoid programme succeeds, and that the host was chosen for a cofactor partner rather than for carbon efficiency — which is the whole week in one example.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • β-farnesene as the C15 terpenoid, C₁₅H₂₄ at 204.36 g mol⁻¹ and degree of reduction 84
  • all acetyl-CoA for the mevalonate route derived from pyruvate, so one CO₂ per acetyl-CoA before the pathway starts
  • one glucose supplying exactly one pyruvate and one glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate for the MEP route
  • no carbon to biomass, maintenance or by-product in either route's bookkeeping
  • no ATP or cofactor limitation imposed; both ceilings are carbon and electron balances only
  • the MEP cofactor demand quoted as approximate and not taken from any assigned chapter
  • reported amorphadiene and β-farnesene figures are titres; the sugar consumed is not stated in the chapter, so no yield is inferred from them

Common misconceptions

  • That a high-value product forgives a bad strain. It forgives a bad yield, which is a different thing. Titre it does not forgive, because the recovery allowance scales with broth concentration — Stanbury's limits are quoted per tonne at a stated w/v — and a dilute broth multiplies the size of every unit operation downstream of it. Nor does it forgive a product locked inside the cell, since that adds lysis, debris removal and, in a Gram-negative, endotoxin clearance to a train that may already be 90% of the cost.
  • That the mevalonate and MEP routes differ mainly in cofactor demand. They differ first in carbon: mevalonate pays three pyruvate decarboxylations to make its three acetyl-CoA and a fourth at Erg19, losing four CO₂ per C5 unit, where MEP builds a C5 from a C3 and a C3 and loses one. That is 0.252 against 0.324 g g⁻¹ for a C15 terpenoid, a 29% difference, and it is fixed by stoichiometry rather than by expression.
  • That a flavonoid or polyketide titre in the milligram range means the pathway is badly expressed. More often the precursor node is the constraint: three malonyl-CoA per naringenin, drawn from a pool the cell regulates tightly for fatty acid synthesis. The naringenin results make the point — 55 mg L⁻¹ from an optimised chassis, 90 mg L⁻¹ once the pathway was split into tyrosine, malonyl-CoA and naringenin modules and balanced. The intermediate pool, not the enzyme level, is the diagnostic.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. For a high-value product, which strain parameter does the economics punish most, and why not yield?

A. Titre, because the recovery allowance scales with how concentrated the product is when it leaves the fermenter. Shuler §11.1 states the correlation directly — citric acid enters recovery near 100 g L⁻¹ and sells for 1 to 2 dollars a kilogram, a therapeutic protein can enter at 0.00001 g L⁻¹ and sell for order 100 million — and puts recovery above 50% of manufacturing cost, especially for intracellular products, while Stanbury reports the fermentation-to-isolation split moving from about 1:1 in older processes to 1:8 or 1:10 for recombinant products made at very low titre, which is to say the fermentation becomes 10% of the cost. Yield is punished least because the substrate is a negligible line: at 0.40 dollars a kilogram of glucose, even a tenfold worse yield is invisible against a product selling in the thousands. The ranking that follows is titre first, then product location — secreted beats periplasmic beats intracellular, because each step deletes unit operations rather than shrinking them — and then yield.

Q. Your flavonoid strain makes 90 mg L⁻¹ and accumulates p-coumaric acid. What does that tell you, and what do you do?

A. That control sits downstream of the starter unit, not upstream: the cell is making p-coumaroyl-CoA's precursor faster than chalcone synthase can consume it, and since CHS runs three malonyl-CoA-dependent elongations per naringenin, the usual cause is malonyl-CoA supply rather than CHS expression. In week 31's terms the accumulating intermediate is the readout that the flux control coefficient has moved, and the summation theorem says raising upstream expression further can only make it worse. So do what the modular work did: treat tyrosine supply, malonyl-CoA supply and naringenin formation as three modules and vary them against each other rather than in series — that is the step that took the reported titre from 55 to 90 mg L⁻¹. Then, before proposing a malonyl-CoA intervention, get an actual pool measurement from a metabolomics database, because Lee Ch 18 gives no concentration for it.

Q. Defend a product and host choice for the capstone in the form the artifact requires. What must be in it, and what makes it a defence rather than a preference?

A. Five things. The eliminating property first — the one requirement the product cannot do without, used to remove hosts rather than to rank them, which is week 42's negative rule. Then the ceiling, derived from degrees of reduction, with the best published strain placed against it as a fraction and the basis of that published number stated, since weeks 40 and 43 both found yields reported above their own ceilings on undeclared bases. Then the cost skeleton: substrate at the achievable yield, utilities at about 8.2 kW m⁻³ if the route is aerobic, and the recovery allowance the titre and the price imply. Then the failure mode being accepted — proteases and sporulation for Bacillus, the Crabtree effect and compartment targeting for yeast, medium cost for a lactobacillus, transfer limitation for a gas or photosynthetic process, morphology for a fungus. What makes it a defence is the fifth: a stated result that would reverse it. For most of the hosts in this matrix that result is one of the three numbers this phase could not find in the reading — a measured secretion titre, a solvent tolerance mechanism, or the regulatory path for an engineered food-grade strain — and saying so is stronger than filling the gap from memory.
