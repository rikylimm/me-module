Growth kinetics and physiological states

Week content

Case thread: Separate counts, growth rate and metabolic flux. Which did the thesis actually measure? Open the case thread.

Growth is autocatalysis with a catalyst whose composition changes. Everything in phase 4 is parameterised by what you learn to measure this week, and the week's real lesson is about identifiability: which parameters a given experiment can determine, and which it cannot however good the fit looks. Paper 2 is sat at the end.

Core ideas

1. The balances, and why they are the ones you already write

Batch: dX/dt = µX and dS/dt = −µX/Y − mX, with µ a function of S. Chemostat with a sterile feed: dX/dt = (µ − D)X and dS/dt = D(S₀ − S) − µX/Y − mX. Setting the derivatives to zero gives the steady state, and the first consequence is the one that makes the chemostat valuable: µ = D. You set the growth rate by turning a pump. Washout follows when D exceeds the maximum µ attainable at S₀. All of this is a CSTR with a self-reproducing catalyst, and nothing in the mathematics is unfamiliar — the biology is in the form of µ(S) and in the maintenance term.

2. Monod, and its honest status

µ = µmax S/(Ks + S) is empirical, not mechanistic: it has the Michaelis-Menten form because saturation is a general property of carrier-mediated uptake, but it describes whole-cell growth rather than one enzyme, and Ks is not a binding constant for anything. It fails in predictable ways — at very low substrate where maintenance dominates, under multiple limitation, during transients where the cell's composition lags, and for inhibitory substrates, where Haldane-type or other forms are used instead. Use it as a convenient interpolation and say so; treating Ks as a physical constant of the organism is a common and visible error.

3. Identifiability, which is the week's real content

Here is the thing worth internalising. In batch culture the substrate is far above Ks for nearly the whole run, so µ sits at µmax and carries essentially no information about Ks. The substrate passes through S ≈ Ks only briefly at the end, when biomass is high, sampling is sparse, and measuring a low residual concentration is hardest. Fitted µmax and Ks therefore come out strongly correlated with a wide, often meaningless, interval on Ks — and a fit that looks beautiful can still be telling you nothing about one of its parameters. Demonstrate this to yourself with a joint confidence region rather than two standard errors; the ellipse's tilt is the correlation, and seeing it once changes how you read every published parameter pair.

4. Designing the experiment that does resolve the parameters

A chemostat series at four to six dilution rates spanning roughly 0.1 to 0.8 of µmax resolves what batch cannot. Low dilution rates carry the maintenance information, because the Pirt term m/µ dominates there. Mid-range rates carry Ks, because that is where S is comparable to it. High rates near washout bound µmax. The substrate assay must be validated near its detection limit, since residual concentrations at low D are small and the whole maintenance estimate rests on them. State the replicate structure and the number of volume changes before each sample — five is a common minimum — because a chemostat result without those numbers is not a steady-state result.

5. Product kinetics: Luedeking-Piret and what it classifies

qP = αµ + β separates product formation into a growth-associated term and a non-growth-associated one. Three classes follow: growth-associated, where β ≈ 0 and product tracks growth, as for ethanol; non-growth-associated, where α ≈ 0 and product forms in stationary phase, as for many secondary metabolites; and mixed. The crossover point µ = β/α tells you where half the product is non-growth-associated, and that number decides the process: below it, a two-stage process with a growth phase then a production phase makes sense; above it, growth-coupled operation gives more product per unit time at the cost of carbon spent on biomass. The decision is settled by which of yield and volumetric productivity the economics punishes, which is week 52.

6. Physiological states you must be able to recognise

Overflow metabolism above a critical growth rate, which week 13 explained as proteome allocation and which fed-batch feeding is designed to avoid. Diauxie on mixed substrates, from the catabolite repression of week 15. The Crabtree effect in yeast, the same proteome logic with ethanol as the product. The stringent response under amino acid starvation, which shuts down ribosome synthesis and changes the biomass composition substantially. And death kinetics, which Doran treats explicitly and which matters for sterilisation design in week 48 and for long fermentations where viability falls. Recognising which state a culture is in is the diagnostic skill; computing rates within a state is the easy part.

Worked anchor

Estimate parameters from two chemostat points, and then see why two is not enough.

Feed 25 g/L glucose. At D = 0.20 h⁻¹: X = 9.6 g/L, S = 0.30 g/L. At D = 0.35 h⁻¹: X = 9.1 g/L, S = 1.40 g/L.

Apparent yields: Y₁ = 9.6/(25 − 0.3) = 0.389 g/g; Y₂ = 9.1/(25 − 1.4) = 0.386 g/g.

Monod parameters from µ = D: 0.20(Ks + 0.30) = µmax(0.30) and 0.35(Ks + 1.40) = µmax(1.40). Divide one by the other to eliminate µmax: (0.20/0.35) × (Ks + 0.30)/(Ks + 1.40) = 0.30/1.40. Solving gives Ks ≈ 0.52 g/L and µmax ≈ 0.55 h⁻¹.

Now maintenance, via Pirt. 1/Y₁ = 2.571 at 1/µ = 5.00; 1/Y₂ = 2.591 at 1/µ = 2.857. Slope = (2.571 − 2.591)/(5.00 − 2.857) = −0.0093 — negative, and therefore unphysical.

That negative slope is the whole point of the exercise. The two reciprocal yields differ by less than their measurement error, so the data cannot resolve maintenance at all, and a regression through two points will always return something. Reporting a maintenance coefficient from two chemostat points is a real and common error. The correct conclusion is to state that m is not determined by this data and to specify the experiment that would determine it — four to six dilution rates, with the lowest well below 0.2 µmax where the maintenance term actually dominates.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • steady state with no wall growth and no substrate in the feed vessel
  • Monod kinetics on a single limiting substrate
  • constant yield apart from the maintenance term
  • both measured points lie exactly on the curve

Common misconceptions

  • That Ks is a physical constant of the organism. It is an empirical parameter of a whole-cell correlation, and reported values for the same organism span orders of magnitude depending on how they were obtained.
  • That a good fit means well-determined parameters. Batch data fits Monod beautifully while leaving Ks essentially unconstrained, which is why the joint confidence region matters more than the curve.
  • That the productivity-optimal dilution rate is where you should operate. It sits close to washout, so a small disturbance loses the culture — which is why industry operates well below it.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Derive the chemostat steady state and the washout condition from the balances.

A. From dX/dt = (µ − D)X = 0 with X ≠ 0, µ = D. Substituting Monod, D = µmax S/(Ks + S), so S = Ks D/(µmax − D). From dS/dt = D(S₀ − S) − µX/Y = 0 at steady state, X = Y(S₀ − S) neglecting maintenance. Washout occurs when the required S exceeds S₀, that is when D ≥ µmax S₀/(Ks + S₀), which for S₀ ≫ Ks is approximately µmax. Note the practical consequence: measuring the washout dilution rate gives you µmax directly, and is often the most reliable way to obtain it.

Q. Why does batch data poorly constrain Ks, and what experiment fixes it?

A. Because substrate sits far above Ks for nearly the whole run, holding µ at µmax and carrying no information about Ks; the region S ≈ Ks is traversed only briefly at the end, when biomass is high, sampling is sparse and low residual substrate is hardest to measure. Fitted µmax and Ks are therefore strongly correlated with a wide interval on Ks. The fix is a chemostat series at four to six dilution rates spanning roughly 0.1 to 0.8 of µmax, with a substrate assay validated near its detection limit and the number of volume changes before sampling recorded.

Q. A product follows Luedeking-Piret with α = 0.15 g/g and β = 0.02 g gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹. At what growth rate is half the product non-growth-associated, and what does that imply for the process?

A. Half non-growth-associated means αµ = β, so µ = 0.02/0.15 = 0.133 h⁻¹. Below roughly 0.13 h⁻¹ the non-growth term dominates, so a two-stage process — grow fast, then hold at low growth rate for production — captures most of the product while spending less carbon on biomass. Above it, growth-coupled operation gives higher volumetric productivity at the cost of carbon diverted to biomass. Which to choose depends on whether the economics punishes yield or productivity more, which is settled in week 52 rather than here.

Appendix — Fermi drills

Systems-biology interviews open with estimation questions. Each is answerable from numbers in these modules.

Q. Roughly how many ATP molecules does an E. coli cell consume per second at maximal growth?

A. Take maintenance at about 20 mmol ATP gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ and growth-associated demand at roughly 55 mmol ATP per gDW of biomass made. At µ = 1.0 h⁻¹ the growth term contributes about 55 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹, so total ≈ 75 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹. Per cell: 75 × 3×10⁻¹³ = 2.3×10⁻¹¹ mmol/h = 6.3×10⁻¹⁸ mol/s × 6.02×10²³ ≈ 4×10⁶ molecules/s, so of order 10⁶–10⁷ ATP per cell per second. Sanity check against the pool: ATP sits near 1–3 mM, which in 1 fL is about 10⁶ molecules, so the entire pool turns over roughly once a second — exactly the figure quoted for ATP turnover, which is the check that the estimate is self-consistent.

Q. How many ribosomes does a cell need to double in 30 minutes?

A. Protein is about 55% of 3×10⁻¹³ g, so 1.65×10⁻¹³ g, equal to 1.65×10⁻¹³/110 ≈ 1.5×10⁻¹⁵ mol of residues = 9×10⁸ residues. These must be made in 1,800 s, so 5×10⁵ residues/s. A ribosome elongates at roughly 15–20 residues/s, giving about 3×10⁴ ribosomes. The measured figure at fast growth is 5–7×10⁴, so the estimate is correct to a factor of two — the gap being ribosomes that are idle or in assembly, which is itself the content of the growth laws.

Q. What fraction of the proteome is a ten-enzyme heterologous pathway at 5 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹?

A. Per enzyme at 30 s⁻¹ and half saturation, effective rate 15 s⁻¹. Flux per cell = 5 × 3×10⁻¹³ mmol/h = 2.5×10⁵ molecules/s, so 1.7×10⁴ copies per enzyme, and 1.7×10⁵ copies across ten. At 45 kDa that is 1.3×10⁻¹⁴ g = 13 fg, against 180 fg of protein: about 7% of the proteome. Which predicts a growth penalty of roughly the same order, and explains why a ten-enzyme pathway at industrial flux is a burden problem before it is a kinetics problem.

Q. How much CO₂ does a 50 m³ fermenter vent per hour at 40 gDW/L?

A. Take a respiratory quotient near 1 and an oxygen uptake of 5 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹. Biomass = 50,000 L × 40 g/L = 2×10⁶ g. CER ≈ 5 × 2×10⁶ = 10⁷ mmol/h = 10⁴ mol/h, which is 440 kg CO₂ per hour, or about 250 m³ of gas per hour at STP. Useful for two reasons: it sizes the off-gas handling, and it tells you that dissolved CO₂ in a tall vessel is not a trace concern.

Q. How many generations separate a vial from a 50 m³ production vessel?

A. Final biomass about 2×10⁶ g from a starting inoculum of roughly 10⁻³ g, a 2×10⁹-fold increase, so log₂(2×10⁹) ≈ 31 doublings. This is the number that decides whether plasmid stability matters, whether an evolved escape mutant has time to sweep, and whether a growth-coupled design is necessary rather than merely elegant.

Q. What volume of glucose solution feeds a 50 m³ fed-batch for 24 hours?

A. At 0.3 g product per g glucose and a target of 50 g/L product in 50 m³, you need 2.5×10⁶ g of product and therefore 8.3×10⁶ g of glucose. At a 500 g/L feed that is 1.7×10⁴ L, or 17 m³ — a third of the working volume. Which is why fed-batch vessels start at half volume, and why feed concentration is itself a design variable rather than a detail.

Q. How long would it take a single cell to become one gram of biomass at µ = 0.6 h⁻¹?

A. One cell is 3×10⁻¹³ g, so ln(1/3×10⁻¹³)/0.6 = 28.8/0.6 ≈ 48 hours. Two days spans twelve orders of magnitude in mass, which is the arithmetic behind why selection screens 10⁹ variants overnight and why a plate-based screen handles 10³.

Q. What is the diffusion time of a metabolite across a cell, and why does it matter?

A. With D ≈ 5×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s for a small metabolite in cytoplasm and L = 10⁻⁶ m, t ≈ L²/2D = 10⁻¹²/10⁻⁹ = 10⁻³ s, a millisecond. Far faster than any enzymatic turnover, which is why a bacterial cell is treated as well-mixed and why intracellular gradients are ignored — and why the same calculation for a 1 mm fungal pellet, giving seconds to minutes, means the opposite conclusion there.

Q. How much heat does an aerobic fermentation generate per cubic metre?

A. Oxygen consumption releases roughly 460 kJ per mole of O₂ across most substrates. At OUR = 100 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ that is 46 kJ L⁻¹ h⁻¹ = 46 MJ m⁻³ h⁻¹, about 13 kW per cubic metre. For a 50 m³ vessel, 640 kW of cooling duty — which is why heat removal, not just oxygen supply, constrains large aerobic fermentations.

Q. What titre does a $400/kg product need before purification stops dominating cost?

A. Downstream cost scales with the water processed. At 5 g/L you handle 200 L of broth per kg of product; at 50 g/L, 20 L. If handling, concentrating and purifying costs of order $1–3 per litre of broth for a multi-step train, the first case is $200–600/kg and the second $20–60/kg. So the threshold sits near the point where downstream falls to perhaps 10–15% of price, which for most products is tens of grams per litre — the origin of the titre thresholds quoted in week 52.
