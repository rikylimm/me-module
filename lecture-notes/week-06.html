Enzyme kinetics II: allostery and regulation

Week content

The cell's own control system — the thing that will fight every pathway you build. Allostery converts a modest concentration change into a large activity change, which is why feedback inhibition is such an effective regulator and why removing it is usually the first intervention in a biosynthetic strain. The week also kills a myth you will meet in older literature.

Core ideas

1. Cooperativity, and what the Hill coefficient actually says

A cooperative enzyme's rate-versus-substrate curve is sigmoid rather than hyperbolic: binding at one site changes the affinity of the others. The Hill equation, v/Vmax = Sⁿ/(K + Sⁿ), fits this empirically, and n is bounded above by the number of sites. It is not a count of sites, nor of molecules bound simultaneously; only n equal to the site number would indicate fully concerted all-or-none binding. A tetramer with n = 2.8 is strongly but not infinitely cooperative. The physiological point: a four-fold change in ligand moves a hyperbolic enzyme from 20% to 50% saturation but a cooperative one from roughly 5% to 80%, so cooperativity turns a dial into a switch.

2. MWC and KNF: two models, one observable

The concerted (MWC) model posits that the whole oligomer flips between a tense T state and a relaxed R state, with all subunits switching together; ligand binds R preferentially and so pulls the equilibrium. The allosteric constant L = [T]/[R] in the absence of ligand sets the baseline, and effectors act by changing L. The sequential (KNF) model allows subunits to change individually, with ligand binding inducing a conformational change that alters neighbours' affinity. MWC explains positive cooperativity elegantly and cannot produce negative cooperativity; KNF can produce both. Distinguishing them requires structural and binding data, not kinetics alone — which is worth knowing because papers often assert one without the evidence.

3. The two canonical systems, and why each is taught

Haemoglobin is taught because it shows cooperativity's physiological purpose: the sigmoid curve means oxygen is loaded in the lung and unloaded in tissue over a narrow pressure range, which a hyperbolic binder could not do. Aspartate transcarbamoylase is taught because it shows feedback architecture: CTP, the pathway's end product, inhibits the first committed step, while ATP activates it, so the enzyme integrates two signals and balances purine against pyrimidine synthesis. Study ATCase as the template for every biosynthetic regulation you will meet in week 14.

4. Covalent modification and zero-order ultrasensitivity

Phosphorylation cascades give a second control layer with different properties: slower, amplifiable, and capable of extreme steepness. When the modifying and demodifying enzymes are both saturated with their substrate — the zero-order regime — the fraction modified becomes an extremely steep function of the kinase/phosphatase ratio, far steeper than any Hill coefficient from binding alone. This is a switch built from two ordinary enzymes, and it is worth understanding because dynamic pathway control in week 36 uses the same logic.

5. Feedback inhibition architectures, and what to delete

End-product inhibition acts on the first committed step, because inhibiting anywhere later wastes carbon on intermediates that accumulate. For branched pathways there are three solutions: isozymes, each inhibited by a different end product; concerted inhibition, requiring all end products together; and cumulative inhibition, where each product contributes partial inhibition. Knowing which architecture a pathway uses tells you what a feedback-resistant design must do — deleting one isozyme achieves nothing if two others remain, which is exactly why amino acid strain lineages took decades. Note also that the useful mutation often removes cooperativity rather than affinity, because the sigmoid is what makes the inhibition sharp.

6. PFK is not the pacemaker of glycolysis

Older textbooks name phosphofructokinase as the rate-limiting step. Control analysis in week 31 shows this is wrong in its strong form: control is distributed across the pathway, sums to one, and redistributes the moment you amplify anything. PFK is heavily regulated — ATP inhibits, AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate activate — but heavily regulated is not the same as rate-controlling. Regulation keeps a flux at its set point; control determines how flux responds to a perturbation. Keep the two words distinct; conflating them is the single most common error in metabolic engineering reasoning.

Worked anchor

Why is a sigmoid better than a hyperbola for regulation? Compare saturation at two ligand concentrations, L and 4L, taking the midpoint at 2L.

Hyperbolic (n = 1): at L, v/Vmax = L/(2L + L)... using K at the midpoint 2L, saturation = L/(2L+L) = 0.33 at L, and 4L/(2L+4L) = 0.67 at 4L. A four-fold ligand change doubles activity.

Cooperative (n = 4): saturation = Lⁿ/(Kⁿ + Lⁿ) with K = 2L. At L: 1/(16 + 1) = 0.06. At 4L: 256/(16 + 256) = 0.94. The same four-fold ligand change takes the enzyme from 6% to 94% — effectively off to effectively on.

That is the whole argument for cooperativity, and it is also the argument for removing it when engineering: a feedback-resistant allele that abolishes cooperativity flattens the response so the enzyme stays active as the end product accumulates.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • Hill form with an integer coefficient, not a mechanism
  • K placed at the stated midpoint
  • binding at equilibrium, no competing ligand

Common misconceptions

  • That the Hill coefficient counts binding sites or bound molecules. It is an empirical steepness measure bounded by the site count.
  • That regulated means rate-limiting. Regulation holds a flux at a set point; control determines the response to perturbation, and the two frequently sit on different enzymes.
  • That removing feedback always raises flux. It often raises the intermediate pool instead, until something downstream becomes limiting or an intermediate becomes toxic.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Write the Hill equation and state precisely what n means and what it does not.

A. v/Vmax = [S]ⁿ/(K + [S]ⁿ), or equivalently fractional saturation = [S]ⁿ/(K₀.₅ⁿ + [S]ⁿ). n is an empirical measure of the steepness of the binding curve, bounded above by the number of binding sites, and equal to that number only in the limit of fully concerted all-or-none binding. It is not the number of sites, not the number of molecules bound simultaneously, and not a mechanism — two different mechanisms can give the same n.

Q. A branched pathway uses cumulative inhibition. What must a feedback-resistant design achieve, and why is deleting one isozyme insufficient?

A. Under cumulative inhibition each end product contributes partial inhibition of the shared first step, and the effects compound, so relieving one product's contribution leaves the others intact and the step still substantially inhibited. A resistant design must abolish the binding of every contributing effector, which usually means engineering the allosteric site of that single enzyme rather than deleting genes. Deleting an isozyme is the right move only under the isozyme architecture, where each end product inhibits its own dedicated copy of the first enzyme — and even then the other isozymes keep carrying flux, which is why amino acid strain lineages required several generations of changes.

Q. Distinguish regulation from control in one sentence each, using PFK as the example.

A. Regulation is the cell's mechanism for holding a flux at a set point in the face of changing conditions — PFK is inhibited by ATP and activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate, so glycolytic flux tracks energy demand. Control is the quantitative sensitivity of steady-state flux to a change in an enzyme's activity, measured by the flux control coefficient, and PFK's is typically small — so PFK regulates glycolysis strongly while controlling it weakly, which is why overexpressing it does little.
