Vectors, expression systems and chassis preparation

Week content

Where the construct lives decides how it behaves, and the decisions are quantitative: an origin fixes copy number and so both dosage and segregational stability, a marker commits you to a recurring cost at production scale, a promoter choice sets an inducer bill per cubic metre. The arithmetic most often skipped is the one this week insists on — how many generations a seed train actually takes, and what fraction still carries the plasmid when it gets there.

Core ideas

1. Copy number is a property of the origin's control circuit

Initiation is negatively regulated by a plasmid-encoded antisense RNA or iteron, so copy number is set by a feedback loop and mutations in it move the set point. The usable grades are high (pUC-type, hundreds), medium (p15A and pBR322-type, tens), low (pSC101-type, a handful) and broad-host-range (pBBR1-type, modest and transferable across proteobacteria). Treat published numbers as ranges that depend on host, growth rate and temperature, and note that output saturates in dose: Shuler’s §14.5 rule of thumb is that 25 to 50 copies roughly doubles protein while 50 to 100 gives less than a doubling, because transcription, translation and folding capacity are shared. Two plasmids sharing a control circuit are incompatible, which is why multi-plasmid designs take origins from different families.

2. Segregational loss is set by the copy-number distribution, not the mean

For random partition the probability of a plasmid-free daughter is P = 2^(1−Z) with Z the number of independent replicative units. Shuler's Example 14.1 makes the point: 40 single copies give P = 1.8 × 10⁻¹²; if half the DNA is dimers and a fifth tetramers the units fall to 12 + 10 + 2 = 24 and P = 1.2 × 10⁻⁷; and a population split half at 10 copies and half at 70 — the same mean of 40 — gives P = 9.8 × 10⁻⁴, some 5 × 10⁸ times worse. Multimerisation and copy-number variance decide stability, and a recombination-deficient host suppresses the first.

3. What kills a culture is the growth-rate difference, amplified over generations

Plasmid-free cells appear at R = P·μ⁺ and then outgrow the producers at Δμ = μ⁻ − μ⁺. Shuler's two-population model (eqs. 14.7–14.22) reduces, when Δμ dominates R, to f⁻ ≈ (f⁻₀ + R/Δμ)e^(Δμ·t): the generation rate sets the seed, the growth differential sets the exponent. So reducing burden usually beats reducing loss probability, because Δμ sits in an exponent and P does not, and delaying induction to the last doublings is the cheapest way to hold Δμ near zero for most of a run.

4. Selection costs money at scale and is often not permitted

Selection is free in a flask and not in a vessel: 50 mg L⁻¹ over 50 m³ is 2.5 kg of kanamycin per batch, and the antibiotic is destroyed by the very enzyme you selected for, so maintenance is not guaranteed either. Food and therapeutic products add a residue objection outright. The alternatives — auxotrophic or essential-gene complementation, antitoxin against a chromosomal toxin, operator-repressor titration — convert a consumable into a one-off construction cost.

5. Integration trades tunability for stability

A chromosomal copy segregates with the chromosome, needs no selection and no replication capacity, and removes both failure modes above — at one or a few copies, and with re-engineering now an edit rather than a plasmid prep. The routes are site-specific recombination into attB, homologous recombination at a chosen locus, transposon delivery, and engineered landing pads at characterised sites. Dosage is recovered by integrating at several loci; the honest comparison is dosage and iteration speed against stability and operating cost.

6. Inducible systems are chosen on leakiness, range and inducer economics

lac/IPTG is strong, leaky and expensive in bulk; the ara system is tighter but catabolite-repressed and, with its native transporter, all-or-none across the population at intermediate inducer, so a reported average hides a bimodal culture; tet systems are tight and tunable but the inducer is an antibiotic; rhamnose is graded and cheap. Autoinduction by phosphate depletion, acetoin or quorum sensing removes the inducer cost and the addition step, at the price of tying the switch to a process variable you also care about.

Worked anchor

Generations in a real seed train, and what the loss model predicts.

1 mL at OD 1 to 50 m³ at OD 50: biomass ratio = 5 × 10⁷ in volume × 50 in density = 2.5 × 10⁹, so n = log₂(2.5 × 10⁹) = 31.2 doublings. Thirty-one, where people guess ten.

Segregation alone, for a burdened, partly multimerised plasmid at P = 10⁻⁴ per division: the producing fraction is (1 − 10⁻⁴)³¹ = 0.9969, so 0.3% plasmid-free. Negligible on its own.

Add the growth difference. μ⁺ = 0.60 h⁻¹, μ⁻ = 0.75 h⁻¹, so Δμ = 0.15 h⁻¹ and the 31 doublings take t = 31 × ln2/0.60 = 35.8 h. Then R = P·μ⁺ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ h⁻¹, R/Δμ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴, and with f⁻₀ = 0 the Case 1 solution gives

f⁻ ≈ 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ × e^(0.15 × 35.8) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 215 = 0.086.

Nine per cent of the harvest is plasmid-free — thirty times the segregation-only answer from the same P. Halving the burden to Δμ = 0.075 h⁻¹ gives f⁻ ≈ 0.012, a sevenfold improvement that costs nothing per batch, while cutting P tenfold gives only 0.0086: P enters linearly and Δμ enters the exponent.

The inducer line, for comparison: IPTG at 0.1 mM in 50 m³ is 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ × 5 × 10⁷ L × 238 g mol⁻¹ ≈ 1.2 kg per batch. Induction at the last two doublings is a process decision, not a preference.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • 1 mL at OD 1 to 50 m³ at OD 50, so a biomass ratio of 2.5×10⁹
  • every doubling productive, no lag and no death
  • P = 2^(1−Z) with Z the same in every cell
  • Δμ and R constant over the whole train
  • the Case 1 approximation, Δμ ≫ R, with f⁻₀ = 0
  • IPTG at 0.1 mM throughout the final vessel volume

Common misconceptions

  • That a high-copy origin means high product. Output saturates above roughly 50 copies, and the extra copies cost replication capacity and segregational margin.
  • That antibiotic selection holds a plasmid in a production vessel. The resistance gene destroys or modifies the antibiotic, so pressure falls during the run — and 2.5 kg of kanamycin per 50 m³ batch is a cost, not a control.
  • That mean copy number predicts stability. P = 2^(1−Z) is set by replicative units in individual cells, so multimers and a broad distribution can worsen loss by eight orders of magnitude at constant mean.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. How many generations does a 50 m³ seed train take, and what does your loss model predict for an unselected plasmid?

A. From 1 mL at OD 1 to 50 m³ at OD 50 the biomass ratio is 2.5 × 10⁹, so 31 doublings. At P = 10⁻⁴, segregation alone leaves 0.3% plasmid-free; add a 0.15 h⁻¹ growth advantage over the 35.8 h the train takes and Case 1 gives about 9%. The asymmetry is the lesson: P enters linearly and Δμ enters an exponent, so burden reduction and late induction beat stability engineering.

Q. Your product is a food ingredient and the host is a Lactobacillus. Argue the vector decision.

A. Antibiotic markers are out on residue and resistance grounds, which removes the easiest selection and most off-the-shelf shuttle vectors with it, and transformation efficiency is binding. That leaves a food-grade shuttle vector with auxotrophic or essential-gene complementation, or integration with the marker excised, which also removes the plasmid-free population from a long fermentation. The cost is iteration speed: screen week 34's library on a plasmid and integrate only the winner.

Q. Two plasmids, the same mean copy number, and one culture collapses. What do you measure?

A. The distribution and the multimer fraction, not the mean: single-cell copy number by quantitative PCR on sorted cells, and undigested plasmid on a gel for dimers and higher multimers. Both cut the number of independent replicative units, and P = 2^(1−Z) is exponential in Z. Then measure Δμ directly, by competing plasmid-bearing against plasmid-free cells in one vessel.
