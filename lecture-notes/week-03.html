Protein structure and folding

Week content

The catalyst is encoded, folded, degraded and evolvable — four properties your reactor catalysts never had. This week builds the structural vocabulary you need to read an enzyme's datasheet critically, and it plants the two facts that govern protein engineering in week 37: folding is marginally stable, and activity and stability trade against each other.

Core ideas

1. Four levels, and which interactions hold each

Primary is the covalent sequence, which determines everything else. Secondary is local backbone hydrogen bonding: α-helix with 3.6 residues per turn and i to i+4 bonding, β-sheet parallel or antiparallel with inter-strand bonding, and turns. Tertiary is the three-dimensional fold, held by the hydrophobic core, plus hydrogen bonds, ionic pairs and sometimes disulfides. Quaternary is subunit assembly, which matters to you because it determines whether a gene deletion removes the activity — a heteromeric complex is an AND in the gene-protein-reaction rule of week 21, and that distinction is a leading source of false essentiality predictions.

2. Folding is marginally stable, and that is a design constraint

ΔG of folding is typically −20 to −60 kJ/mol: the difference between two large numbers, each hundreds of kJ/mol. A single buried hydrophobic-to-polar substitution can cost 5–15 kJ/mol, so a handful of such mutations unfolds a protein. Two consequences. First, random mutagenesis libraries lose activity mostly by destabilising the fold, not by hitting the active site — which is why week 37's campaigns begin by raising stability to create a mutational buffer. Second, thermostability engineering is additive and achievable, because you are adding small increments to a small number.

3. The active site is a small, strained, unrepresentative part of the protein

Catalytic residues are frequently charged in hydrophobic environments, held in strained geometries, and conserved far beyond what stability requires. That is the structural origin of the stability-activity trade-off: the active site is optimised for catalysis at the cost of being locally suboptimal for folding. When you look at a structure, the residues within 5 Å of the substrate are the ones you cannot freely mutate; the surface is where most tolerated variation lives.

4. Disulfides, the periplasm, and why secretion sometimes decides the host

Disulfide bonds form in oxidising environments. The bacterial cytoplasm is reducing, so a protein needing disulfides will not fold correctly there — it must go to the periplasm, which has the Dsb oxidation machinery, or to the eukaryotic ER. This is not a detail: it decides host and compartment for whole product classes, and it is why 'express it in E. coli' fails for many secreted eukaryotic proteins regardless of codon optimisation.

5. Protein lifetime is set by growth, not by degradation, for most enzymes

Bacterial proteins are mostly stable: active degradation targets misfolded, tagged or regulatory proteins, while a well-folded metabolic enzyme is removed principally by dilution as the cell divides. Its effective half-life is therefore ln2/µ — about 42 minutes at µ = 1.0 h⁻¹ and 3.5 hours at µ = 0.2 h⁻¹. Three things follow. At steady state the enzyme concentration is its synthesis rate divided by (µ + kdeg), so a slow-growing cell accumulates more enzyme from the same promoter, which is why expression level and growth rate cannot be varied independently. Switching off a promoter does not remove an enzyme quickly; it dilutes it over several generations, which bounds how fast dynamic regulation in week 36 can act downwards. And a degron tag, which imposes real degradation, is the remedy when you need faster removal than dilution provides.

6. Inclusion bodies: a rate problem, not a sequence problem

Folding competes with aggregation. The nascent chain exposes hydrophobic surface; if folding is slower than synthesis, those surfaces find each other instead of their own core. So the remedies all work by slowing synthesis relative to folding: lower temperature, weaker promoter or less inducer, co-expressed chaperones, or periplasmic export. Note the diagnostic implication, which is the single most useful troubleshooting fact in the course: high transcript with low activity is most often resolved by separating soluble from insoluble protein and running a gel — a one-day experiment that halves the hypothesis space.

7. Reading a structure, and reading a predicted structure

A crystal structure is a model fitted to density, with a resolution and an R-factor you should check; side-chain positions at 2.8 Å are far less certain than the backbone, and loops are often missing entirely. A predicted structure carries a per-residue confidence, is generally reliable for the fold and the core, and is unreliable exactly where you most want it: flexible loops, induced-fit rearrangements, ligand positions, and the effect of mutations. Use prediction to locate the active site and the conserved core; do not use it to argue that a substitution will work.

Worked anchor

Estimate how many destabilising mutations a protein tolerates. Take ΔG(folding) = −40 kJ/mol and an average destabilising substitution at −8 kJ/mol.

Five such substitutions bring ΔG to roughly zero, at which point half the protein population is unfolded at equilibrium and, in vivo, the unfolded fraction is degraded or aggregates. In practice soluble expression collapses well before that, typically after two or three.

Now the library arithmetic that follows: error-prone PCR at an average of 3 substitutions per gene, with perhaps a third of random substitutions destabilising by this much, means a large fraction of your library is already non-functional before you ask anything about activity. That is the quantitative reason week 37 recommends raising stability first, and the reason a library's useful size is far smaller than its nominal size.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • two-state folding at equilibrium, ΔG = −40 kJ/mol
  • an average destabilising substitution costs 8 kJ/mol
  • substitution effects additive and independent
  • roughly a third of random substitutions destabilise by that much

Common misconceptions

  • That more disulfides means more stable. In a reducing cytoplasm an unformed disulfide is worse than none, and misoxidised pairs are a common cause of aggregation.
  • That an AlphaFold model settles a mechanistic question. It predicts a fold well and a ligand pose or a mutational effect poorly, which is exactly where mechanism lives.
  • That inclusion bodies mean the gene is wrong. They usually mean synthesis outpaced folding, which is a rate problem fixed by expression conditions.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Name the four catalytic strategies' structural requirements and explain why active-site residues are often locally destabilising.

A. Covalent catalysis needs a positioned nucleophile, often with a residue network lowering its pKa. General acid-base needs an ionisable group with a pKa near the operating pH, usually histidine. Metal catalysis needs a coordination geometry held by several ligating residues. Proximity needs a rigid scaffold holding two substrates in register. All four require charged or polar residues in constrained, frequently buried, geometries — which is locally unfavourable for folding, since burial of charge is costly and strain is energetically expensive. Hence the stability-activity trade-off: the active site is optimised for catalysis at the fold's expense.

Q. Your protein needs two disulfide bonds. Where do you express it, and why does the cytoplasm fail?

A. The periplasm of E. coli, using a signal peptide and the Dsb oxidation system, or a eukaryotic host's endoplasmic reticulum. The bacterial cytoplasm is maintained reducing by thioredoxin and glutaredoxin systems, so cysteines stay reduced and the disulfides either do not form or form incorrectly, giving misfolded protein and aggregation. Engineered oxidising-cytoplasm strains exist as a third option.

Q. Transcript is abundant, activity is low. What is the first experiment, and what does each outcome rule out?

A. Fractionate the lysate into soluble and insoluble and run a gel. Protein present in the insoluble fraction means it is being made but misfolding, which rules out transcription, translation and degradation problems and points at expression rate, temperature or chaperones. Protein absent from both fractions means it is not being made or is degraded immediately, which rules out aggregation and points at translation initiation, codon usage or proteolysis. Protein soluble and abundant means the problem is downstream of expression entirely: cofactor, substrate supply, thermodynamics or inhibition.
