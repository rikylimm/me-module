Network reconstruction and the stoichiometric matrix

Week content

Case thread: Build a toy network from cassava sugars. Label every reaction as hypothetical. Open the case thread.

The stoichiometric matrix is a connectivity map, a data table and a linear operator at once. Once you see that it is the mass-balance matrix you have written since second year, the rest of the phase is linear algebra with biology attached. Build one by hand this week, in a notebook, and verify that every column balances before you trust anything built on it.

Core ideas

1. What S is, concretely

Rows are metabolites, columns are reactions, and the entry Sᵢⱼ is the stoichiometric coefficient of metabolite i in reaction j — negative if consumed, positive if produced, zero if absent. A genome-scale model of E. coli has roughly 1,800 metabolites and 2,600 reactions, so S has about 4.7 million entries of which perhaps 10,000 are non-zero: it is more than 99% empty. That sparsity is not incidental. It says that each metabolite participates in only a handful of reactions, which is why metabolism has recognisable pathways at all rather than being a homogeneous soup.

2. Exchange reactions and the system boundary

A reconstruction needs a boundary. Exchange reactions are pseudo-reactions that let a metabolite enter or leave the system — written conventionally as 'A_e ⇌' with uptake as negative flux. They are not real reactions and they have no genes, which matters twice: they are where you define the medium, and they are where models most often wrongly permit growth, because a draft reconstruction that adds an exchange for every extracellular metabolite will predict utilisation of substrates the organism cannot transport. When a model grows on something it should not, check the exchanges and the transporters before anything else.

3. The six axioms, which are worth stating properly

Palsson sets out the conceptual basis as six statements. Cellular functions rest on chemistry, so they can be written as balanced equations. Annotated genomes plus experimental data permit genome-scale reconstruction. Cells operate under constraints — physico-chemical, topological, environmental and regulatory. Cells function in a context-specific way, so the expressed subset matters. Mass and energy are conserved, which gives S·v = 0 at steady state. And cells evolve under selection, which licenses optimality arguments. None is startling alone; together they are the whole justification for what follows, and the sixth is the only one that is an assumption about behaviour rather than physics.

4. Module-by-module reconstruction, and why glycolysis is taught first

You build a reconstruction by adding modules and checking each: glycolysis, then the pentose phosphate pathway, then the TCA cycle, verifying balance and recomputing the null space at each stage. Palsson teaches this on glycolysis because its matrix is small enough to read by eye and its null space is interpretable by inspection. Do the same: build your phase 2 pathway atlas as a matrix, column by column, and assert that each column balances for C, H, O, N, P and charge. The assertion is the point — a mistyped transketolase coefficient will not announce itself later.

5. Protein states can be stoichiometric too

Regulation can be represented in the same formalism. The PFK example writes each bound state of the enzyme as a species and each binding event as a reaction, so an allosteric enzyme becomes a subnetwork of twenty-odd reactions within the matrix. This is worth seeing once because it shows the formalism's reach and its cost: representing one regulated enzyme properly adds more reactions than the whole of glycolysis, which is why genome-scale models do not do it and why regulation is handled by constraints instead.

6. A reconstruction is not a model

The reconstruction is the knowledge base: reactions, genes, evidence, localisation, directionality. The model is the reconstruction plus the constraints needed to compute — bounds, a medium, an objective. One reconstruction supports many condition-specific models, and the distinction matters when you read a paper: a claim about the reconstruction is a claim about biochemical knowledge, while a claim from a model is conditional on the constraints someone chose.

Worked anchor

Build S for a three-reaction toy and read it.

Reactions: (1) A → B, (2) B → C, (3) B → D, plus exchanges for A in, C out and D out.

Metabolites A, B, C, D as rows; reactions v₁, v₂, v₃ and exchanges b_A, b_C, b_D as columns:

v₁ v₂ v₃ b_A b_C b_D A −1 0 0 +1 0 0 B +1 −1 −1 0 0 0 C 0 +1 0 0 −1 0 D 0 0 +1 0 0 −1

Four metabolites, six reactions. Rank is 4, so the null space has dimension 2: two degrees of freedom. Choose a basis and the two vectors are recognisable as the two routes — A to C and A to D — which is the simplest possible demonstration that null space basis vectors are pathways.

Now the check that matters: every column sums correctly in atoms, not just in species. If A, B and C are all C₃ compounds and D is C₂, then reaction 3 is unbalanced and no amount of linear algebra will tell you so. Assert elemental balance in code before you compute anything.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the six listed reactions are the whole network
  • rank computed over the reals, with no thermodynamic constraints yet
  • exchanges as drawn

Common misconceptions

  • That S is a model. It is a data structure; the model is S plus bounds, a medium and an objective.
  • That a balanced column means a correct reaction. Balance is necessary and not sufficient — a balanced reaction can still be one the organism does not perform.
  • That exchange reactions are reactions. They are boundary conditions with no genes, and they are where spurious growth predictions usually originate.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. What does the sparsity of S tell you about metabolism?

A. That each metabolite participates in only a handful of reactions out of thousands — typically two or three. That is the structural reason metabolism decomposes into recognisable pathways rather than being a uniformly connected network, and it is why pathway-based thinking works at all. It is also why genome-scale linear programs solve quickly despite their size: sparse matrices are cheap.

Q. A draft model predicts growth on citrate, which the strain cannot use. Where do you look first?

A. At the exchange and transport reactions, not the intracellular pathway. Draft reconstructions frequently add an exchange and a generic transporter for every extracellular metabolite, so the intracellular citrate metabolism is present and correct while the organism in fact lacks an aerobic citrate uptake system. Check whether the transport reaction has a gene associated with it; if it does not, it is a modelling artefact.

Q. Distinguish a reconstruction from a model in three sentences.

A. A reconstruction is a structured knowledge base: every reaction with its stoichiometry, gene association, evidence, directionality and localisation. A model is that reconstruction plus the constraints required to compute a flux distribution — uptake bounds, a defined medium, capacity limits and an objective function. One reconstruction therefore supports many models, and a claim made from a model is conditional on constraints a person chose, whereas a claim about the reconstruction is a claim about biochemical knowledge.
