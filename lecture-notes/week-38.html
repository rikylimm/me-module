Data-driven protein design and high-throughput screening

Week content

The part of the field that changed most recently, and the week where the discipline is epistemic rather than arithmetic. The governing fact is an asymmetry in the data: there are over 100 million protein sequences and over 100,000 structures, and almost no sequence-to-function measurements. Everything these models can and cannot do follows from that, and so does the only honest way to test them — which is not a random split.

Core ideas

1. The data asymmetry is the whole explanation

Protein Engineering Ch 6 states the position plainly: UniProt holds more than 100 million unique sequences and the Protein Data Bank more than 100,000 experimentally determined structures, both growing exponentially, while data mapping sequence to function remains scarce. The chapter's further observation matters more than the counts — function data is hard to standardise because it depends on assay conditions and even on who ran the assay, so statistical models are most useful on data generated by one group under one protocol. Two consequences follow. Models trained on sequences alone can only have learned what evolution constrained, because that is the only signal in the training set. And a supervised model of your enzyme's activity has to be trained on your measurements, which means the campaign generates its own training data and the cost of the first batch is unavoidable.

2. A sequence model predicts likelihood under evolutionary constraint, not activity

Trained to reconstruct masked or next residues across a large sequence database, a protein language model learns the statistics of what has survived selection. Its per-position output is therefore a statement about how constrained a site has been across the family, which correlates with being important for something — folding, binding, catalysis, regulation, or an interaction with a partner the engineering project does not care about. It does not distinguish between these, and it cannot: nothing in the training signal separates a residue conserved because it is catalytic from one conserved because it contacts a chaperone. Zero-shot variant effect prediction works as well as it does because most substitutions at highly constrained positions are bad, which is a statement about the base rate and not about the variant.

3. Conservation and the active site agree in the middle and disagree at the edges

Mapping per-position conservation onto the structural active site from week 3 produces three categories, and the third is the useful one. Conserved and in the first shell: catalytic or substrate-contacting, and not to be randomised. Variable and remote: free, and where a stability campaign can work. Conserved and remote is the interesting class — these are folding nuclei, allosteric paths, interface residues and sites under constraint for reasons outside the assay, and they are exactly where a model's confidence is high and its relevance to the engineering objective is unknown. The converse class, variable and in the first shell, is where directed evolution has historically found its gains, because evolution had no reason to optimise for the non-natural substrate.

4. Supervised fitness models fail on combinations because epistasis is not in the training set

A model trained on single mutants learns a sum of independent effects, because that is all single-mutant data can identify. Epistasis — the dependence of one substitution's effect on another's presence — is by construction unmeasured, so the model extrapolates additively into a space where additivity is the thing being violated. This is the specific reason that a model with excellent held-out accuracy on singles predicts double and triple mutants badly, and it is also why the limonene epoxide hydrolase result is informative: Protein Engineering notes that CAST/ISM produces strong cooperative effects beyond additivity, which is exactly the regime a single-mutant model cannot see. Deep mutational scanning supplies millions of variants, but of one property, in one assay, usually at low mutational depth.

5. A predicted structure supports a pocket and not an energy

Structure prediction is reliable enough for fold assignment, domain boundaries and the approximate shape and lining of a binding pocket, which is sufficient for choosing CAST positions — and choosing CAST positions is what week 37 needed a structure for. It does not deliver the conformational ensemble, the transition state geometry, or folding free energy differences at the few kJ mol⁻¹ resolution that a stability prediction needs. Docking into a predicted structure inherits both limits and adds its own scoring-function error. The practical rule is that a predicted structure is admissible for deciding where to randomise and inadmissible as evidence for a mechanism, which is also the rule for a crystal structure, only more so.

6. Active learning converts a model into a campaign, and the arithmetic is cost per improvement

The loop is: measure a batch, fit, propose the next batch, repeat. Three parameters decide it. Batch size is set by what the assay runs at once, not by the algorithm. Acquisition strategy sets the exploration-exploitation balance — proposing only the predicted best exploits a model that is wrong off-distribution, while proposing only the most uncertain learns fast and delivers nothing. The stopping rule is the same kind of object as week 37's: a measurable result by a date. The comparison that matters is total measurements per unit improvement, and it has a break-even, because the first batch of a model-guided campaign buys no improvement at all.

7. Screening technology is matched to the property, and the numbers are published

Lee §9.5.1 gives the throughput ladder for metabolite measurement explicitly. With a chromatographic separation it is difficult to go below five minutes per sample, and resolving similar compounds in a complex matrix reduces throughput to 200–400 samples per day. Omitting separation and relying on high-resolution or tandem mass spectrometry with flow injection or direct infusion gives about one sample per minute, so 1,000–2,000 per day, and makes untargeted profiling of up to 10,000 samples in a few weeks possible — the approach used to profile whole single-knockout collections. The trade is the one every rung on every ladder in this phase has: throughput against the ability to resolve isomers and suppress matrix effects. A screen that cannot separate the product from its isomer has a false-positive rate, not a throughput.

8. Structures are becoming an omics layer, which is the same integration problem in a new coordinate

Lee §2.6.3 reports that complete structural proteomes have been assembled for the current E. coli and human reconstructions, and that the GEM-PRO workflow evaluates a genome-scale model down to tertiary structure. The three stated uses are worth holding onto because they are claims about what structures buy: physicochemical properties of the structural proteome become an omics data source supporting residue-level analysis; structure-based functional assignment improves the model's proteome allocation predictions; and fold and family distributions across a network allow comparison between strains and species. This is the same move as every integration in week 39 — a measurement layer mapped onto a mechanistic network so that correlations can become causes — and it inherits the same risks.

Worked anchor

Cost per improvement: classical directed evolution against a machine-learning-guided loop, on the same target as week 37. The assay is the microtitre screen at 10⁴ wells per week; take the fully loaded cost per well as the unit, so the answer is in wells rather than currency and survives a change of reagent price.

Classical, as designed in week 37. Two NDT sites of three residues, 5,175 transformants per site for 95% coverage, two rounds. Total measurements 10,350 wells, plus about 50 confirmation assays. Expected outcome, from the published base rate for semi-rational campaigns, is a two- to five-fold improvement, so take four-fold. Cost per fold of improvement: 10,400/4 = 2,600 wells per fold.

Machine-learning-guided. The first batch has to be measured before the model exists. Ch 6's framing says the useful training data is your own, under one protocol, so take a design-of-experiment batch of 500 variants sampled across the same six positions — one week of screening — to fit a sequence-function model. Then run active learning in batches of 96, the plate size, at one batch per round. Suppose six rounds. Total measurements 500 + 6 × 96 = 1,076 wells, about an eighth of the classical campaign.

The comparison turns entirely on the expected improvement, which is where honesty is required. If the model-guided loop achieves the same four-fold, the cost is 269 wells per fold, a factor of ten better. If epistasis defeats it and it achieves two-fold, it is 538 wells per fold, still better. If it achieves nothing because 500 training variants are too few to identify interactions among six positions — and the number of pairwise interactions among six NDT positions is 15 pairs times 144 combinations, which is 2,160 parameters against 500 observations — it costs 1,076 wells and returns zero.

That last line is the actual result of the calculation. The model is underdetermined at the batch size the assay permits, so the honest design is not a choice between the two but a hybrid: run site one classically at 5,175 wells to get a dense, high-coverage data set at one site, use it to fit a model that is identifiable because it only has to cover 12³ = 1,728 combinations at one site, and use the model to propose site two's 96 variants rather than screening its 5,175. Total 5,175 + 500 + 96 = 5,771 wells against 10,400, with the first round's result in hand before the model is trusted with anything.

The break-even condition, stated generally: the model-guided arm wins when the measurements saved on later rounds exceed the measurements spent on training, which requires the training set to be large relative to the number of interaction parameters at the positions being modelled. That ratio, not the model class, is what to compute before committing.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • cost measured in assay wells rather than currency, so reagent price changes do not move the comparison
  • the classical arm as designed in week 37: two NDT sites of three residues, 5,175 transformants each, two rounds
  • a four-fold improvement assumed for the classical arm, taken as the midpoint of reported two- to five-fold outcomes
  • active-learning batch size fixed at 96 by the plate rather than chosen by the algorithm, and six rounds assumed
  • a 500-variant training set taken as the largest affordable first batch at one week of screening
  • pairwise interaction parameters counted as 15 position pairs × 144 amino acid combinations = 2,160 under NDT, with higher-order terms ignored
  • identifiability judged by parameters against observations alone, with no regularisation or prior credited
  • throughput figures for separation-based and flow-injection metabolite screening taken from Lee §9.5.1 as reported

Common misconceptions

  • That a protein language model knows which residues are catalytic. It knows which residues have been constrained across the family, and nothing in its training signal distinguishes catalysis from folding, allostery, or a contact with a partner irrelevant to your assay. The informative class after mapping conservation onto structure is conserved-and-remote, precisely because the model is confident there and its reason is unknown.
  • That a model with good held-out accuracy on single mutants will rank double mutants well. Single-mutant data cannot identify interaction terms, so the fit is additive by construction and extrapolates additively into the regime where additivity fails. The number to check is parameters against observations: six NDT positions have 2,160 pairwise interaction parameters, which a 500-variant training set cannot determine.
  • That a predicted structure is weaker evidence than a crystal structure for the same claim. It is weaker, but the error that matters is the same one: both are a single static model standing in for an ensemble, and neither supports a transition state or a folding free energy at the resolution a stability prediction needs. A predicted structure is admissible for choosing where to randomise, which is what it is used for here.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. What can a protein language model not extrapolate to, and why does that follow from how it was trained?

A. It cannot extrapolate to anything evolution had no occasion to constrain: activity on a non-natural substrate, stability at a temperature the family never experienced, expression in a non-native host, or tolerance of a process solvent. The training objective is reconstruction of residues from sequence context across a database of sequences that exist, so the only signal available is which residues selection preserved. It therefore scores a variant by how typical it is of the family, and a variant that is atypical because it is bad and one that is atypical because it does something new are indistinguishable to it. This is also why its predictions degrade as mutational distance from the family grows, which is the direction every engineering campaign travels.

Q. You are given a published ML-guided design paper reporting a 20-fold improvement. Name three claims it probably does not support.

A. First, that the model caused the improvement, unless there is a matched arm in which the same number of variants was chosen at random or by a baseline additive model — without it, the result is confounded with the library design and the number of variants measured. Second, that the reported accuracy generalises, if the train-test split was random over variants rather than held out by position or by mutational depth: a random split leaks, because a double mutant in the test set shares both of its substitutions with singles in the training set. Third, that the method transfers, if the training data came from one assay in one laboratory — which Ch 6 argues is the condition under which such data is usable at all, and which therefore also bounds its transferability. A fourth worth checking is whether the 20-fold is in the screen's units or in the confirmation assay on purified protein, since proxies inflate.

Q. Your assay runs 96 wells a week and you want a model of six positions. What do you do?

A. Not fit the model of six positions. The interaction terms alone are 15 pairs by 144 amino acid combinations, about 2,160 parameters under NDT, and at 96 wells a week a training set large enough to determine them is years of assay. The three available moves are: reduce the positions modelled, fitting one site of three at 12³ = 1,728 combinations, which a dense classical round at that site already samples; reduce the alphabet, since a three-amino-acid code at six positions is 3⁶ = 729 combinations and a few hundred measurements become meaningful; or raise the throughput by changing format, which means making the property fluorescent and cell-associated and so returns to week 36's biosensor. The general rule is to compute parameters against achievable observations before choosing a model class, because the assay's throughput, not the algorithm, determines what is identifiable.
