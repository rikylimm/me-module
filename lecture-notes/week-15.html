Lipids, membranes, transport and uptake

Week content

Substrate must get in and product must get out. Uptake kinetics sets your maximum rate; export, or its absence, sets your toxicity ceiling. Most failed strain designs fail at the membrane rather than in the pathway, and this week is where you learn to spot that before building anything.

Core ideas

1. The bilayer, and why a hydrophobic product is a membrane problem

A phospholipid bilayer is about 4 nm thick with a hydrophobic interior equivalent to a thin oil phase, and its fluidity is tuned by fatty acid chain length and unsaturation — which is how cells adapt to temperature and to solvents. A hydrophobic product partitions into that interior according to its octanol-water coefficient, so the aqueous titre you measure is not the concentration the membrane experiences. As it accumulates the bilayer disorders, the proton-motive force leaks, membrane proteins lose activity, and the cell arrests. This is why alcohols above about C4, terpenoids and many aromatics have titre ceilings well below their stoichiometric ones, and why the countermeasures are efflux pumps, membrane engineering, or in situ product removal rather than more pathway flux.

2. Fatty acid synthesis: the acetyl-CoA and NADPH sink

The cycle extends a growing chain by two carbons per round, using malonyl-CoA as the extender and consuming two NADPH per round. A C16 fatty acid therefore costs 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 ATP for the carboxylations and 14 NADPH. Two consequences. Lipid is the most expensive cell component per gram, which is why lipid content matters in a biomass equation. And malonyl-CoA, made by acetyl-CoA carboxylase, is held at very low concentration and tightly regulated — which is why flavonoid, polyketide and fatty-acid-derived products are so often precursor-limited at the first step, and why acetyl-CoA carboxylase overexpression appears in so many of those papers.

3. Four transport modes, with their energy source and stoichiometry

Passive diffusion: no carrier, no energy, down-gradient only, and significant only for small uncharged species — oxygen, CO₂, water, the undissociated acids of week 2. Facilitated diffusion: a carrier gives specificity and rate but still only down-gradient. Primary active transport: ATP hydrolysed directly, as in ABC transporters, typically one ATP per solute. Secondary active transport: the proton-motive force spent via symport or antiport, so the cost is indirect but real, and the stoichiometry is usually one H⁺ per solute. Group translocation: the phosphotransferase system, chemically modifying the substrate during transit. Always ask which mode a substrate uses before computing a yield, because the answer changes the ATP balance and sometimes the carbon balance too.

4. The PTS, and why it is a stoichiometric decision

Write the chain out: PEP phosphorylates EI, which phosphorylates HPr, which phosphorylates EIIA, then EIIB, which phosphorylates the glucose as EIIC translocates it. Net: one PEP consumed per glucose imported, arriving as glucose-6-phosphate. Glycolysis makes 2 PEP per glucose, so after PTS uptake only 1 remains — halving the ceiling for any PEP-derived product, as the week 14 phenylalanine calculation showed. Replacing the PTS with a galactose permease plus glucokinase restores both PEP at the cost of one ATP, which must come from somewhere; in practice that means the replacement pays off in aerobic strains with ATP to spare and not in anaerobic ones. The PTS also has a second role: its phosphorylation state is the sensor for catabolite repression.

5. Catabolite repression and the mixed-feedstock problem

When glucose is present, the PTS components are largely dephosphorylated, which lowers cyclic AMP and so lowers activation of promoters for alternative carbon sources, while EIIA inhibits the uptake systems for those sources directly. The result is a hierarchy: glucose first, then a lag, then the second sugar. For a lignocellulosic feedstock containing glucose and xylose this is a direct economic problem, since the diauxic lag wastes fermenter time and the xylose may be left unconsumed. The three standard remedies: delete or modify the PTS so the sensing is broken, express the xylose system constitutively, or split the task between two strains in co-culture — each with its own costs, and the first often reducing growth rate on glucose.

6. Export, efflux and compartments

Product leaves by passive diffusion if it can, by a native transporter if one happens to exist, or not at all — in which case the product accumulates to toxicity and must be recovered by lysis. Engineering export means either finding an efflux pump that accepts the product, which is a screening problem, or using a host with relevant native transporters. In yeast there is a further option: place the pathway in a compartment, which isolates a toxic intermediate from the cytosol but creates a transport problem for every substrate and cofactor the pathway needs. Note that cofactor pools differ between compartments, so a pathway moved to the peroxisome may lose its NADPH supply unless a shuttle exists — a failure mode that has caught several published designs.

Worked anchor

Does replacing the PTS pay? Compute the ceiling for a PEP-derived product both ways.

With PTS: glucose → 2 PEP, minus 1 PEP consumed in uptake, leaves 1 PEP. Ceiling: 1 mol product per mol glucose.

Without PTS, using GalP permease plus glucokinase: glucose enters on the proton-motive force (one H⁺, which the cell must regenerate by respiration) and is phosphorylated by glucokinase at the cost of 1 ATP. Both PEP remain. Ceiling: 2 mol per mol — but you are now 1 ATP short, and if the only way to make it is to run 1 PEP through pyruvate kinase, you are back towards 1.

So the question is where the compensating ATP comes from. Aerobically, respiration supplies it at the cost of a little extra oxygen demand, and the ceiling genuinely approaches 2 — which is why galP-glk strains are the standard in aerobic aromatic production. Anaerobically there is no such source, so the replacement buys little.

Two further costs that papers often omit: glucose uptake rate usually falls after PTS deletion, because the permease is slower, so titre rises while productivity may fall; and catabolite repression is disrupted, which may be desirable for mixed feedstocks or undesirable for regulation you were relying on. State both when you propose this intervention — it is the sort of balanced answer that distinguishes a considered design.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • GalP plus glucokinase fully functional at the required flux
  • proton-motive force regenerated by respiration
  • one ATP per phosphorylation
  • a stoichiometric ceiling, with kinetics and regulation set aside

Common misconceptions

  • That measured titre is the concentration the cell experiences. A hydrophobic product partitions into the membrane, so the bilayer sees far more than the aqueous phase reports.
  • That PTS replacement is a free doubling of yield. It restores PEP but costs an ATP and usually slows uptake, so it pays aerobically and rarely anaerobically.
  • That moving a pathway to a yeast compartment is simply isolation. Cofactor pools differ between compartments, so NADPH supply must be checked or arranged — several published designs failed exactly here.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Write the PTS stoichiometry for glucose and state the yield consequence for a PEP-derived product.

A. PEP + glucose(out) → pyruvate + glucose-6-phosphate(in), via the EI, HPr, EIIA, EIIB, EIIC phosphorelay. One PEP is consumed per glucose imported. Since glycolysis produces 2 PEP per glucose, only 1 remains for a PEP-derived product, halving the stoichiometric ceiling from 2 to 1 mol per mol. Replacement by a permease plus glucokinase restores both PEP at the cost of 1 ATP, which pays off aerobically where respiration can supply that ATP.

Q. A hydrophobic product is limited at 8 g/L, well below its stoichiometric ceiling. Give three mechanisms and a countermeasure for each.

A. Membrane partitioning disordering the bilayer and dissipating the proton-motive force — countered by membrane engineering, by evolved tolerance, or by in situ product removal that keeps the aqueous concentration low. Inhibition of specific membrane proteins including transporters and respiratory complexes — countered by identifying and overexpressing the affected component, or again by ISPR. Absence of an export route, so the product accumulates intracellularly far above the measured aqueous titre — countered by screening efflux pumps or by choosing a host with relevant transporters, and diagnosed by measuring the intracellular concentration rather than inferring it.

Q. Why does catabolite repression matter economically for a lignocellulosic feedstock, and what are the options?

A. Because the hydrolysate contains glucose and xylose, and repression causes sequential rather than simultaneous use: a diauxic lag wastes fermenter time, lowering volumetric productivity, and the xylose may be incompletely consumed, lowering yield on total sugar. Options: break the sensing by modifying or deleting PTS components, express the xylose uptake and catabolic genes constitutively, or split the sugars between two strains in co-culture. Each has a cost — PTS modification commonly reduces growth rate on glucose, constitutive expression imposes burden, and co-culture adds process complexity and stability risk.
