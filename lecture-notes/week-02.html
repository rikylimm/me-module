Water, bonds and the chemistry of the cytosol

Week content

Case thread: The thesis reports broth pH. What would distinguish lactate from other acids, and what remains unmeasured? Open the case thread.

The chemistry of the solvent everything happens in. Engineers routinely skip this and then misread a pH-dependent titre collapse as a biological mystery. The week's payload is small and permanent: the four weak interactions and their energies, the Henderson-Hasselbalch relation, and the mechanism by which a weak-acid product poisons the cell that makes it.

Core ideas

1. The hydrophobic effect is entropic, and that is the whole point

A non-polar solute in water forces the surrounding water molecules into a more ordered cage, which costs entropy. Aggregating non-polar surfaces together releases that ordered water, so the driving force for protein folding, membrane formation and substrate binding is largely the entropy of the released water rather than any attraction between the non-polar groups themselves. Consequences you will use: the hydrophobic core of a protein is not glued, it is squeezed; a bilayer assembles spontaneously; and a hydrophobic product will partition into the membrane rather than stay in the aqueous phase, which is the week 15 toxicity problem.

2. Four weak interactions, with their energies

Hydrogen bond, 4–30 kJ/mol depending on geometry and environment. Ionic interaction, 2–20 kJ/mol in water (far stronger in a protein interior where the dielectric is low, which is why buried charges are rare and catalytically interesting when present). Van der Waals, about 1–4 kJ/mol per contact but numerous. Hydrophobic effect, context-dependent and entropy-driven. Compare these with a covalent bond at 350–450 kJ/mol and with thermal energy RT ≈ 2.6 kJ/mol at 310 K. Two conclusions: individually these interactions are barely above noise, so specificity comes from many of them acting together over a complementary surface; and a protein's folded state is only 20–60 kJ/mol more stable than its unfolded state, which is a handful of hydrogen bonds.

3. pKa and Henderson-Hasselbalch, derived not memorised

For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. Take logarithms and rearrange: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). At pH = pKa the two forms are equal, and that is where buffering capacity is maximal because adding acid or base moves the ratio least on a log scale. Useful pKa values: phosphate 2.1/7.2/12.3, so phosphate buffers near neutrality; carbonate 6.4/10.3; acetate 4.76; lactate 3.86; citrate 3.1/4.8/6.4; the imidazole of histidine about 6.0, which is why histidine does general acid-base catalysis at physiological pH; cysteine thiol about 8.4, lowered substantially in an active site.

4. The weak-acid mechanism, which is the week's real content

Only the undissociated acid HA is lipophilic enough to cross a membrane by passive diffusion; the anion A⁻ cannot. Outside the cell at low pH, a substantial fraction of the product is HA. It diffuses in. Inside, where pH is near 7.2, it dissociates, releasing a proton into the cytosol and leaving an anion that cannot leave. The cell must now pump that proton back out at the cost of ATP, and the anion accumulates. The net effect is a collapsing pH gradient and an ATP drain that grows with titre. This single mechanism explains why organic acid fermentations have a titre ceiling, why they are run near neutral pH with base addition, and why that choice then creates a salt burden and an acidification step downstream. It is the clearest example in the course of a biological limit that is purely physical chemistry.

5. Osmolarity, water activity, and the other end of the titre problem

High product or salt concentrations lower water activity, and the cell responds by accumulating compatible solutes — glycine betaine, trehalose, proline — at metabolic cost. For a neutralised organic acid process the salt concentration at high titre can be the binding constraint rather than the acid itself. Rule of thumb: above roughly 1 osmol/L most industrial hosts are under measurable stress. This is why 'just raise the titre' has a physical ceiling independent of the pathway.

Worked anchor

Lactic acid, pKa 3.86. Compute the undissociated fraction at pH 6.0 and at pH 4.5.

f(HA) = 1/(1 + 10^(pH − pKa)). At pH 6.0: 1/(1 + 10^2.14) = 1/(1 + 138) = 0.0072, so 0.7%. At pH 4.5: 1/(1 + 10^0.64) = 1/(1 + 4.37) = 0.186, so 19%.

A 1.5 unit drop in pH raises the inhibitory species 26-fold at the same total titre. Now the second half: at equilibrium of HA across the membrane, with internal pH 7.2 and external 4.5, the anion accumulates internally by roughly 10^(7.2−4.5) = 500-fold relative to the external anion. That concentration is physically unreachable, so the cell never attains the equilibrium — it arrests or dies first. Which tells you the real design choice: ferment near neutral pH and pay for base and downstream acidification, or engineer tolerance and pay in strain development.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • pKₐ 3.86 at the relevant temperature and ionic strength
  • only the undissociated acid crosses the membrane
  • cytoplasmic pH held at 7.2 while external pH falls
  • activities approximated by concentrations

Common misconceptions

  • That the anion is the inhibitor. It is the undissociated acid that crosses the membrane; the anion is what gets trapped. Getting this backwards leads to the wrong engineering conclusion about pH.
  • That buffering capacity scales with concentration alone. It also depends on how close pH is to pKa — a 100 mM buffer two units from its pKa is nearly useless.
  • That the hydrophobic effect is an attraction between non-polar groups. It is the entropy of the water released when their surfaces are buried.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Derive Henderson-Hasselbalch from the dissociation equilibrium without looking.

A. Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. Take log₁₀ of both sides: log Ka = log[H⁺] + log([A⁻]/[HA]). Multiply by −1: pKa = pH − log([A⁻]/[HA]). Rearrange: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).

Q. At pH 5.0, what fraction of acetic acid (pKa 4.76) is undissociated, and what does that imply for an acetate process?

A. f(HA) = 1/(1 + 10^(5.0−4.76)) = 1/(1 + 10^0.24) = 1/(1 + 1.74) = 0.36, so 36% is the membrane-permeant, inhibitory species. At pH 7 the same calculation gives 0.6%. Acetate processes therefore cannot be run acidic: the strain is poisoned by its own product long before the stoichiometric ceiling, so you run near neutral and pay for base and for downstream acidification, or you engineer tolerance.

Q. Why is the free energy of protein folding only 20–60 kJ/mol, and what does that predict about point mutations?

A. It is the small difference between two large opposing terms — the favourable burial of hydrophobic surface and hydrogen bonding against the large conformational entropy lost on folding. Because the net is small, a single buried hydrophobic-to-polar substitution costing 5–15 kJ/mol removes a substantial fraction of the margin, and two or three such mutations unfold the protein. This predicts that random mutagenesis libraries lose function mainly by destabilisation rather than by active-site damage, which is why stability is engineered first.
