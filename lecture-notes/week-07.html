Week 7 of 55 · Molecular foundations · Core

Nucleic acids and the central dogma

Week content

The information layer, studied for what it does to your pathway rather than for its own sake. Four dials sit between a gene and a working enzyme — transcript level, translation initiation, elongation rate, and folding or degradation — and phase 6 turns all four. The week closes with Paper 1, so budget two sessions for consolidation and two for the examination.

Core ideas

1. Transcription in bacteria, in the detail you will actually use

RNA polymerase holoenzyme is core plus a sigma factor; the sigma determines promoter recognition, so swapping sigma factors switches whole regulons. A σ70 promoter has a −35 and a −10 hexamer with a spacer, and promoter strength is largely how well those elements match consensus — which is why promoter libraries are built by mutating them. Termination is either intrinsic, via a GC-rich hairpin followed by a U-tract, or Rho-dependent. What matters for engineering: promoter strength spans perhaps 1,000-fold across a good library; terminators are not perfect and read-through into downstream genes is a real and frequently unnoticed artefact; and transcription and translation are coupled in bacteria, so a stalled ribosome can terminate transcription prematurely.

2. Translation initiation is the usual bottleneck

The ribosome binds the Shine-Dalgarno sequence roughly 8–13 bases upstream of the start codon, and initiation rate depends on how accessible that region is — meaning the mRNA's secondary structure around the start matters as much as the SD sequence itself. This is why computational RBS design predicts expression better than promoter strength does, and why the first thing to vary in a tuning library is the 5′ untranslated region. A practical consequence worth internalising: the same RBS in front of a different gene gives a different expression level, because the folding of the start region depends on the downstream sequence. Context dependence is not noise; it is the mechanism.

3. Elongation, codon usage, and what rare codons actually do

Each codon is read by a tRNA whose abundance varies hugely. A rare codon stalls the ribosome, which slows elongation, causes queuing, raises the chance of premature termination and of co-translational misfolding, and can deplete the rare tRNA pool to the host's detriment. Codon adaptation indices quantify how well a gene matches the host's tRNA pool. Two cautions: codon optimisation is not a universal fix, because some slow codons are functional — rare-codon clusters can act as folding pauses — and optimisation algorithms can accidentally introduce strong secondary structure or internal SD-like sequences.

4. mRNA stability and why transcript level misleads

Bacterial transcript half-lives are typically 2–8 minutes, set by endonucleolytic cleavage and secondary structure. A measured steady-state transcript level is therefore a ratio of synthesis to degradation, and two genes with the same level can have very different synthesis rates. Combined with the independence of the four dials, this gives the week's key conclusion: transcript abundance is a weak predictor of flux. It is the reason week 39's omics integration is harder than it looks, and the reason that 'the gene is highly expressed' is never an adequate answer to 'why is flux low'.

5. Operons, and the structural difference that decides host

Bacteria transcribe polycistronic messages: one promoter, several genes, one transcript, with independent ribosome binding sites. This is extremely convenient for pathway engineering — one promoter controls a whole module, and relative expression is set by the individual RBSs. Eukaryotes do not do this in the same way: yeast needs a promoter and terminator per gene, or a 2A peptide or internal ribosome entry strategy. Add introns, capping, polyadenylation and nuclear export, and the result is that a yeast pathway construct is substantially larger and more complex than the equivalent in E. coli. That is a real cost in the host decision, set against yeast's compartments and P450 capability.

Worked anchor

A plant gene is expressed in E. coli from a strong promoter. Transcript is abundant; protein is not. Enumerate the explanations and order the experiments.

Six mechanistically distinct causes: rare codons stalling elongation; mRNA secondary structure occluding the RBS so initiation fails despite high transcript; rapid transcript turnover with high synthesis masking a short half-life; misfolding and aggregation into inclusion bodies; proteolytic degradation; and a toxic product selecting for non-expressing escape mutants that now dominate the culture.

Order by information gained per day, not by elegance. First, fractionate soluble from insoluble and run a gel — one day, no new construct, and it splits the hypothesis space roughly in half between 'protein made but insoluble' and 'protein not made'. If insoluble: lower temperature, weaken expression, try chaperones. If not made: vary the 5′ UTR and test codon-optimised synthesis. Only then consider degradation, with a protease-deficient strain, and escape, with flow cytometry on the population.

That ordering principle is the answer interviewers want, more than the list itself.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the promoter performs as specified and the transcript measurement is sound
  • the six causes are not mutually exclusive and may act together
  • ranking is by information per day for this host, not in general

Common misconceptions

  • That a strong promoter guarantees expression. Initiation of translation, not transcription, is usually what limits a heterologous protein.
  • That codon optimisation always helps. It can remove functional pause sites and can introduce secondary structure; it is a hypothesis to test, not a fix to apply.
  • That an abundant transcript means an abundant protein. Four independent steps and a degradation rate sit in between, which is why flux and transcript correlate so weakly.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Where between gene and active enzyme is the rate most often limited, and what is the evidence for your answer?

A. Translation initiation. The evidence: ribosome profiling shows initiation, not elongation, as the step with the widest dynamic range between genes; RBS and 5′ UTR variation produces larger expression changes than promoter variation across comparable libraries; and computational models of initiation predict protein level better than transcript level does. The caveat worth stating is that this is the modal answer rather than the universal one — for a codon-pathological heterologous gene, elongation can dominate, and for an aggregation-prone product the limit is after translation entirely.

Q. Why does the same RBS give different expression in front of different genes?

A. Because initiation rate depends on the accessibility of the ribosome binding site and start codon, and accessibility depends on the secondary structure of the whole 5′ region — which includes the first tens of bases of the coding sequence. A downstream sequence that pairs with the SD region occludes it. This is why RBS strength is context-dependent rather than an intrinsic property, why RBS calculators take the coding sequence as input, and why silent substitutions in the first few codons are a standard tuning lever.

Q. You have one day to diagnose high transcript and low activity. What do you do, and what does each outcome rule out?

A. Fractionate soluble from insoluble protein and run a gel, because it costs a day, needs no new construct, and splits the hypothesis space roughly in half. Insoluble protein present rules out transcription, translation and degradation, pointing at expression rate, temperature or chaperones. No protein in either fraction rules out aggregation, pointing at translation initiation, codon usage or proteolysis. Abundant soluble protein rules out the whole expression axis and points downstream — cofactor, substrate supply, thermodynamic infeasibility or inhibition — each of which has its own next experiment.
