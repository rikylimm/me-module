13C metabolic flux analysis I: principles and atom mapping

Week content

Flux balance analysis tells you what a network could do; labelling tells you what it did. This week is the forward half — how carbon moves through central metabolism and how that produces a pattern a mass spectrometer sees. Build the simulator and verify it by hand, because week 29 is this model run backwards and a wrong atom map inverts to a precise, confident, wrong flux map.

Core ideas

1. What extracellular rates cannot see, stated formally

Measure every uptake and excretion rate perfectly and you have fixed S_ext·v, not v. Anything in the null space of the external stoichiometry is invisible: it can take any value with every measurable rate unchanged. Three families live there. Cycles returning carbon to their origin, of which the pentose phosphate pathway is the standard case — oxidative-branch carbon comes back as fructose-6-phosphate and triose phosphate, so the only distinct external product is CO₂, which has other sources. Bidirectional exchange, where the net flux is observable and the two magnitudes are not. And parallel routes of identical overall stoichiometry, such as Embden-Meyerhof against Entner-Doudoroff. Labelling resolves all three, because these routes rearrange skeletons differently even when they balance identically.

2. Atom mapping, and the transitions worth memorising

An atom transition network states which substrate carbon becomes which product carbon, for every reaction. Aldolase splits fructose-1,6-bisphosphate so C1–C3 become DHAP and C4–C6 become GAP, and triose phosphate isomerase maps DHAP C1 onto GAP C3 — so glucose C1 and C6 arrive at the same carbon and both become the methyl of pyruvate. 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase releases glucose C1 specifically as CO₂, which is the whole basis of the PPP measurement. Transketolase moves two-carbon units and transaldolase three. And fumarate and succinate are symmetric, so label entering one end is scrambled to both — the classic trap, and why a map read off a pathway chart is usually wrong somewhere in the TCA cycle.

3. Isotopomers, cumomers and EMUs: three representations of one thing

A metabolite of n carbons has 2ⁿ isotopomers, so 64 for glucose, and a full isotopomer balance over central metabolism is thousands of nonlinear equations. Cumomers are a change of variables — sums over isotopomers sharing a labelled subset — which makes the system block-triangular and solvable by cascade rather than iteration. Elementary metabolite units go further: starting from what the instrument measures and working backwards, only certain carbon subsets are ever needed, typically reducing the problem by an order of magnitude without approximation. A teaching network can afford the full balance and should use it; the reduction is a computational convenience, not a concept, and at genome scale it is what makes the method run.

4. What the instrument reports is a mass distribution vector

For a fragment of n carbons you get fractional abundances m+0 through m+n, summing to one. That is not positional information — the MDV has already summed over which carbons carry label. Position comes from measuring several overlapping fragments of the same metabolite, since GC-MS fragmentation of a derivatised amino acid gives known partial skeletons and the differences between their MDVs localise the label. So a well-chosen second fragment is worth more than a more sensitive instrument on the first. Proteinogenic amino acids are the usual target: abundant, stable, and each reporting a precursor — alanine for pyruvate, aspartate for oxaloacetate, glutamate for 2-oxoglutarate.

5. Natural abundance correction is larger than you expect

About 1.1% of carbon is ¹³C, so an unlabelled twelve-carbon fragment shows roughly 12% at m+1 before any tracer. In practice the derivative is tert-butyldimethylsilyl (TBDMS), and the two fragments ¹³C-MFA papers quote are M−57, from loss of the tert-butyl group, which retains the metabolite's full carbon skeleton, and M−159; which carbons each fragment keeps has to be taken from the derivatisation chemistry for that metabolite rather than assumed, and that mapping is not verified here. Derivatisation compounds it: a trimethylsilyl group adds three carbons and a silicon, and silicon is 4.7% ²⁹Si and 3.1% ³⁰Si, so a derivatised fragment can carry a natural m+1 above twenty per cent. The correction is linear — build the matrix from the elemental composition of the actual fragment including the derivative and apply its inverse — so you must know exactly which atoms the fragment contains. Run an unlabelled culture through the identical workup: if the correction is right, its corrected MDV is m+0 = 1 within noise.

6. Tracer choice, and why mixtures are standard

1-¹³C glucose is the classic probe of the pentose phosphate split, because the oxidative branch releases exactly that carbon as CO₂ while glycolysis retains it. U-¹³C glucose labels everything, so the signal is in which fragments stay intact and which are cleaved and recombined — the better probe of anaplerosis and the pyruvate node. 1,2-¹³C₂ glucose separates Entner-Doudoroff cleanly. Because the information is complementary, mixtures are the usual general-purpose choice and the ratio is itself a design variable for week 29 — one published recipe is 80% 1-¹³C with 20% U-¹³C, an example rather than a default, since the best mixture depends on the target flux, the network, the organism, the fragments you can measure and the cost. Select in silico against the flux you care about, not by copying the last paper in your organism.

Worked anchor

Resolving the pentose phosphate split with 1-¹³C glucose, by hand.

Let α be the fraction of glucose-6-phosphate entering the oxidative branch.

Through glycolysis: aldolase puts glucose C1–C3 into DHAP and C4–C6 into GAP, and triose phosphate isomerase maps DHAP C1 onto GAP C3, so the label becomes the methyl carbon of one pyruvate. One glucose gives two pyruvates, exactly one of which is m+1, so this route yields a pyruvate m+1 fraction of 0.5.

Through the oxidative PPP: 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase releases C1 as CO₂, so the ribulose-5-phosphate is unlabelled and the carbon returning through transketolase and transaldolase carries no label either. This route contributes 0.

First order, and wrong by enough to matter: pyruvate m+1 ≈ 0.5(1 − α) assumes both routes deliver the same number of trioses, and they do not. Three G6P through the oxidative branch give 3 CO₂ + 2 F6P + 1 GAP — five trioses' worth of carbon, not six — so a glucose entering the PPP delivers 5/3 pyruvate against glycolysis's 2. Labelled pyruvate per glucose is (1 − α); total pyruvate is 2(1 − α) + (5/3)α = 2 − α/3. The relation to use is therefore m+1 = (1 − α)/(2 − α/3). Measure alanine, correct for natural abundance, and a corrected m+1 of 0.35 gives α = 0.34, where the first-order form returns 0.30 — a 13% error in the answer, which is larger than the measurement error this week's own error budget allows. Keep the first-order form only to show what neglecting the triose stoichiometry costs. Published splits for glucose-limited aerobic E. coli are of this order, but cite a primary source with its growth conditions rather than this calculation if the number is going anywhere.

Three things separate this from a publishable number. Recycling: returned carbon can go round again, so the relation is not exactly linear and the forward model must solve the cycle self-consistently. Pool mixing: malic enzyme and PEP carboxykinase return TCA carbon to pyruvate with a different pattern, from an oxaloacetate that has passed through symmetric fumarate. And the natural abundance correction is doing real work — an uncorrected 0.35 might be 0.30 corrected, which in the corrected relation gives α = 0.44 rather than 0.34.

So verify the simulator against this with recycling off, then switch it on and watch the discrepancy appear. If it appears in the wrong direction, the atom map is wrong and you have found it cheaply.

The negative half is the part to write down: no rate measurement can do this, because the branch's only distinct external product is CO₂, which pyruvate dehydrogenase and the TCA cycle also produce. It is the clearest demonstration of why labelling exists, and Brief 9's opening requirement.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • metabolic and isotopic steady state
  • no recycling of PPP carbon in the first-order version
  • no anaplerotic return of label to pyruvate
  • the atom map is exact and the MDVs are corrected for natural abundance
  • equal analytical response across isotopologues
  • alanine reports the pyruvate pool faithfully

Common misconceptions

  • That labelling measures flux. It measures labelling patterns; flux comes from fitting a model to them, so fluxes are only as good as the atom map — which is what week 29's chi-squared test is for.
  • That an atom map can be read off a pathway chart. Symmetric intermediates break it, and a map that misses them fits badly for reasons that look like noise.
  • That natural abundance correction is cosmetic. A silylated fragment can show over twenty per cent at m+1 with no tracer present, larger than most signals being interpreted.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Prove that extracellular rates cannot determine the oxidative PPP flux, then explain how 1-¹³C glucose resolves it.

A. Carbon entering the oxidative branch returns to glycolysis as fructose-6-phosphate and triose phosphate, so the branch has no distinct external stoichiometry except CO₂, which pyruvate dehydrogenase, the TCA cycle and biosynthetic decarboxylations also produce. The cycle flux therefore lies in the null space of the external stoichiometry and can take any value with every measurable rate unchanged, so it is unidentifiable from rates however precisely measured. 1-¹³C glucose resolves it because 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase releases precisely glucose C1 as CO₂ while glycolysis retains it and delivers it to the methyl carbon of pyruvate: to first order, pyruvate m+1 ≈ 0.5(1 − α).

Q. Why is a well-chosen second fragment worth more than better precision on the first?

A. Because the MDV has already summed over position. One fragment says how many carbons are labelled, not which, so the positional information that distinguishes flux splits comes from differences between overlapping fragments. A fragment covering a different subset of the skeleton adds independent constraints; more precision on an existing fragment only tightens constraints you already have, and in a structurally limited fit it tightens them around the wrong answer. The same logic makes a parallel labelling experiment with a second tracer the strongest available validation.

Q. You inherit a colleague's atom transition network. What do you check first, and how?

A. The symmetric molecules, because that is where maps are usually wrong: fumarate and succinate must scramble label between their halves and an inherited map often treats them as oriented. Then aldolase and triose phosphate isomerase, since mapping glucose C1 and C6 onto the same triose carbon carries most of the information in the data. Then the decarboxylations: glucose C1 at 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, pyruvate C1 at pyruvate dehydrogenase. The test is mechanical — simulate a known flux vector with a single-position tracer and check two metabolites by hand. Map errors give qualitatively impossible patterns rather than slightly wrong ones, so they are cheap to find.
