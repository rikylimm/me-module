The four subspaces of S

Week content

Four subspaces, each with a biological meaning. This week converts linear algebra you already have into intuition about networks, and it answers a question you will be asked in interview: how many measurements does a flux study actually need? Keep the toy networks small enough to read by eye; the point is interpretation, not computation.

Core ideas

1. The four subspaces and their dimensions

For S with m metabolites, n reactions and rank r: the column space has dimension r and lives in metabolite space; the right null space has dimension n − r and lives in flux space; the row space has dimension r in flux space; and the left null space has dimension m − r in metabolite space. The rank-nullity theorem gives these for free, and the two null spaces are where the biology is. Everything else in constraint-based modelling is a statement about restricting the right null space.

2. The right null space is the space of steady states

S·v = 0 defines the set of flux vectors consistent with mass balance, and that set is exactly the right null space. Its dimension n − r is the number of degrees of freedom: how many fluxes must be fixed, by measurement or by bounds or by an objective, before a unique solution exists. For the E. coli core model with 95 reactions and rank near 68, that is 27 degrees of freedom; at genome scale it is several hundred. This single number explains why flux balance analysis needs an objective function at all — mass balance alone leaves far too much freedom.

3. Basis vectors are pathways, with a caveat

Any basis of the null space spans the same space, and a numerically convenient basis from a singular value decomposition is not biologically interpretable. Bases chosen for interpretability — elementary flux modes, extreme pathways — consist of vectors that are themselves minimal functional routes through the network, which is why they read as pathways. The caveat is combinatorial explosion: the number of elementary modes grows faster than exponentially with network size, so they are computable for small networks and hopeless at genome scale. That limitation is why the field moved to optimisation rather than enumeration.

4. The left null space holds conserved moieties

A vector in the left null space is a linear combination of metabolite concentrations that the network cannot change: total adenylate (ATP + ADP + AMP), total nicotinamide in each pool, total coenzyme A, total quinone. These are conserved because no reaction in the network creates or destroys the moiety; it is only passed between carriers. Two practical uses. In a kinetic model these totals must be supplied as parameters, because the differential equations cannot determine them. And an unexpected extra conserved pool is a structural diagnostic — it usually means a missing exchange reaction or an unintended dead end, so checking the left null space is the fastest sanity check on any new reconstruction.

5. Singular value decomposition, used for structure rather than numbers

Writing S = UΣVᵀ gives orthonormal bases for all four subspaces at once, with the singular values ranking how strongly each mode participates. The spectrum is informative: a steep drop means a few modes dominate the network's structure. The left singular vectors group metabolites that behave together, the right singular vectors group reactions. None of this is directly biological — the vectors are orthogonal by construction, not by biology — so treat SVD as a structural probe and not as a pathway-finding method.

6. What the subspaces do not tell you

Worth stating plainly, because the formalism is seductive. The null space contains every mass-balanced flux distribution, including ones that are thermodynamically impossible, kinetically unreachable, or that require more enzyme than the cell possesses. Restricting it is the whole business of the next four weeks: bounds for capacity, directionality and loop constraints for thermodynamics, proteome constraints for enzyme cost, and an objective for the final selection. The subspaces define what is conceivable; everything else defines what is possible.

Worked anchor

Compute and read the subspaces of the toy network from week 20.

Four metabolites, six reactions, rank 4. So: column space 4, right null space 2, row space 4, left null space 0.

The right null space of dimension 2 means two independent routes, which on inspection are A→C and A→D. Fix one flux — say measure the C exchange — and with mass balance the other is determined.

The left null space is empty, which makes sense: every metabolite can enter or leave through an exchange, so nothing is trapped. Now delete the D exchange and recompute. D can now only be produced and never consumed, so it is a dead end; reaction 3 is forced to zero, the effective rank falls, and the left null space acquires a dimension corresponding to D being conserved at whatever it started with.

That is the diagnostic in miniature. When you compute the left null space of a real reconstruction and find a conserved pool you cannot name, you have almost certainly found a missing exchange rather than a new piece of biology.

Common misconceptions

  • That a null space basis is the set of pathways. Any basis spans the space; only particular bases, chosen for the purpose, consist of interpretable routes.
  • That elementary modes scale. They explode combinatorially, which is precisely why the field optimises rather than enumerates at genome scale.
  • That the null space contains achievable states. It contains mass-balanced ones, including thermodynamically impossible and enzymatically unaffordable ones.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. A network has 112 reactions, 84 metabolites and rank 79. Give all four subspace dimensions and interpret two.

A. Column space 79, right null space 112 − 79 = 33, row space 79, left null space 84 − 79 = 5. The right null space of 33 means 33 independent fluxes must be fixed by measurement, bounds or an objective before a unique solution exists — which is why an objective function is needed at all. The left null space of 5 means five conserved moieties, typically total adenylate, the two nicotinamide pools, coenzyme A and quinone, which must be supplied as parameters in any kinetic model.

Q. You compute the left null space of a new reconstruction and find a conserved pool you cannot name. What is the likely cause?

A. A missing exchange reaction or an unintended dead end: some metabolite or moiety can neither enter nor leave the system, so its total is trapped and appears as a conservation relation. Occasionally it is real biology — a genuine carrier you had not thought of — but the first hypothesis should be a boundary error. Checking the left null space is therefore the fastest structural sanity check available on a new model.

Q. Why did the field move from enumerating elementary modes to optimisation?

A. Because the number of elementary flux modes grows faster than exponentially with network size, so enumeration is tractable for networks of tens of reactions and impossible for thousands. Optimisation finds one point in the null space satisfying the constraints rather than describing the whole space, which scales to genome-scale models. The cost is that you learn about one flux distribution rather than the structure of all of them, which is why flux variability analysis and sampling exist as partial substitutes.
