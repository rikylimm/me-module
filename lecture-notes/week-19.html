Genome annotation and what it is worth

Week content

A reconstruction is only as good as the annotation beneath it, and annotation is mostly inference rather than measurement. This week you learn how a genome becomes a gene list, how often that list is wrong, and how to judge the evidence behind any functional assignment you later rely on. It is the least glamorous week in the phase and it is what stops you trusting a model more than it deserves.

Core ideas

1. How a genome becomes a gene list, and where inference enters

Sequencing gives contigs; gene calling finds open reading frames by codon statistics and ribosome binding site context; then function is assigned, almost always by homology to something already annotated. That last step is where the uncertainty lives. A BLAST hit at 70% identity over most of the length is strong evidence of a shared fold and reasonable evidence of a shared reaction class; it is weak evidence of identical substrate specificity, and essentially no evidence about which cofactor the enzyme prefers. Orthologues, which arose by speciation, usually keep function; paralogues, which arose by duplication, frequently diverge — so annotation transferred along a paralogue is the single commonest source of a reaction in a model that the organism cannot actually perform.

2. Evidence codes, which you should read before any annotation

Databases record how a function was assigned, and the classes differ enormously in strength: experimental characterisation in this organism; experimental in a related organism; inferred from sequence similarity; inferred from genomic context such as operon membership or gene neighbourhood; and automatic electronic annotation with no curator involvement. In a typical bacterial genome, somewhere between a tenth and a third of metabolic genes have experimental evidence in that organism and the rest are inferred. Before you build an argument on a reaction, look at which class it sits in. A model prediction that depends on an electronically annotated transporter is a hypothesis about a transporter, not a result.

3. Orphan activities and hypothetical proteins: the two-sided gap

Two complementary problems. Orphan enzyme activities are reactions known biochemically to occur in an organism with no gene assigned — perhaps a fifth of EC numbers with characterised activity have no sequence attached anywhere. Hypothetical proteins are the reverse: genes with no assigned function, typically a quarter to a third of any genome. The two gaps are not independent, since many orphan activities are presumably carried out by some of those hypothetical proteins. For modelling, orphans are the bigger nuisance: the reaction must be in the network for the organism to work, but it has no gene, so it cannot be deleted in silico and will never appear in an essentiality prediction.

4. Why strains drift, and what that costs an engineer

Mutation rates are roughly 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁹ per base per generation, so a culture reaching 10¹² cells contains, for every base in the genome, many independent mutants. Any variant that grows even slightly faster is enriched, and in a producing strain the fastest-growing variant is usually the one that has lost or silenced the pathway. Add insertion sequences, which inactivate constructs at appreciable frequency, and recombination between repeated sequences, and you have the mechanism behind a strain that performs at 2 L and fails at 1 m³. This is genetics appearing as a process problem, and the defences are genetic: integrate rather than carry a plasmid, minimise burden, and couple production to growth.

5. Pangenomes and why 'the organism' is not one genome

Sequence many isolates of a species and the gene content varies substantially: a core genome present in all, and an accessory genome that differs between strains. For E. coli the core is a few thousand genes while the pangenome runs to tens of thousands. The practical consequence is that a reconstruction is of a strain, not a species, and applying an iJO1366-style model built on K-12 MG1655 to a different isolate imports assumptions that may be wrong about transporters, sugar utilisation and stress responses. Always check which strain a published model was built from and whether yours is the same.

6. The databases, and what each is for

NCBI for sequence and the submitted annotation. UniProt for protein-level information with evidence codes, which is the one to check when you want to know how a function was assigned. KEGG for pathway topology and orthology groups, useful for checking whether a route exists in your organism. MetaCyc and EcoCyc for curated, literature-backed reactions, with EcoCyc being unusually thorough for E. coli specifically. BiGG for models themselves. Learn to query each from a script once; by week 23 you will want to pull annotations and reactions programmatically rather than by hand.

Worked anchor

Judge an annotation before you build on it.

Suppose your pathway needs a dehydratase, and the organism's genome lists one. Work through the chain:

1. What is the evidence class? Open the UniProt entry. If it says 'inferred from homology' with no publication, you have a sequence similarity and nothing more. 2. To what, and how similar? A 45% identity hit over 60% of the length to a characterised enzyme of the same class tells you the fold is probably right and the specificity is unknown. 3. Orthologue or paralogue? Check whether the hit's organism has a single copy or a family. A family means divergence is likely and the specific member matters. 4. Is there genomic context? If the gene sits in an operon with other genes of the same pathway, that is independent evidence — weak individually, strong in combination with homology. 5. What does the model prediction depend on? If your conclusion changes when this reaction is removed, the annotation has become an experimental priority rather than a modelling detail.

The discipline is the point. Most published modelling work does not do this, and the difference between a prediction you can defend and one you cannot is usually whether you did.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the databases are current as of reading, with evidence codes recorded honestly
  • sequence identity is computed over a meaningful alignment length

Common misconceptions

  • That an annotated genome is a list of known functions. Most assignments are inferred from homology, and the evidence class is recorded — look at it.
  • That a model of a species applies to your strain. Reconstructions are strain-specific, and accessory gene content differs substantially within a species.
  • That scale-up failure is a process problem. A good part of it is genetics: more generations means more opportunity for a faster-growing non-producer to sweep.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Name five ways a functional annotation can be wrong, and the model error each produces.

A. Wrong EC assignment by homology to a paralogue, producing a reaction the organism cannot perform; missing annotation for a promiscuous side activity, producing a false essentiality call; wrong substrate specificity, producing the wrong product or cofactor; wrong subcellular localisation in a compartmentalised organism, producing a transport requirement that does not exist; and a transporter inferred generically for every extracellular metabolite, producing growth on substrates the organism cannot use. Each has a specific curation remedy rather than being a defect of the method.

Q. Why is an orphan reaction a particular nuisance for a deletion screen?

A. Because it must be present in the network for the organism's metabolism to balance, but it has no gene associated with it. In silico it therefore cannot be deleted, so it never appears as essential or non-essential, and any gene whose only route to a function runs through that orphan will be mispredicted. The honest treatment is to flag orphan reactions in the model and to report how many predictions depend on them.

Q. A producing strain gives 8 g/L at 2 L and 3 g/L at 1,000 L from the same protocol. What genetic explanation would you test first, and how?

A. Genetic instability. Scaling adds roughly ten doublings of inoculum build-up, so a variant that has lost or silenced the pathway and grows even a few per cent faster can become a large fraction of the population by the time the production vessel fills. Test by plating the final broth and screening colonies for production, checking plasmid retention without selection, and sequencing the construct from the end of the run rather than from the seed. If confirmed, the remedies are chromosomal integration, lower burden, or growth coupling.

Apply it to your fermentation experience

Transfer prompt. If you used a genome sequence or annotation for one organism, identify a pathway claim that annotation supports and one it cannot establish without experimental evidence.

Use your actual thesis methods and results where you remember them. Mark missing information as unknown; do not assume the organisms were co-cultured, that a metabolite was measured, or that a proposed mechanism was demonstrated.
