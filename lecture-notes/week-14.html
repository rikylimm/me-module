Biosynthesis and the precursor tree

Week content

Biosynthesis is where industrial metabolic engineering began and where most of its money still is. It is also the source of every coefficient in a biomass equation, so this week does double duty: it teaches the pathways you may engineer, and it generates the numbers week 16 needs. Treat it as a costing exercise — each amino acid has a price in precursors, NADPH and ATP, and that price is what decides a theoretical yield.

Core ideas

1. Nitrogen enters by two routes with different prices

Glutamate dehydrogenase aminates 2-oxoglutarate directly using NADPH, costs no ATP, and has a high Km for ammonium — so it works only when nitrogen is plentiful. The glutamine synthetase–glutamate synthase couple (GS-GOGAT) first makes glutamine at the cost of one ATP, then transfers the amide nitrogen to 2-oxoglutarate, consuming NADPH. The net is one extra ATP per ammonium assimilated, bought in exchange for a far lower Km. That is a clean example of a pattern you will meet repeatedly: the cell pays energy for affinity. Once nitrogen is in glutamate and glutamine, transaminases distribute it, which is why these two metabolites are the nitrogen hub and why glutamate is the most abundant intracellular metabolite in most bacteria at 50–100 mM.

2. The amino acid families, organised by precursor

Learn them as a tree rooted in the twelve precursors, not as twenty separate pathways. Glutamate family from 2-oxoglutarate: glutamate, glutamine, proline, arginine. Aspartate family from oxaloacetate: aspartate, asparagine, methionine, threonine, lysine, isoleucine — the industrially dominant family. Pyruvate family: alanine, valine, leucine. Serine family from 3-phosphoglycerate: serine, glycine, cysteine. Aromatic family from erythrose-4-phosphate plus PEP: phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan. Histidine, alone, from PRPP and ATP. Two engineering consequences follow directly. The aspartate family shares a committed step, aspartokinase, so a lysine strain and a threonine strain compete at the same branch point. And the aromatic family's dependence on E4P plus PEP explains why those strains always involve transketolase and PTS interventions, as week 10 established.

3. Branch-point regulation and what a feedback-resistant design must achieve

Three architectures solve the problem of regulating a branched pathway from a shared step. Isozymes: several copies of the first enzyme, each inhibited by a different end product — E. coli's three aspartokinases are the textbook case, inhibited respectively by lysine, threonine and methionine-related signals. Concerted inhibition: the first step is inhibited only when all end products are present together. Cumulative inhibition: each product contributes partial inhibition and the effects compound. Which architecture is present determines the intervention. Under isozymes you must address every copy that carries flux, which is precisely why industrial lysine strains took several strain generations rather than one deletion. Under cumulative inhibition deletion helps nothing and you must engineer the allosteric site itself. And note from week 6 that the most useful mutations often abolish cooperativity rather than affinity, because the sigmoid is what makes the inhibition sharp.

4. Nucleotides: expensive, regulated, and salvaged

Purines are built onto PRPP stepwise, consuming glycine, glutamine, aspartate, formate from tetrahydrofolate and CO₂, at a cost of several ATP; pyrimidines build the ring first and attach PRPP afterwards. Both routes are expensive — on the order of 5–10 ATP-equivalents per nucleotide including the amino acid precursors — which is why salvage pathways exist and why they matter industrially: a fast-growing production strain spends a substantial share of its biosynthetic budget on RNA, and RNA content rises with growth rate (the week 1 growth laws again). The regulatory point worth carrying: PRPP sits at the junction of purine, pyrimidine, histidine and tryptophan synthesis, so its pool is contested and its availability constrains several product classes at once.

5. Costing an amino acid, which is the week's transferable skill

The procedure: identify the precursors and their count, the NADPH consumed, the ATP consumed, the carbons lost as CO₂, and any nitrogen assimilation cost. Then convert to a theoretical yield on glucose. For lysine: oxaloacetate plus pyruvate, 4 NADPH, 1 ATP, with one CO₂ fixed at the anaplerotic step. Published ceilings cluster near 1.0 mol lysine per mol glucose when anaplerosis carboxylates and NADPH comes from the pentose phosphate pathway, rising towards 1.3 if a non-decarboxylating NADPH route is assumed — so state your assumptions, because the number is assumption-dependent and an interviewer is testing whether you know that. Best published strains reach about 0.55 g/g, roughly two thirds of theoretical, with the gap going to biomass, maintenance, by-products and the carbon lost supplying NADPH.

6. Where sulfur and one-carbon units come from, briefly

Sulfate assimilation is costly: reduction to sulfide consumes ATP for activation and several NADPH, which is why cysteine and methionine are among the most expensive amino acids and why methionine remains a hard industrial target. One-carbon units ride on tetrahydrofolate at several oxidation levels and feed purine synthesis, thymidylate, and methionine's methyl group via S-adenosylmethionine. You need this only at the level of knowing that these are expensive and regulated — but if your product is a methylated natural product, SAM supply becomes a first-order design constraint and you should recognise it immediately.

Worked anchor

Cost phenylalanine from glucose, and see where the constraint sits.

Precursors: erythrose-4-phosphate (C4) plus two phosphoenolpyruvate (C3 each) enter the shikimate pathway, giving chorismate, then prephenate, then phenylpyruvate, then transamination to phenylalanine (C9).

Carbon: 4 + 3 + 3 = 10 carbons in, 9 in the product, so one CO₂ released at the prephenate dehydratase step. Carbon efficiency of the route itself is 90%.

Now trace back to glucose. E4P comes from the non-oxidative pentose phosphate branch; PEP comes from glycolysis. If uptake is by the PTS, one PEP per glucose is consumed just to bring the sugar in — so the two PEP the pathway needs come at the expense of PEP that would otherwise be available, and the theoretical yield is roughly halved relative to a non-PTS strain. Published maxima are near 0.3 g/g with PTS and near 0.5 g/g with a permease-plus-glucokinase replacement, the difference being almost entirely this PEP accounting.

The lesson is the one week 15 develops: for a PEP-derived product, the transporter is a stoichiometric decision, not a logistics detail. Before designing anything in the shikimate pathway, do this arithmetic — it tells you that the first intervention is at the membrane and the second is at transketolase, and only the third is in the pathway itself.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the shikimate route as drawn, one CO₂ at prephenate dehydratase
  • uptake by the PTS, so one PEP is spent on transport
  • no competing drain on E4P or PEP

Common misconceptions

  • That deleting a feedback-inhibited enzyme relieves the inhibition. It removes the enzyme; you need an allele that still catalyses but no longer binds the effector, which is a point mutation in the allosteric site rather than a knockout.
  • That all amino acids cost about the same. Cysteine, methionine, tryptophan and histidine are several times more expensive per mole than alanine or glycine, which matters when a product or a medium is dominated by one of them.
  • That NADPH demand for an amino acid is a fixed number. It depends on which route the organism uses and on whether the dehydrogenases are NAD or NADP dependent, which differs between hosts.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Compare GDH and GS-GOGAT for ammonium assimilation, and say when each is used.

A. GDH aminates 2-oxoglutarate with NADPH, costing no ATP, but has a high Km for ammonium, so it operates when nitrogen is abundant. GS-GOGAT costs one additional ATP per ammonium — glutamine synthetase consumes it — but has a much lower Km, so it operates under nitrogen limitation. The cell therefore pays one ATP for affinity, which is a recurring pattern. In a defined medium with excess ammonium, expect GDH to dominate; in a fed-batch running nitrogen-limited, expect the GS-GOGAT route and the associated extra ATP demand to appear in your energy balance.

Q. Name the precursors of the aromatic amino acids and state the two interventions this implies before any shikimate pathway change.

A. Erythrose-4-phosphate and phosphoenolpyruvate. First, raise E4P supply by overexpressing transketolase, and possibly transaldolase, since the non-oxidative pentose phosphate branch is the only source and it usually limits before the shikimate enzymes do. Second, raise PEP availability by replacing PTS uptake with a permease plus glucokinase, or by attenuating pyruvate kinase, since PTS consumes one PEP per glucose imported. Only after those does amplifying the shikimate pathway itself pay.

Q. Best lysine strains reach about 0.55 g/g against a theoretical near 0.81 g/g. Where does the missing third go?

A. Biomass, which must be made and carries carbon away from product; maintenance, which consumes substrate at no product yield and dominates at low growth rate; by-products such as organic acids and trehalose; and carbon lost as CO₂ supplying the 4 NADPH per lysine through the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway — roughly two carbons per lysine if that is the sole NADPH route. The last item is why later strain generations targeted NADPH supply and anaplerosis rather than the lysine pathway itself.
