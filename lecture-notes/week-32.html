Week 32 of 55 · The molecular toolbox · Core

DNA manipulation: PCR, cloning and assembly

Week content

The build step, and the only week whose deliverable is a protocol rather than a model. The content is not the enzymology of any one method but the arithmetic that chooses between them — error rate against construct length, overlap design against repeat content, hands-on days against calendar days against money — and the verification cascade, which is best written as what each check excludes and what remains possible afterwards.

Core ideas

1. Polymerase fidelity sets how many colonies you sequence

Errors accumulate per base per duplication, so expected substitutions in a finished amplicon are roughly e·L·d, with d about 20 effective duplications for a 25–30 cycle reaction from nanograms. Reported fidelities run from about 10⁻⁵ errors per base per duplication for Taq to about 10⁻⁷ for proofreading enzymes; take the supplier's figure, not a remembered one. For a 3 kb insert at 10⁻⁷, e·L·d = 0.006 and 99% of clones are clean. At 10⁻⁵ the same build expects 0.6 errors, so only e⁻⁰·⁶ = 55% are clean. The clean fraction falls exponentially with length.

2. Primer design is three constraints, not one

Annealing temperature, specificity and secondary structure. Use nearest-neighbour Tm above about 14 nt; the 2(A+T)+4(G+C) rule drifts badly past 20 nt and ignores salt. Anneal a few degrees below the lower Tm and keep the pair within about 2 °C, since the hotter primer otherwise dominates the first cycles. Specificity lives at the 3′ end: a mismatched 5′ tail still extends, which is how overhangs are added, while a 3′ mismatch mostly does not. Hairpins and dimers compete with template for primer, and a 3′-end dimer is amplified preferentially because it is its own substrate.

3. Restriction-ligation fails on uniqueness, not chemistry

The method needs sites that cut at the junctions and nowhere else, so its limit is the chance of an internal site. For a 6-cutter at roughly uniform base composition the expected count in a 1 kb fragment is 2 × 1000/4096 = 0.49 across both strands, so about 39% of kilobase fragments carry one; across five fragments all are clean with probability 0.61⁵ = 8%. That, plus the surviving scar in the junction — translated if it lands in frame — is why multi-fragment work moved on.

4. Gibson trades uniqueness for overlap, and repeats punish it

Chew-back, anneal, fill and ligate in one isothermal reaction, with 15–40 bp of designed overlap and no site requirement at all. The failure mode moves to sequence similarity: two junctions sharing homology anneal to each other, so a construct using one terminator twice assembles in the wrong order at a rate set by that similarity. The design rules follow — every overlap unique, junctions placed away from repeated parts, and a synonymous or alternative version used at one copy where a part must repeat. Efficiency falls with fragment count, so five-way assemblies are routine and ten-way are a project.

5. Golden Gate cuts outside its own site, which is what makes it scarless and one-pot

Type IIS enzymes such as BsaI recognise an asymmetric site and cut a fixed distance away, so the four-base overhang is yours to choose and the recognition site is destroyed by the cut. Digestion and ligation run together: correct joins are stable, re-ligated parents are re-cut, and the reaction is driven towards the assembly. The cost is domestication — internal sites removed by one synonymous codon each, needed for a kilobase coding sequence about 39% of the time by the arithmetic above. Overhangs are picked from characterised high-fidelity sets rather than invented, because some combinations mis-ligate.

6. Synthesis and yeast assembly are the two ends of the same spectrum

Ordering a domesticated, codon-adjusted, verified fragment removes the cloning and adds lead time: the trade is calendar against hands-on time more than money. Yeast homologous recombination takes many fragments at once, on overlaps of tens to hundreds of base pairs and the host's own machinery, which keeps it the method for very large or repetitive assemblies even when the eventual host is bacterial.

7. Every verification step is blind to something

Colony PCR says a junction of roughly the right size exists; it cannot see point mutations and reports happily on a construct carrying a second, wrong insert. A diagnostic digest gives size and orientation and catches gross rearrangement, nothing below its resolution. Sanger reads the insert for about 700–900 informative bases per primer and says nothing about the backbone — where a Gibson misassembly or an origin mutation hides. Whole-plasmid sequencing catches all of it and is now the cheapest of the four per construct actually verified.

Worked anchor

Cost a five-gene build, in hands-on days, calendar days and money. Five coding sequences averaging 1.2 kb into a 4 kb backbone, 10 kb final.

Restriction-ligation is excluded on arithmetic: all five fragments are clean of a given 6-cutter with probability 0.61⁵ = 0.08, and five compatible unique sites would be needed in the backbone besides.

Golden Gate. Domestication of about two of the five genes at a 39% chance each, one primer pair apiece. Amplify five inserts plus backbone with overhang primers (1 day), one-pot assembly and transformation (1 day), colony PCR on 8 and whole-plasmid sequencing on 2 (2 days). Hands-on 4 days, calendar 7 with sequencing turnaround.

Gibson. No domestication, but six junctions needing unique 25 bp overlaps, and a terminator shared between two genes must be swapped. Same days, same cost. Synthesis: no construction days, 10–15 working days of lead time, 6 kb of coding sequence as the dominant line item.

So the choice is not cost but which constraint your parts violate: repeats without internal sites, Golden Gate; internal sites without repeats, Gibson; both, or a deadline longer than the lead time, buy the fragments. The one fact that moves the answer is the sequence of the parts, which is why domestication and repeat screening come first, not last.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • expected errors per molecule taken as e·L·d, with about 20 effective duplications
  • errors independent and Poisson-distributed along the amplicon
  • restriction site frequency estimated as 2L/4⁶ at uniform base composition
  • internal site presence independent between the five fragments
  • sequencing and consumable prices as quoted at the time of writing

Common misconceptions

  • That a high-fidelity polymerase makes sequencing optional. It changes the expected error count by two orders of magnitude, not to zero, and the count scales with length: 6 kb at 10⁻⁷ still leaves one clone in eight wrong.
  • That Golden Gate is scarless and Gibson is not. Both are scarless if designed so; the Golden Gate overhang is four bases of your choosing, usually placed at a codon boundary.
  • That sequencing the insert verifies the construct. It verifies the insert. Misassemblies and origin mutations sit in the backbone, which is where a cheap read does not go.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. You must assemble five genes once. Which method, and what single fact would change your answer?

A. Golden Gate by default — one pot, directional, scarless, and five fragments is well inside its range. The fact that changes it is the sequence of the parts: internal type IIS sites at about 39% per kilobase mean domestication, and enough of them push you to Gibson, while a repeated promoter or terminator is a Gibson misassembly risk and argues back. With both problems, or a deadline longer than the synthesis lead time, buy the fragments domesticated.

Q. Colony PCR gives the expected band and the insert sequences perfectly. What can still be wrong?

A. Everything outside the two windows you looked through. The backbone is unread, so a junction formed at a second homologous site, or a mutation in the origin or marker, would not show. The colony may carry two plasmids with the PCR reporting the correct one. And a long insert verified from one end is only partly verified, since each read covers 700–900 informative bases. Whole-plasmid sequencing closes all three.

Q. Why does an eleven-fragment Gibson usually fail where a five-fragment one works?

A. Two compounding reasons. Every junction must find its partner, so success scales as roughly pⁿ and ten junctions punish a per-junction efficiency that five tolerate; and the number of overlap pairs that could cross-anneal grows as n², so misassembly competes harder in the same tube. The remedies are hierarchical assembly or yeast recombination.
