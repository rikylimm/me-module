Dynamic regulation and biosensors

Week content

Feedback control applied to a pathway, which is the clearest place in the course where a chemical engineer arrives already equipped. The content is not the molecular biology of any one sensor but the arithmetic that chooses between them and that places the operating point: a sensor is four numbers — specificity, detection range, sensitivity and reporter dynamic range — and a loop is a gain and a delay. The week's hardest idea is that a 110-fold sensor placed at the wrong concentration resolves nothing, so the design decision is where on the transfer curve you sit, not which reporter you fuse.

Core ideas

1. Static tuning sets one operating point for a process that has several

Week 34 chose a promoter and a copy number, and those choices are fixed for the run. A fed-batch run is not: substrate falls from 20 g L⁻¹ to near zero, biomass rises two orders of magnitude, oxygen transfer saturates, and a toxic intermediate accumulates only after the pathway is carrying flux. A tuning optimal at inoculation is wrong at harvest, and the growth-production trade-off makes the error systematic rather than random — the expression level that maximises product per cell is below the level that maximises product per litre, because the latter needs the biomass that the former suppresses. Dynamic regulation does not improve on the best static tuning at any single instant; it improves on the fact that no single instant is representative.

2. There are four sensor classes and they fail differently

Protein Engineering Ch 2 divides them by what does the sensing. RNA-type riboswitches put an aptamer upstream of an expression platform, so ligand binding changes a secondary structure and the ribosome or terminator reads the consequence; they need no protein and so impose almost no burden, but the aptamer must be selected for each new ligand. DNA-type sensors act through a DNA-binding event rather than a conformational switch. Transcription-factor sensors hand the problem to an evolved allosteric protein, which is why they amplify — a small change in ligand is converted through gene expression into a large change in phenotype — and why they are confined to metabolites some organism already had a reason to sense. Enzyme-type sensors convert the analyte chemically into something visible, as DOPA dioxygenase turns DOPA into the fluorescent pigment betaxanthin; they work on intermediates no regulator recognises, at the cost of consuming the thing being measured.

3. A sensor is characterised by four numbers, and three of them are not the dynamic range

§2.4 names specificity, detection range, sensitivity and reporter dynamic range, and they answer different questions. Specificity sets the false-positive rate: the theophylline aptamer binds theophylline about 10,000-fold more tightly than caffeine, which is why a screen built on it returned no false positives across a large library, whereas a transcription factor with a broad effector profile returns hits that respond to something else in the medium. Detection range is the span of analyte concentrations that produce any response at all. Sensitivity is the derivative — the change in output per change in analyte — and it is what decides whether a 30% improvement is visible. Reporter dynamic range is the output span between off and full on, reported as 110-fold for the DOPA sensor. Note that response time is not in this list; §2.4 does not treat it, and a loop analysis needs it, so it has to come from the promoter and maturation literature rather than from this chapter.

4. Where you place the operating point decides what the sensor can resolve

Model the response as a Hill curve, output = R_min + (R_max − R_min)·sᵃ/(Kᵃ + sᵃ). The useful quantity is the local logarithmic gain, d ln(output)/d ln(s), which for a Hill form peaks near s = K and falls to zero at both ends. With K placed at the wild-type concentration the sensor is maximally sensitive to the improvements you are looking for; with K placed three-fold above it, a candidate sitting at 1.3× wild type moves almost nowhere on the curve, and the 110-fold dynamic range is spent on concentrations no variant in the library reaches. This is why sensor engineering is mostly promoter and operator engineering: moving K, by changing operator affinity or sensor expression level, is the design variable, and widening R_max/R_min is secondary.

5. Architecture: feedback, feedforward, and the two-stage switch that is neither

Negative feedback from an accumulating intermediate onto the enzyme that makes it holds the intermediate near a set point and is the architecture the control course predicts. Feedforward from the substrate anticipates rather than corrects, which matters when the lag between sensing and actuation is comparable to the disturbance. The architecture most often actually implemented is neither: a two-stage switch that runs growth to a threshold and then redirects carbon. Lee §18.2.5.3 is the worked case — flux to farnesyl diphosphate is raised while the downstream squalene synthase Erg9 is throttled, so the native ERG9 promoter is replaced with a copper- or methionine-inducible promoter or the glucose-dependent P<i>HXT1</i>, giving enough Erg9 for biomass in the growth phase and little in the production phase. Alternatives named there are deleting the promoter's upstream activation sequence or adding a degradation tag, which move the switch from transcription to protein turnover and change its speed accordingly.

6. Quorum sensing buys timing without an inducer, and pays in tunability

An inducible promoter needs someone to add the inducer at the right optical density, which is a manual step in the laboratory and a cost line plus a validation problem at scale. A quorum-sensing circuit makes the switch a function of cell density itself, so the timing is set at design time by the synthase and receptor strengths rather than at run time by an operator. The cost is that the set point is now a property of the construct: changing when the switch fires means rebuilding, and the same circuit fires at a different time in a different medium or vessel because the autoinducer concentration depends on dilution rate and on whether the molecule is stable at the run's pH.

7. Gain and delay, which is the part no assigned text supplies

A genetic loop oscillates when the loop gain exceeds one at the frequency where the phase lag reaches 180°. Both terms are estimable. The gain is the product of the local logarithmic sensitivities around the loop — sensor gain times promoter gain times the elasticity of the controlled step — and a Hill coefficient of 2 contributes at most 2 to it, which is why cooperativity is what makes a genetic circuit capable of oscillating at all. The delay is the sum of transcription, translation, folding and reporter maturation, tens of minutes in total, against a dilution time set by the growth rate: at μ = 0.6 h⁻¹ the protein half-life by dilution is about 70 minutes, so the delay is a substantial fraction of the loop's own time constant. The practical reading is that a high-gain loop with a slow reporter is the configuration that rings, and that lowering the gain — a weaker operator, a less cooperative sensor — is the first fix.

8. Converting a screen into a selection, and the escape problem

A screen measures every clone; a selection makes the clone measure itself, which is the only way to reach 10⁸ and above. Coupling titre to growth — the sensor driving an antibiotic resistance gene, or complementing an auxotrophy — converts throughput from a cost into a non-issue, and Protein Engineering Ch 2 notes that resistance markers used in tandem widen the effective dynamic range in units of growth rate and tighten stringency. The failure is specific and predictable: any mutation that produces signal without producing product is selected harder than the phenotype you want, because it is cheaper. Reporter mutation, operator mutation that makes the promoter constitutive, and loss of the sensor's repression are all escapes, and all are more probable per cell than the improvement being selected for. The countermeasures are a second orthogonal reporter, periodic reversion of the selection, and sequencing the sensor rather than only the target in every hit.

Worked anchor

Can a biosensor screen resolve a 30% improvement? Take a transcription-factor sensor for the pathway intermediate, driving GFP, characterised as in §2.4: reporter dynamic range R_max/R_min = 110, Hill coefficient n = 2, and K tunable by operator choice. The wild-type strain sits at s₀. Flow cytometry on 10⁴ events per clone has a coefficient of variation of about 15% on the population mean fluorescence, dominated by expression noise rather than by counting statistics.

First, the gain at the operating point. For a Hill form the logarithmic gain is d ln R / d ln s = n·Kⁿ/(Kⁿ + sⁿ). At s = K it is n/2 = 1.0; at s = 3K it is 2/(1+9) = 0.2; at s = K/3 it is 2·9/10 = 1.8.

Now the signal. A 1.3-fold improvement is Δln s = ln 1.3 = 0.262. Placed at K, the output moves by 1.0 × 0.262 = 0.262 in ln units, a 30% rise in fluorescence. Taking the 15% CV as 0.15 in ln units, that is 0.262/0.15 = 1.7 sigma — detectable, marginally, and only with replicates. Placed at 3K the output moves by 0.2 × 0.262 = 0.052, a 5% rise, which is 0.35 sigma and invisible. Placed at K/3 it moves 1.8 × 0.262 = 0.47, a 60% rise and 3.1 sigma, which is comfortable.

So the sensor does not have a resolution; the sensor plus its operating point does, and the same 110-fold reporter gives either a four-sigma or an undetectable readout depending on a choice about operator affinity. Note also that the 110-fold dynamic range never entered the arithmetic, because the question was a derivative and the dynamic range is a range.

Second, how many clones. If the improvement sought is present at 1 in 10³ of the library, a plate-based screen of 10³ clones finds about one of them. At the operating point K, a one-tailed threshold set at 1.7 sigma lets through about 4% of unimproved clones, so the same 10³ clones also yield roughly 40 false positives for that one true hit — a confirmation assay on 41 candidates. At K/3 and 3.1 sigma the pass rate is about 0.1%, so the expected false-positive count across the whole plate set is one. The screen's real output is therefore not a hit rate but a confirmation workload, and moving K is what sets it.

Note what did not help. Widening the reporter from 110-fold to 2,000-fold changes nothing at all here, because the logarithmic gain of a Hill form is independent of the amplitude R_max − R_min — the amplitude cancels when the derivative is taken in log units. The only two levers on resolution are K, through operator affinity or sensor expression, and the Hill coefficient n, which sets the maximum gain at n/2. A three-fold shift in K is one round of operator mutagenesis; a change in n means a different sensor protein.

The stopping decision follows: if no operator variant puts K within about three-fold of the wild-type concentration, the sensor cannot support a screen at this library size and the money belongs in a growth-coupled selection instead, where throughput buys back what resolution cannot.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • sensor response modelled as a Hill curve with n = 2, so the logarithmic gain is n·Kⁿ/(Kⁿ + sⁿ)
  • the 110-fold reporter dynamic range and the Hill coefficient taken as independent of where K sits
  • assay coefficient of variation 15% on the population mean of 10⁴ events, dominated by expression noise rather than counting statistics
  • output noise taken as multiplicative, so a CV in linear units is a standard deviation in ln units
  • fluorescence proportional to the reporter protein and the reporter protein to promoter output, with no maturation lag inside the measurement
  • the improvement sought present at 1 in 10³ of the library, which is an assumption about the library and not a measurement
  • false-positive rates read off a normal distribution at 1.7 and 3.1 sigma, one-tailed
  • K movable by operator mutagenesis over at least a three-fold range, which is the premise the stopping decision tests

Common misconceptions

  • That a wide dynamic range makes a sensor sensitive. Dynamic range is the span between off and full on; sensitivity is the slope at the concentration you are actually at. A 110-fold reporter with K three-fold above the operating point resolves a 30% improvement as a 5% signal, which is invisible against a 15% assay CV. Move K, not R_max.
  • That negative feedback always stabilises. It stabilises when the loop gain is low and the delay is short. A genetic loop has a delay of tens of minutes from transcription, translation and maturation, and cooperativity raises the gain, so a tightly tuned negative loop is exactly the configuration that oscillates. The chemical engineering intuition transfers, including the part about overshoot.
  • That a growth-coupled selection removes the screening problem. It removes the throughput problem and creates an escape problem. Any mutation that produces signal without product is selected harder than the phenotype you want, because it is cheaper to acquire; reporter and operator mutations are more probable per cell than the improvement. A selection without a counter-check is a device for enriching cheats.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. What determines whether a genetic feedback loop oscillates, and how would you detect it experimentally?

A. Loop gain above one at the frequency where the phase lag reaches 180°. The gain is the product of the logarithmic sensitivities around the loop — sensor, promoter, and the elasticity of the controlled step — with cooperativity contributing up to its Hill coefficient; the lag is transcription plus translation plus folding plus reporter maturation, tens of minutes, against a dilution time of about 70 minutes at μ = 0.6 h⁻¹. Detection needs time-resolved single-cell measurement, because a population average of cells oscillating out of phase is flat: either a microfluidic device with per-cell traces, or synchronisation followed by sampling at intervals short compared with the suspected period. A flat bulk fluorescence trace is not evidence of a settled loop.

Q. Your sensor has a 110-fold dynamic range and your best variant gives no measurable signal increase. List the explanations in order of how cheaply you can test them.

A. Cheapest first: the operating point is wrong, so test by titrating exogenous analyte across three orders of magnitude and finding where the response curve actually sits relative to the strain's concentration. Next, the analyte is not reaching the sensor — compartmentalisation, export, or degradation — tested by measuring the intracellular pool directly. Next, the reporter is saturated at the top rather than the bottom, tested by the same titration read for a plateau. Next, the variant does not in fact make more product, tested by the confirmation assay the screen was supposed to replace. Last and most expensive, the sensor has mutated or the promoter has gone constitutive, tested by sequencing the sensor cassette in the clone. The ordering matters because the first explanation is both the most likely and the only one fixed without new biology.

Q. Why would you choose a quorum-sensing switch over an inducible promoter, and what do you give up?

A. You give up run-time control and gain process simplicity. An inducible promoter needs an operator to add inducer at the right density, which is a manual step, a cost line and a validation burden at scale, and in a food or pharmaceutical process the inducer may be unacceptable in the product. Quorum sensing makes the switch a function of cell density, so the timing is fixed at design time by synthase and receptor strengths. What you give up is tunability: moving the set point means rebuilding the circuit, and the same construct fires at a different time in a different medium, vessel or dilution rate, because the autoinducer concentration depends on both accumulation and the molecule's stability at the run's pH. The right question is whether the set point needs to change more often than the construct does.
