Flux balance analysis

Week content

Case thread: What can FBA predict here, and what did the thesis not measure? Open the case thread.

The linear program you already know, with biology in the objective. The formulation takes ten minutes; the week's real content is the assumptions, the duality, and the discipline of checking the model's arithmetic against your own. Install the toolchain properly now — the environment you built in week 1 has drifted by twenty-two weeks and needs re-pinning before anything else.

Core ideas

1. The formulation, derived rather than quoted

Start from the dynamic mass balance, dx/dt = S·v, where x is metabolite concentrations. Intracellular pools turn over in seconds while growth changes over hours, so set dx/dt = 0 and you have S·v = 0. Add capacity bounds lb ≤ v ≤ ub and you have a feasible region: a convex polytope in flux space, bounded and high-dimensional. Choose a linear objective cᵀv and you have a linear program. That is all FBA is. Note the two decisions hidden in it: the bounds, which are mostly guesses, and the objective, which is an evolutionary argument rather than a physical law.

2. Sign conventions, which cost everyone a day

Exchange reactions are written as A_e ⇌ with uptake negative. So the maximum glucose uptake rate is set as the lower bound of the glucose exchange: a bound of −10 permits uptake up to 10 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹, and setting it to 0 forbids uptake while still permitting secretion. Anaerobiosis is the oxygen exchange lower bound set to zero. Getting this backwards produces a model that grows without a carbon source or cannot grow at all, and it is the single commonest beginner error in the toolchain.

3. The geometry, which is worth holding in your head

The feasible region is a convex polytope: mass balance gives hyperplanes, bounds give half-spaces. The objective is a vector, and the optimum sits where that vector is maximised over the polytope — generically at a vertex. Two consequences follow directly. If the objective vector happens to be parallel to an edge or a face, every point on that edge is optimal and the solution is degenerate, which is both common and the geometric reason flux variability analysis exists. And the optimum lies on the boundary, meaning at least some constraints are active: identifying which ones are binding tells you what is limiting, which is the dual problem.

4. Duality, read as marginal value

Every linear program has a dual, and the dual variables of the mass-balance constraints are shadow prices: the marginal change in the objective per unit change in the availability of a metabolite. A shadow price of 0.045 gDW/mmol for NADPH means one extra mmol of NADPH per gram dry weight per hour would raise growth by 0.045 h⁻¹. Reduced costs do the same job for reactions. These numbers are often more informative than the flux distribution, because they say what is scarce rather than what is flowing — and scarcity is what you engineer.

5. The assumptions, ranked by fragility

Rank them, because listing them is what everyone does and ranking them is what demonstrates understanding. Most fragile: that the cell optimises the chosen objective — an evolutionary argument that holds for a long-adapted wild type in a constant environment and fails for an engineered strain or immediately after a perturbation. Then the biomass composition, measured in one condition and applied to all. Then capacity bounds, mostly guessed. Then network completeness for the condition. Least fragile: steady state itself, which follows from the timescale separation. Note that the weakest assumption is the one that makes FBA work at all.

6. Cross-checking, which is the habit that matters

Never report a model number you have not checked against something independent. Compute ATP per glucose by hand through glycolysis, pyruvate dehydrogenase and the TCA cycle with a stated P/O, then compute it from the model with the biomass reaction bounded to zero and the maintenance reaction as objective, and resolve the discrepancy in writing. The resolution is itself informative: it will turn on which NADH dehydrogenase and oxidase the model carries and what maintenance it holds. A student who reconciles the two understands the model; a student who reports only the model's number does not.

Worked anchor

Maximum ATP yield, by hand and by model, and why they differ.

By hand, for E. coli on glucose with the efficient respiratory chain: glycolysis gives 2 ATP and 2 NADH; pyruvate dehydrogenase 2 NADH; the TCA cycle 2 GTP, 6 NADH and 2 FADH₂. With Complex I and cytochrome bo₃ at 8 charge equivalents per NADH and 4.3 H⁺ per ATP, that is 1.86 ATP per NADH and about 0.93 per FADH₂: 4 + 18.6 + 1.86 ≈ 24.5 ATP.

By model: set the glucose exchange lower bound to −10, oxygen unconstrained, the biomass reaction bounded to zero, and the ATP maintenance reaction as the objective. Divide the result by 10.

The model typically returns somewhat less than the hand value, in the low twenties. Resolve it rather than note it. Candidate explanations, each checkable: the model may use a different proton stoichiometry for its dehydrogenase or oxidase; it may carry a non-zero lower bound on maintenance that consumes part of the yield; its ATP synthase may be parameterised with a different H⁺/ATP; and it may route some flux through the less efficient NADH dehydrogenase if that is unconstrained and equivalent. Inspect the reactions, find which applies, and write the answer down.

This exercise is Brief 6's core and it is the clearest demonstration available that you can use the tool and interrogate it.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the efficient respiratory chain: Complex I and cytochrome bo₃
  • 4.3 H⁺ per ATP, GTP counted as ATP
  • biomass bounded to zero and maintenance as stated
  • the model's own stoichiometry may differ — that is the point of the comparison

Common misconceptions

  • That FBA predicts what a cell does. It predicts what a cell could do while optimising an objective you chose, under bounds you chose.
  • That setting an uptake limit means setting an upper bound. Uptake is negative flux, so the limit is the lower bound — the error that costs everyone a day.
  • That a flux distribution is the result. The shadow prices are often the result; they say what is scarce, and scarcity is what you engineer.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Write the FBA problem formally for anaerobic growth on glucose at 10 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹.

A. maximise cᵀv subject to S·v = 0 and lb ≤ v ≤ ub, with v_EX_glc_e ≥ −10, v_EX_o2_e = 0, and c selecting the biomass reaction. The sign convention matters: uptake is negative flux, so the uptake limit appears as a lower bound on the exchange reaction, and oxygen is excluded by setting its exchange lower bound to zero while leaving secretion permitted.

Q. Interpret a shadow price of 0.021 gDW/mmol for ATP and 0.045 for NADPH, and say what you would do with them.

A. One extra mmol of ATP per gDW per hour would raise growth rate by 0.021 h⁻¹; one extra mmol of NADPH by 0.045 h⁻¹. NADPH is therefore about twice as valuable per mole, so the network is more constrained by reducing power than by energy under these conditions — a warning for a reductive product, which competes with growth for the scarcer currency. To act on it, test the prediction: supply reducing power without carbon, or express a transhydrogenase, and check whether growth and yield respond in the predicted ratio. If they respond in the opposite order, the model's cofactor balance is wrong, which is itself a useful result.

Q. Rank the assumptions of FBA by fragility and justify the extremes.

A. Most fragile is optimality of the chosen objective, because it is an assumption about behaviour rather than physics and fails for engineered strains and after perturbations. Then biomass composition, measured in one condition and applied to all. Then capacity bounds, mostly guessed. Then network completeness for the condition in question. Least fragile is steady state, which follows from metabolite pools turning over in seconds while growth changes over hours. The ranking matters because the assumption doing the most work is also the least secure.
