Strain design and growth coupling

Week content

The payoff of the phase: turning a model into a genotype. It is also where model-driven design must be criticised honestly, because most published designs underperform their predictions and the reasons are systematic rather than accidental. Paper 3 is sat at the end of this week.

Core ideas

1. The production envelope, and how to read it

Plot product yield against growth rate by maximising product at each fixed growth rate. The resulting region's upper boundary is the maximum achievable yield at that growth rate, and the two ends tell you the most: the maximum product yield at zero growth, which is the stoichiometric ceiling, and the yield at maximum growth, which is usually near zero for an unengineered strain. The engineering goal is to deform this envelope so that high product yield is the only option at any appreciable growth rate. Reading an envelope is the first thing to do with any design, and producing one is required by Brief 8.

2. Growth coupling, stated precisely

A design is growth-coupled when product formation is stoichiometrically required for growth — when every flux distribution that produces biomass also produces product. Weak coupling means product is required above some growth rate; strong coupling means at any growth rate. The mechanism is always the same: delete every alternative sink for something growth necessarily generates, usually a reduced cofactor, so that the product pathway becomes the unique balance. The reason to want it is not elegance but population genetics — a fermentation spans roughly thirty generations from vial to production vessel, and without coupling the fastest-growing non-producer sweeps.

3. Bilevel design, and what the algorithms actually do

The design problem is naturally bilevel: choose deletions such that, when the cell subsequently maximises growth, product formation is high. The inner problem is the cell's optimisation and the outer is yours, which is formally awkward and is handled by converting the inner problem to its optimality conditions, giving a mixed-integer program. OptKnock does this for deletions; descendants add insertions, up- and down-regulation, and heuristic search for larger design spaces. What matters for you is the structure, not the solver: the algorithms assume the cell optimises growth, so every criticism of that assumption in week 23 applies to their output.

4. Why designs underperform, and the five levels to check

Systematically, not accidentally. Thermodynamic: a step that is feasible in the model is near equilibrium at real concentrations. Kinetic: the pathway enzymes cannot carry the predicted flux at achievable expression. Proteome: the required enzyme mass is a large fraction of the budget, so growth falls and volumetric productivity with it. Regulatory: the host down-regulates something the design depends on. Toxicity: the product or an intermediate inhibits at the titre required. A design document without these five, each with a measurement that would detect it, is incomplete — and writing them is what Brief 8 grades.

5. Escape routes, and checking them against your actual medium

A coupling argument is only as good as the set of alternatives you enumerated. Common escapes: transhydrogenase activity shuttling electrons between cofactor pools; hydrogen evolution via formate hydrogen lyase; an unannotated dehydrogenase; respiration on a trace electron acceptor present in the medium but not in the model; and suppressor mutations restoring a deleted activity. The first four are checkable in the model, and the medium one is the trap — check the coupling against the medium you will actually use, including its complex components, not a generic minimal one.

6. What to build first, which is a judgement rather than an output

Algorithms return many designs of similar predicted yield. Choose among them on grounds the model cannot see: how many genetic interventions are required, whether the deleted genes are essential under some other condition you will encounter, whether the predicted flux through any step exceeds a plausible enzyme capacity, whether an intermediate is known to be toxic, and whether the coupling survives the escapes above. A defended choice between two algorithmic designs is worth more in an interview than the design itself.

Worked anchor

Construct a growth-coupled design by hand, then attack it.

Target: a product requiring 2 NADH per mole, made anaerobically.

The argument. Anaerobically, ATP comes only from substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis. Glycolysis necessarily produces NADH at glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. The NAD pool is conserved — it appears in the left null space from week 22 — so NADH must be reoxidised or glycolysis halts. Delete lactate dehydrogenase, alcohol dehydrogenase and the fumarate reductase route, and the product pathway is the only remaining reoxidation route. Therefore: no product, no NADH reoxidation; no reoxidation, no glycolysis; no glycolysis, no ATP; no ATP, no growth. The coupling is proved from conservation rather than asserted from an algorithm.

Now attack it. Transhydrogenase: can the cell move electrons to NADPH and dispose of them in biosynthesis? Check whether the model has one and whether the real strain does. Hydrogen: is formate hydrogen lyase present? Delete it or check its expression anaerobically. Unannotated dehydrogenases: how many genes in this genome are annotated as oxidoreductases with unknown substrate? Medium: does your medium contain nitrate, fumarate, DMSO or TMAO as a trace component — because any of them restores respiration and breaks the coupling entirely. Suppressors: at 10¹² cells, every single-base reversion exists.

The design is the easy part. The enumeration of escapes is the part that distinguishes a defensible proposal.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • anaerobic, with no trace electron acceptor in the medium
  • the model carries no unannotated oxidoreductase
  • the NAD pool is conserved, as the left null space says
  • minimal medium, so no route is rescued by a supplied nutrient

Common misconceptions

  • That growth coupling is elegance. It is population genetics: without it the fastest-growing non-producer sweeps across a thirty-generation seed train.
  • That the algorithm chooses the design. It returns many of similar predicted yield; choosing among them on grounds the model cannot see is the engineering.
  • That checking escapes in the model is enough. The commonest escape is a trace electron acceptor in a medium the model does not describe.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Prove growth coupling for an anaerobic design from conservation rather than from an algorithm.

A. Anaerobically ATP comes only from substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis, and glycolysis necessarily produces NADH at GAPDH. The total NAD pool is a conserved moiety — it lies in the left null space — so NADH must be reoxidised for glycolysis to continue. With every alternative sink deleted, the product pathway is the unique reoxidation route. Hence no product means no reoxidation, no reoxidation means no glycolytic flux, no glycolysis means no ATP, and no ATP means no growth. The chain is stoichiometric throughout.

Q. Name the five levels at which a model-driven design underperforms, with a discriminating measurement for each.

A. Thermodynamic — compute ΔrG′ from measured intracellular concentrations and look for a step near equilibrium. Kinetic — measure in vitro activity in lysate and compare with the flux required. Proteome — quantify the pathway's share of total protein and compare growth against an inert-protein control at matched expression. Regulatory — measure transcript and protein for the pathway and for the host genes the design depends on. Toxicity — add the product or intermediate exogenously at the target intracellular concentration and measure growth and specific productivity.

Q. Your coupled design works in silico. Name four escape routes and say which is hardest to see.

A. Transhydrogenase activity moving electrons between the NADH and NADPH pools; hydrogen evolution through formate hydrogen lyase; an unannotated oxidoreductase providing an alternative sink; and respiration on a trace electron acceptor — nitrate, fumarate, DMSO or TMAO — present in the medium. The last is hardest to see, because it is absent from the model entirely: the coupling is checked against a minimal medium while the experiment runs on something richer. Suppressor mutations are a fifth, inevitable at 10¹² cells, and the reason coupling must be stringent rather than marginal.
