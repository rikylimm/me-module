13C metabolic flux analysis II: estimation, statistics and design

Week content

Case thread: Which 13C tracer would resolve the split, and what precision is needed? Open the case thread.

The inverse problem. Fitting fluxes to labelling data is nonlinear estimation with an unpleasant covariance structure, and the statistics is where most published MFA is weakest. Three things separate a flux claim from a flux opinion: a multi-start optimisation, an interval from a method that does not assume local linearity, and a goodness-of-fit test you are prepared to let fail.

Core ideas

1. The objective, and why it is minimised a hundred times

Weighted least squares: minimise Σᵢ ((xᵢ,meas − xᵢ,sim(v))/σᵢ)² over the free fluxes, with x_sim from week 28's forward model. The weights are not cosmetic — unweighted, a precise fragment and a noisy one speak equally, and the σᵢ are what turn the residual sum into a statistic with a known distribution. The surface is nonlinear with local minima, so one run is not a result. Restart from random points until the best objective has been recovered repeatedly — how many that takes depends on the dimension, the bounds, the parameterisation and the optimiser, so report the strategy and the spread of objective values you obtained rather than a round number. Restarts landing on several distinct minima of similar objective value signal a genuine identifiability problem rather than a numerical one.

2. Chi-squared is the model's check on itself

At the optimum, and only if the weighted residual model is correctly specified, the objective is compared against a chi-squared distribution. Counting the degrees of freedom is where this goes wrong: it is the number of independent data constraints minus the rank of the fitted model, and MDV elements are not independent — each vector sums to one, so one element per fragment is fixed by the others, and natural abundance correction couples them further. Fitted pool sizes, scaling and other nuisance parameters, and active constraints reduce it too. With the effective count in hand, the expectation is the degrees of freedom and the standard deviation √(2·dof), which locates the acceptance region closely enough to judge a fit by eye; take the quantile from the distribution itself when the decision is marginal or formal. This is the most valuable property of the method and the one most often skipped, because it lets the data tell you the model is wrong. Note the limit: a passing test says model and data are consistent, not that the model is correct — compensating errors fit perfectly — which is why a second tracer fitted jointly is the real validation.

3. Diagnosing a failure, in order of probability

Structural error first: a missing reaction, a wrong atom mapping, an omitted by-product, a wrong biomass drain. Second, underestimated measurement errors, which inflate the statistic with nothing wrong with the model. Third, an unmodelled bidirectional exchange, which shows as systematic residuals on the metabolites whose label it scrambles. Fourth, a violated steady state. Discriminate by residual pattern rather than preference: structural errors cluster on particular fragments and mass positions, an inadequate error model inflates residuals roughly uniformly. Rescaling the errors until the test passes is the one response guaranteed to teach you nothing.

4. Confidence intervals: three methods, one of them right

Linearised covariance inverts the weighted Jacobian, (JᵀΣ⁻¹J)⁻¹, and reads intervals off the diagonal: nearly free, the usual software default, and valid only where the objective is quadratic near the optimum — true for well-determined net fluxes, false for exchange fluxes and anything near a bound, where it returns symmetric intervals that are too narrow. Parameter continuation walks one flux away from the optimum while re-optimising all the others, until the objective rise crosses a threshold: asymmetric, sometimes unbounded, one optimisation per point. It is profile likelihood proper only when the objective carries that likelihood interpretation and the nuisance parameters are re-optimised consistently, and the threshold depends on the confidence level, on how many parameters are profiled at once, and on the residual model being right. Bootstrap agrees with continuation when the problem is well-posed and is honest about the error model you assumed. Exchange fluxes are the usual case where the two part company, though not inevitably: whether one is bounded at all depends on the network topology, the tracer and the fragments measured.

5. What actually tightens an interval

Four interventions in descending value. Add extracellular rates: labelling alone fixes the map only up to its scale, so folding in well-measured uptake and secretion often halves intervals across the network. Run a parallel labelling experiment with a second tracer and fit jointly, which adds information of a different kind and tests the structure at the same time. Measure more fragments, particularly covering different subsets of a skeleton. Improving precision on fragments you already have comes last — in a structurally limited fit it tightens the interval around a biased answer, which is worse than a wide interval around the right one.

6. Design the experiment in silico, then run it

Which tracer or mixture minimises the variance of the flux you care about? Simulate data at a plausible flux vector, compute the Jacobian, invert for the covariance, read off the target variance, repeat per candidate. Optimise a scalar — one flux's variance for a specific question, the determinant or trace for a general-purpose map. The circularity is real and manageable: design against a range of plausible flux vectors and prefer a tracer whose advantage holds across it. Often two tracers differ by a few per cent and the choice is a cost decision, which costs a day to establish rather than an experiment.

7. Instationary MFA, and what it costs

Stationary MFA needs metabolic and isotopic steady state. The isotopic half can take hours with large pools or slow flux, and for an autotroph fed labelled CO₂ it may never arrive inside the metabolic steady state's lifetime. INST-MFA relaxes it: label at metabolic steady state and fit the transient. The forward model becomes ODEs over isotopomer fractions rather than an algebraic balance, one to two orders of magnitude more expensive, and pool sizes become parameters, since the transient's timescale is pool/flux — so week 27's metabolomics is an input, not an extra. The transient also constrains some fluxes the steady-state pattern cannot.

Worked anchor

A failed chi-squared, diagnosed arithmetically.

The fit returns χ² = 84 on 41 degrees of freedom. Expectation 41, standard deviation √82 = 9.06, so the observed value is (84 − 41)/9.06 = 4.7 standard deviations high: a decisive rejection. The fluxes may still be approximately right, but the intervals computed from this fit are meaningless, because they assume the residuals are what the error model says.

Test the cheapest explanation first. If every σᵢ were underestimated by a common factor k the statistic scales as 1/k², so k = √(84/41) = 1.43 would bring it to expectation. Is a 43% across-the-board underestimate plausible? From three technical replicates, entirely — three replicates estimate a standard deviation to only about 50% (the relative uncertainty on an SD is 1/√(2(n−1)); 40% would need four replicates), and replicate spread omits derivatisation and correction error anyway. From a validated error model with biological replicates, no.

Then look at residuals rather than the total. Standardise every MDV element and sort. Spread evenly across fragments and mass positions, the error model is the suspect; concentrated — the m+1 and m+2 of aspartate at five and six standard deviations while everything else sits near one — the cause is structural and touches oxaloacetate: a missing anaplerotic reaction, an unmodelled exchange on malate dehydrogenase, or a fumarate symmetry you failed to encode.

Test that properly. Add the candidate reaction and refit; one free flux costs one degree of freedom, so the gain must be much larger. Suppose χ² drops from 84 to 39 on 40 degrees of freedom: a drop of 45 for one parameter is overwhelming, since on one degree of freedom 3.84 is already the 5% threshold, so the reaction belongs. Then look at what it did to the fluxes. If the anaplerotic flux moved by a factor of two, the original map was wrong in exactly the way a passing eye never catches, because it looked plausible — which is the lesson for the critique note, and why Brief 9 asks you to omit a reaction deliberately and confirm the statistic catches it.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the weighted residual model is correctly specified
  • measurement errors independent and normally distributed
  • degrees of freedom counted as measurements minus fitted fluxes, which ignores MDV closure
  • a single structural fault rather than several compensating ones

Common misconceptions

  • That a good fit validates the flux map. A passing chi-squared says model and data are consistent; compensating errors fit perfectly. Validation comes from a second tracer.
  • That the software's confidence intervals are confidence intervals. The default is linearised covariance, symmetric and too narrow wherever the objective is not locally quadratic — which includes every exchange flux.
  • That a failed chi-squared means noisy data. It means model and data disagree, and the most probable cause is a structural error in the model.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Your fit gives χ² = 96 on 44 degrees of freedom. Diagnose it, in order.

A. Rejected: on the stated degrees of freedom, expectation 44 and standard deviation √88 = 9.4, so 96 sits 5.5 standard deviations high. In order of probability: a structural error — missing reaction, wrong atom mapping, omitted by-product; underestimated measurement errors, which would need a common factor of √(96/44) = 1.48 to explain the excess, plausible from a handful of technical replicates and not otherwise; an unmodelled bidirectional exchange; and a violated steady state. Discriminate by sorting the standardised residuals, since structural errors cluster on particular fragments while an inadequate error model inflates them uniformly. Then test the leading hypothesis by adding the reaction and refitting, judged against the degree of freedom it costs.

Q. Why do exchange fluxes come back with unbounded intervals, and what should you report?

A. Because beyond a certain magnitude, raising a bidirectional exchange stops changing the labelling pattern: once the step equilibrates label across itself, further increases are invisible, the likelihood surface flattens and the upper bound runs to infinity. Linearised covariance cannot express that and returns a finite symmetric interval, which is simply wrong; continuation finds the flat direction and reports the bound honestly. Report the net flux with its interval, the exchange on the transformed scale that maps the infinite range onto a finite one, and the statement that it is bounded below but not above — a real conclusion about reversibility, not a defect.

Q. You have budget for one more experiment: a second tracer, more fragments, or better precision. Choose and justify.

A. The second tracer, almost always. It adds information of a different kind rather than more of the same, and a structural error that fits one tracer usually fails against two fitted jointly, so it validates and tightens at once. More fragments comes second, and is better when the target flux is constrained by one skeleton and the structure is not in doubt. Better precision comes last: in a structurally limited fit it tightens the interval around a biased estimate, and in a sound fit the returns are modest because the limit is the information content of the pattern, not the noise on it.
