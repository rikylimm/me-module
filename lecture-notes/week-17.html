Week 17 of 55 · Quantitative physiology · Core

Stoichiometry, yields and data reconciliation

Week content

The most useful thing you bring to an interview: the ability to compute, on paper and in ten minutes, the best any strain could possibly do — and then to show that published data does not close. This week is almost entirely your own discipline applied to biological data, and it produces two portfolio artifacts.

Core ideas

1. Elemental balances, done the engineer's way

Write the growth and production stoichiometry as a chemical equation: a C-mol of substrate plus ammonia plus oxygen gives biomass plus product plus CO₂ plus water. Balance C, H, O and N, which gives four equations. Count your unknowns — typically the yields of biomass, product, CO₂ and water on substrate, plus oxygen. With four balances and five unknowns you need one measurement to close, which is exactly why a single measured yield plus elemental balances determines everything else. This is the method Doran Ch 4 develops and it is strictly more powerful than the degree-of-reduction shortcut, because it tells you water and oxygen too.

2. Degree of reduction as the fast route

γ per C-mol, referenced to CO₂, H₂O and NH₃: carbon contributes 4, hydrogen 1, oxygen −2, nitrogen −3. Glucose 4.00, ethanol 6.00, acetate 4.00, lactate 4.00, glycerol 4.67, succinate 3.50, butanol 6.00, methane 8.00, biomass 4.20. The available-electron balance says electrons in substrate equal electrons in biomass plus product plus those passed to oxygen. For a maximum yield calculation set biomass to zero and oxygen to zero, and the ceiling follows immediately as γ_substrate/γ_product per C-mol. It is the thirty-second version of the elemental balance and it is what you do at a whiteboard.

3. Which constraint binds, and what that tells you to do

For each product compute both ceilings — carbon and electrons — and see which is lower. Ethanol: electrons allow 2 mol per glucose, carbon allows 3, so electrons bind at 0.511 g/g, with the two CO₂ released precisely to balance electrons. Acetate: both give 3 mol, so neither is slack and the route is intrinsically efficient. Butanol: electrons allow 1, carbon 1.5, electrons bind. Succinate: more oxidised than glucose, so electrons are in surplus and CO₂ fixation raises the carbon ceiling above what glucose alone supports. The engineering reading: where electrons bind, look for a cheaper electron source or a less reduced product; where carbon binds, attack decarboxylations; where both bind simultaneously, yield is not your problem and titre and tolerance will be.

4. Theoretical maximum by linear programming, and why it differs

Set up the same problem as an LP over a stoichiometric network with the product exchange as the objective, and you get a ceiling that accounts for cofactor and ATP constraints the hand calculation ignores. The two should agree where the hand calculation's assumptions hold, and differ where they do not — typically because the network cannot actually balance ATP or NADPH at the hand-calculated optimum. When they disagree, the LP is usually right and the discrepancy tells you which cofactor is binding. Running both and reconciling them is Brief 4, and the reconciliation is the result rather than either number.

5. Closing a real balance, and gross error detection

Measured data almost never closes. The procedure: compute carbon and electron closure separately, then read the pattern, as week 13 established — carbon short with electrons complete implicates CO₂; both short in proportion implicates biomass; electrons short alone implicates H₂ or a reduced gas; both high implicates one over-reported stream. Then localise properly: with more measurements than degrees of freedom the system is redundant, and redundancy lets you compute a test statistic that identifies which measurement is inconsistent with the others rather than merely that something is. The cardinal rule, which examiners test: a balance is reconciled by finding the missing stream, never by scaling the measured ones.

6. Titre, rate and yield — and the thresholds that matter

Yield sets raw material cost and cannot be bought back with equipment. Titre sets the water load and therefore every downstream separation cost, and below a product-specific threshold a separation becomes infeasible rather than merely expensive. Rate sets annual throughput and therefore capital per kilogram, and is the parameter most readily substituted by capital. For a commodity the ranking is yield, titre, rate; for a high-value product it inverts towards titre first, because substrate is negligible and purification dominates; and for an existing plant rate leads because capital is sunk. Learn to state the regime before giving the ranking — that is the whole content of week 52 previewed here.

Worked anchor

Derive ethanol's ceiling twice, then do succinate to see the contrast.

Ethanol by carbon: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂. Four of six carbons retained, so 0.667 C-mol/C-mol; mass yield 2(46.07)/180.16 = 0.511 g/g.

Ethanol by electrons: glucose carries 24 available electrons; ethanol carries 12 per mole (2 C × 6). 24/12 = 2 mol ethanol, identical. Electrons balance exactly with nothing to spare — the two CO₂ are released precisely because they carry carbon and no electrons, which is the signature of a redox-neutral fermentation. Neither constraint is slack, and that is why ethanol's theoretical yield is approached so closely in practice: there is no cofactor slack to waste.

Succinate by electrons: succinate (C₄H₆O₄) has γ = 3.50 per C-mol, so 14 electrons per mole. 24/14 = 1.71 mol per glucose. By carbon alone, glucose supplies only 6 carbons and succinate needs 4, so 1.5 mol. The electron ceiling exceeds the carbon ceiling — which is only reachable if extra carbon comes from CO₂ fixation, and that is exactly what PEP carboxylase does. With fixation the answer is 1.71 mol/mol, 1.12 g/g; without it, redox limits the route near 1.0.

The general rule to carry: when the electron ceiling exceeds the carbon ceiling, CO₂ fixation is the yield mechanism and your design must include a carboxylation. When the carbon ceiling is lower, attack the decarboxylations instead.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • redox-neutral fermentation with no biomass or maintenance
  • γ per C-mol as tabulated
  • a stoichiometric ceiling, so kinetics and tolerance are excluded

Common misconceptions

  • That a balance can be closed by scaling a measurement. Scaling discards the only information the discrepancy contains, which is that something is unmeasured.
  • That the degree-of-reduction ceiling is always the right answer. It ignores ATP and cofactor constraints, so the LP answer is often lower and the difference identifies the binding cofactor.
  • That carbon yield above 1 C-mol/C-mol is impossible. It requires CO₂ fixation and a product more oxidised than the substrate, which succinate from glucose satisfies.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Derive ethanol's 0.511 g/g twice and say what the agreement of the two routes tells you.

A. By carbon: 2 ethanol and 2 CO₂ per glucose, so 92.14/180.16 = 0.511 g/g. By electrons: glucose 24 available electrons, ethanol 12 each, so exactly 2 mol with nothing left over. The agreement means neither constraint is slack — the two CO₂ are released precisely to balance electrons — which is the signature of a redox-neutral fermentation and the reason real ethanol processes reach 90%+ of theoretical. Contrast a more reduced product such as butanol, where the electron ceiling falls below the carbon ceiling and the gap must be paid for.

Q. A fermentation closes to 88% on carbon and 103% on electrons. Diagnose and order the investigation.

A. Carbon short with electrons complete or slightly high implicates a carbon-bearing, electron-poor stream, overwhelmingly likely CO₂. Order by information per unit effort: integrate off-gas CO₂ first; then check volatile losses to the exhaust; then extend the analytical method for unmeasured soluble by-products; then verify biomass gravimetrically with a measured carbon content rather than an optical density correlation; then audit feed concentration, evaporation and sampling volumes. If closing carbon with measured CO₂ leaves electrons at 103%, that residual sits within the uncertainty of the assumed biomass formula and should be reported as such, not adjusted away.

Q. Rank titre, rate and yield for a commodity product, and name two circumstances that reverse the ranking.

A. Yield, then titre, then rate. Yield sets raw material cost, the largest operating line, and cannot be substituted by capital. Titre sets water load and downstream cost. Rate sets capital per kilogram and is the most substitutable. It reverses for a high-value product, where substrate is negligible and purification dominates, putting titre first; and for an existing plant, where capital is sunk and rate determines sellable output. It also reverses where titre falls below a separation's feasibility threshold, at which point titre is a constraint rather than an elasticity.
