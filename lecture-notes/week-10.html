The pentose phosphate pathway and the NADPH economy

Week content

The pathway that decides whether a reductive product is affordable. Most cofactor-driven yield failures trace back to this week, and the central skill is computing the glycolysis-to-pentose split required by a stated NADPH demand, together with the carbon that split costs.

Core ideas

1. Two branches with entirely different characters

The oxidative branch — glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, lactonase, 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase — is irreversible and generates 2 NADPH per glucose-6-phosphate while releasing one carbon as CO₂, leaving ribulose-5-phosphate. The non-oxidative branch — ribulose-5-phosphate epimerase and isomerase, then transketolase and transaldolase — is fully reversible and simply rearranges carbon skeletons without redox chemistry, interconverting pentoses with fructose-6-phosphate, erythrose-4-phosphate, sedoheptulose-7-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Reversibility is why the pathway can run in several directions depending on what the cell needs, and why its flux is impossible to determine from extracellular rates.

2. The carbon rearrangement, worth doing on paper once

Three pentoses, fifteen carbons, become two fructose-6-phosphate and one glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate: twelve plus three, fifteen carbons conserved. Transketolase moves two-carbon units, transaldolase moves three. Work this through by hand once, tracking individual carbons, then immediately encode the three reactions in your notebook and confirm the columns balance — the transketolase and transaldolase coefficients are the ones most often mistyped. Two things then become permanent: you will never again mis-state the stoichiometry, and you will understand why 1-¹³C glucose resolves the pathway's flux — because the oxidative branch specifically releases glucose C1 as CO₂ while glycolysis retains it and places it at C3 of pyruvate, giving distinguishable labelling downstream. That is the week 28 argument, and it is visible here.

3. Four flux modes, and the condition that selects each

Mode 1, NADPH only: oxidative branch runs, pentoses recycle completely to F6P and GAP, net result is glucose oxidised to CO₂ with NADPH produced. Mode 2, ribose only: non-oxidative branch runs in reverse from F6P and GAP to make R5P without any NADPH, used when nucleotide demand is high and reductant is not. Mode 3, both: oxidative branch runs and the pentose is used directly for biosynthesis. Mode 4, NADPH plus ATP: pentoses recycle through to pyruvate with glycolytic ATP generation. Knowing the modes lets you answer the question a model will put to you: what does the cell do when it needs ribose but not NADPH, or the reverse?

4. The carbon cost of NADPH, which is the week's central number

Two NADPH per decarboxylation means that supplying 2n NADPH costs n carbons. Twelve NADPH requires six glucose-6-phosphate cycled and releases six CO₂ — one whole glucose equivalent of carbon — even with perfect recycling of the pentoses, because the loss is intrinsic to the NADPH-generating step. This single calculation is why strains needing heavy NADPH acquire a transhydrogenase, an NADP-dependent GAPDH, or a non-decarboxylating route: each converts a carbon cost into an ATP or redox cost, which is usually cheaper. Compute this for your own target before reading any strain paper and you will know what the authors were fighting.

5. R5P and E4P: the pathway's other job

Ribose-5-phosphate is the nucleotide precursor, so every growing cell needs a flux of it proportional to its RNA and DNA synthesis — and RNA content scales with growth rate, so pentose demand rises with µ. Erythrose-4-phosphate, available only from the non-oxidative branch, is one of the two aromatic amino acid precursors with PEP. That makes the non-oxidative branch, and transketolase in particular, a standard overexpression target in aromatic strains: the shikimate pathway cannot run faster than E4P is supplied, however much you amplify the shikimate enzymes themselves.

6. The glutathione couple as a redox buffer

NADPH reduces oxidised glutathione (GSSG) to two GSH via glutathione reductase, and the GSH/GSSG couple is held at high ratio and high total concentration — millimolar — so it buffers the cell's reducing environment and absorbs oxidative insults. Two consequences. The thiol-reducing environment of the cytoplasm, which blocks disulfide formation from week 3, is maintained by this system at NADPH's expense. And oxidative stress consumes NADPH that your pathway wanted, which is one reason a producing strain under stress underperforms its NADPH budget.

Worked anchor

A product requires 14 NADPH per mole. What does supplying it from the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway cost, and what are the alternatives worth?

Two NADPH per G6P with one CO₂ released, so 14 NADPH needs 7 G6P cycled and releases 7 CO₂. The 7 ribulose-5-phosphate produced carry 35 carbons and recycle intact through the non-oxidative branch into F6P and triose phosphates, so the only carbon loss is those 7 decarboxylations — slightly more than one glucose equivalent.

Compare: a transhydrogenase supplying all 14 NADPH from NADH costs no carbon but spends proton-motive force for each, raising oxygen demand by roughly 14 × (H⁺ per NADPH) / (H⁺ per O) worth of extra respiration. An NADP-dependent GAPDH converts glycolytic NADH production directly to NADPH at no carbon and no PMF cost, but removes the NADH the respiratory chain was using for ATP, so the cell must make that ATP elsewhere.

The decision rule: if the process is carbon-limited and oxygen transfer has headroom, take the transhydrogenase. If oxygen transfer is the constraint — which week 49 will tell you — take the carbon loss instead. There is no context-free answer, and saying so is the correct answer.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • two NADPH per G6P through the oxidative branch
  • the non-oxidative branch recycles pentose carbon intact
  • transhydrogenase at 1 H⁺ per NADPH
  • no competing biosynthetic demand on the pentose pool

Common misconceptions

  • That recycling the pentoses recovers the lost carbon. The CO₂ is released at the dehydrogenase step and is gone; recycling conserves only the remaining 5 carbons of each pentose.
  • That the pentose pathway is 'the NADPH pathway'. It has four flux modes, one of which makes no NADPH at all, and labelling studies of glucose-limited aerobic E. coli put the oxidative branch at only about 35–60% of total NADPH supply, with NADP-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase supplying much of the rest. Quote that range rather than assuming the pathway dominates.
  • That amplifying shikimate enzymes raises aromatic flux. It usually does not, because E4P supply from the non-oxidative branch limits first — which is why transketolase is the standard target.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. State the oxidative branch stoichiometry and compute the carbon cost of 10 NADPH.

A. G6P + 2 NADP⁺ + H₂O → ribulose-5-phosphate + CO₂ + 2 NADPH + 2 H⁺. Ten NADPH requires 5 G6P cycled and releases 5 CO₂, so five carbons — a little under one glucose equivalent. The five pentoses (25 C) recycle intact to F6P and triose phosphates, so no further carbon is lost.

Q. Why can extracellular rates never reveal the pentose phosphate split ratio?

A. Carbon entering the oxidative branch returns to glycolysis as fructose-6-phosphate and triose phosphate, so the cycle has no distinct external signature apart from CO₂, which has several other sources. The cycle flux lies in the null space of the external stoichiometry: it can take any value without changing any measurable uptake or excretion rate. Resolving it requires labelling, because the oxidative branch releases glucose C1 as CO₂ while glycolysis retains it, giving different downstream mass isotopomer distributions.

Q. An aromatic amino acid strain has ample shikimate pathway expression and low titre. What do you suspect and what do you amplify?

A. Precursor limitation rather than pathway capacity: the shikimate route needs erythrose-4-phosphate plus PEP, and E4P comes only from the non-oxidative pentose phosphate branch. Amplify transketolase, and consider transaldolase, to raise E4P supply; address PEP availability separately by replacing PTS uptake with a permease plus glucokinase, or by attenuating pyruvate kinase. Confirm the diagnosis by measuring intracellular E4P and PEP before building anything.
