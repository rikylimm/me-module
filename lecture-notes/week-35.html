Genome editing: recombineering, CRISPR and multiplexing

Week content

Chromosomal engineering at scale. Every editing method persuades a repair pathway to do something useful, so knowing which repair biology each depends on predicts which will work in a host nobody has edited before — and predicts, correctly, that most published protocols do not transfer. The week ends in an editing plan, which means a biosafety section as much as guides.

Core ideas

1. The repair pathway available decides the method

A break is repaired by homologous recombination, which uses a donor and is precise, by non-homologous end joining, which is error-prone and needs none, or by microhomology-mediated end joining in between (Lee §10.1). Most bacteria have little or no end joining, so an unrepaired break is lethal — which is why Cas9 in a bacterium is a counter-selection against unedited cells rather than a mutagen, and why a donor must be supplied. Eukaryotes have the opposite problem: end joining dominates and competes with the donor, so knockouts come easily and precise edits do not; Lee §10.1 puts homologous integration in mammalian cells at about 1 in 1,000 transfected cells.

2. Recombineering makes a bacterium recombinogenic, and counter-selection makes the edit markerless

Gam blocks the exonuclease that would destroy linear DNA, Exo resects one strand, Beta anneals it at the replication fork. Datsenko and Wanner (PNAS 97:6640, 2000) showed PCR products carrying only 36–50 nt of homology suffice, and that the cassette can then be removed by FLP acting on flanking FRT sites; oligonucleotide recombineering needs Beta alone and no selection, so its limit is detection. Without a nuclease the markerless route is two selected events: the cassette integrates under positive selection, then excises under a counter-selection — sacB on sucrose, rpsL in a streptomycin-resistant host, upp on 5-fluorouracil, manP on mannose, or an I-SceI site. Lee §13.4.1 reports deletions up to 78 kb this way, and the catch: each system needs its own host background.

3. CRISPR-Cas9 is guide design plus a repair template, and its efficiency is published

Cas9 is targeted by a 20-nt spacer to a protospacer adjacent to an NGG motif, and the PAM is a hard requirement: a target without one nearby needs an orthologue with a different PAM. Lee's Bacillus survey (§13.4.2–13.4.3) gives the realistic range — 33–53% in the first report, 90–97% once promoters and delivery were optimised, 80% for a 38 kb deletion, 69% for point mutations, 49–65% for three loci at once. The nickase appears there at both 93–97% and 53%: efficiency belongs to the whole system, not the nuclease.

4. For an essential gene, knockdown beats knockout and can be graded

Deleting an essential gene gives no colony or a suppressor, and either way carries little information; dCas9 blocking transcription gives a dial. In the genome-wide B. subtilis essential-gene CRISPRi library (Lee §13.4.3.2) repression of a chromosomal reporter was titratable with xylose from 3-fold to 150-fold, and eight genes were knocked down together at 140-fold or more in exponential growth. That is what week 25's essentiality analysis wants: a dose-response through the essential range, not a binary verdict.

5. Base and prime editing avoid the break; MAGE embraces the numbers

A deaminase fused to dCas9 or a nickase converts C to T or A to G in a small window with no donor and no break, at up to 100% in Lee §13.4.3.3. Prime editing writes a templated edit from a reverse transcriptase primed by the guide itself, at 20–50% efficiency with 1–10% indels in human cells (Anzalone et al., Nature 576:149, 2019), with no established bacterial workhorse. MAGE goes the other way, cycling oligonucleotide recombineering across many loci at once: over 4 × 10⁹ variants a day (Wang et al., Nature 460, 2009, doi:10.1038/nature08187).

6. Tool transfer fails on homology length, transformation efficiency and mismatch repair

The sharpest number in the week's reading: λ-Red needs about 50 bp of homology in E. coli and roughly 800–1,000 bp in Lactococcus lactis (Lee §15.2.3.3), which alone turns a primer-tail protocol into a cloning project. Lactic acid bacteria are also not natively recombinogenic, so a Beta homologue such as RecT must be expressed and its best variant found empirically, and mismatch repair differs between strains. Transformation efficiency is usually binding, which is why week 33's chassis work comes first.

Worked anchor

The same 1.2 kb deletion, two ways, then the multiplex arithmetic.

Recombineering with counter-selection. Two 70-mers with 50 nt arms amplify the cassette; the λ-Red plasmid is induced and the cells electroporated; transformants are selected and checked by colony PCR; then the plasmid is cured, FLP expressed, the excision counter-selected and the junction sequenced. Hands-on about 5 days, calendar 7–10, a small screening burden because the first step is positively selected, and an FRT scar in the product.

CRISPR-Cas9. A PAM check, three guides cloned by Golden Gate and a donor with two 500–1,000 bp arms; transform and induce; screen 8 colonies, which suffices at Lee's 80–97% for a deletion of this kind. Calendar 5–7 days, scarless. The difference is the donor — 1–2 kb of arms to build against 100 nt of primer tail, traded for no marker, no curing and no scar.

So the decision is iteration count. One deletion: either. Three interventions in sequence: CRISPR, because marker and scar do not accumulate. A host with poor transformation: recombineering, because positive selection finds a rare event and screening does not.

MAGE diversity. Twenty-four sites at 0.30 replacement per cycle: a site is converted with probability 1 − 0.70ⁿ, so 0.76 at four cycles and 0.97 at ten, over a space of 2²⁴ = 1.7 × 10⁷ genotypes. Ten cycles put the expected conversions per cell at 23.3, piling the population into the fully converted corner, while four put the mean at 18.2 with a spread of about ±2.1 and sample the interior. At 10⁹ cells the space is covered many times over either way, so the design variable is where in it the library sits, not how large it is.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • per-site allelic replacement of 0.30 per MAGE cycle, equal at every site
  • sites independent and treated as binary, converted or not
  • editing efficiencies taken from Lee's Bacillus survey and assumed to transfer to this target
  • homology arm lengths as published for the host in question
  • hands-on and calendar days exclude ordering and queueing time

Common misconceptions

  • That CRISPR edits a bacterial genome. In most bacteria it kills the cells whose genome was not edited, because they cannot repair the cut; the donor and homologous recombination do the editing.
  • That a knockout tells you what an essential gene does. It tells you the cell dies or finds a suppressor. The informative experiment is a graded CRISPRi series through the essential range.
  • That a protocol working in E. coli needs only optimisation elsewhere. λ-Red needs about 50 bp of homology in E. coli and 800–1,000 bp in Lactococcus lactis: a different experiment, not a tuned one.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. When would you replace a native promoter rather than knock down with CRISPRi, and what do you lose?

A. When the change must be permanent and heritable without an inducer, in a production strain where a dCas9 cassette and its inducer are liabilities; replacement also avoids polar effects across an operon. What you lose is the dial: CRISPRi gives a dose-response from one construct, 3- to 150-fold in the Bacillus library, whereas each replacement is one point on it. So CRISPRi to find the level, replacement to fix it.

Q. A published CRISPR protocol gives 90% editing in E. coli and nothing in your host. Name the four things you check, in order.

A. Transformation efficiency first, since everything downstream is conditional on delivery. Then homology arm length against the host's own requirement, 50 bp in E. coli against 800–1,000 bp elsewhere. Then Cas9 expression and toxicity. Then repair capacity: any end joining, and what mismatch repair does to a single-stranded donor.

Q. Design the verification for a markerless deletion and say what each step cannot catch.

A. Colony PCR with primers outside both arms gives a size shift and excludes the wild-type allele, but shows a mixed population only if both products amplify comparably. Sequencing the junction catches scars and indels there and nothing elsewhere; a PCR against the delivery plasmid confirms curing. Only whole-genome sequencing catches off-target cuts, secondary recombination and suppressors picked up during selection.
