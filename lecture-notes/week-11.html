TCA cycle, anaplerosis and the glyoxylate shunt

Week content

The branch point that decides whether carbon becomes product, biomass or CO₂. Most C4 product designs live or die on the anaplerotic reactions studied this week, and the host differences at that node are the single clearest case in the course of biology dictating a host choice.

Core ideas

1. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: the committed, irreversible gateway

Three enzymes in one complex: E1 decarboxylates pyruvate onto thiamine pyrophosphate, E2 transfers the acetyl group to lipoamide and then to coenzyme A, E3 reoxidises the lipoamide using FAD and then NAD⁺. The result is acetyl-CoA, CO₂ and NADH, and it is effectively irreversible — which is why acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate and why the carbon lost here cannot be recovered without a carboxylation elsewhere. The complex is inhibited by its own products, acetyl-CoA and NADH, so a cell with high reducing power throttles entry to the cycle. Note the oxygen sensitivity of the anaerobic alternative, pyruvate formate lyase, which produces formate rather than CO₂ and NADH — a different carbon and redox outcome from the same node, and the basis of week 13.

2. Eight reactions, two decarboxylations, and the real yield

Per acetyl-CoA entering: 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP and 2 CO₂. The two decarboxylations are at isocitrate dehydrogenase and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase, and they are what make the cycle a carbon-destroying machine in the service of energy production. Regulation sits at citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase, all responding to energy charge and NADH. For engineering, learn which intermediates are drawn off for biosynthesis — oxaloacetate for aspartate and the aspartate family, 2-oxoglutarate for glutamate and the glutamate family, succinyl-CoA for porphyrins and for lysine via one route — because every carbon drawn off must be replaced.

3. Anaplerosis is replacement, and the node is where host choice is decided

Drawing intermediates off the cycle depletes it, so carbon must be put back: that is anaplerosis. The PEP-pyruvate-oxaloacetate node in E. coli has four reactions. PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ onto PEP to give oxaloacetate, using PEP's own phosphate potential and needing no ATP or biotin. PEP carboxykinase runs the reverse, consuming oxaloacetate and making ATP, and is gluconeogenic. Malic enzyme decarboxylates malate to pyruvate with NAD or NADP reduction, and is cataplerotic. Pyruvate kinase connects PEP to pyruvate. Now the crucial difference: E. coli lacks pyruvate carboxylase, while C. glutamicum and S. cerevisiae have it. Since pyruvate carboxylase fixes CO₂ onto pyruvate rather than PEP, it does not compete with the PTS or with aromatic pathways for PEP — which is a substantial part of why C. glutamicum dominates lysine and why it is attractive for C4 diacids.

4. The glyoxylate shunt: the bypass that conserves carbon

Isocitrate lyase cleaves isocitrate to succinate and glyoxylate; malate synthase condenses glyoxylate with a second acetyl-CoA to give malate. The net effect is to convert two acetyl-CoA into one C4 without either decarboxylation, which is what makes net biosynthesis from acetate or fatty acids possible — without it, growth on acetate is impossible, because every acetyl-CoA entering the cycle would be fully oxidised. For engineering, it is a recurring target for C4 products precisely because it conserves the carbon the cycle would release. It is tightly regulated, typically by phosphorylation of isocitrate dehydrogenase to divert flux, and derepressing it is a standard intervention.

5. Two routes to succinate, and why the reductive one wins

Oxidatively, succinate is four steps from citrate through two decarboxylations, so carbon is lost and the yield is poor. Reductively, oxaloacetate is reduced through malate and fumarate to succinate, fixing CO₂ at the carboxylation step and consuming NADH rather than producing it. Since succinate (γ = 3.50 per C-mol) is more oxidised than glucose (4.00), electrons are in surplus and can pay for fixing an extra fully oxidised carbon — which is why the reductive route has a stoichiometric ceiling of 1.71 mol/mol against roughly 1.0 for the oxidative, and why every serious succinate strain uses carboxylation. The general lesson, which recurs in week 17: for products more oxidised than the substrate, CO₂ fixation is the yield mechanism rather than an environmental talking point.

6. Where the cycle is not a cycle

Under anaerobic or strongly fermentative conditions, many bacteria run the TCA as two branches rather than a cycle: an oxidative arm from citrate to 2-oxoglutarate supplying glutamate, and a reductive arm from oxaloacetate to succinate acting as an electron sink. 2-Oxoglutarate dehydrogenase is often repressed, breaking the ring. This matters because a model with a closed cycle will predict fluxes an anaerobic cell cannot carry, and because the reductive arm is exactly the route a succinate strain exploits. When reading a flux map, check first whether the cycle is closed under the condition reported.

Worked anchor

Compute the maximum anaerobic succinate yield from glucose, with and without CO₂ fixation, and explain the difference.

Without fixation, through the oxidative route: glycolysis gives 2 PEP and 2 NADH; reducing oxaloacetate to succinate needs 2 NADH per succinate, so 2 succinate would need 4 NADH while glycolysis supplies 2. The redox balance caps the route near 1.0 mol/mol, with an oxidised co-product such as acetate acting as the electron sink for the shortfall.

With PEP carboxylase fixing one CO₂ per succinate and the reductive arm carrying the flux, the classical ceiling is 1.71 mol succinate per mol glucose, equal to 1.12 g/g.

Why the surplus exists: glucose carries 24 available electrons per mole; succinate carries 14 per mole. 24/14 = 1.71. Succinate being more oxidised than glucose means the electron budget permits more product than the carbon budget of glucose alone would allow — provided the extra carbon comes from CO₂.

Real strains reach 1.3–1.6 mol/mol, because reaching the ceiling needs either a supplementary reductant, a dual-phase aerobic-anaerobic process, or a hydrogen-rich co-substrate, and because the carboxylase must compete kinetically with pyruvate kinase for PEP. That competition is why PEP carboxylase overexpression and pyruvate kinase attenuation appear together in so many designs.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • anaerobic, with no external electron acceptor
  • redox balance closed on the stated products only
  • CO₂ freely available for fixation
  • no maintenance or biomass demand

Common misconceptions

  • That the TCA cycle is always a cycle. Under anaerobic conditions it frequently runs as two branches with 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase repressed, and a model assuming closure will overpredict.
  • That anaplerosis is a detail of cofactor bookkeeping. It is the specific reaction set that decides whether a C4 product is feasible in a host, and E. coli's lack of pyruvate carboxylase is a genuine disadvantage for that product class.
  • That the glyoxylate shunt makes energy. It conserves carbon at the cost of the NADH and FADH₂ the bypassed steps would have made, which is exactly the trade you want for a product and exactly the wrong trade for growth.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Name the four reactions of the PEP-pyruvate-oxaloacetate node in E. coli, their directions, and the host difference that matters.

A. PEP carboxylase: PEP + CO₂ → oxaloacetate, anaplerotic, no ATP or biotin needed. PEP carboxykinase: oxaloacetate + ATP → PEP + CO₂, gluconeogenic. Malic enzyme: malate → pyruvate + CO₂ with NAD or NADP reduction, cataplerotic. Pyruvate kinase: PEP → pyruvate + ATP. The difference: E. coli lacks pyruvate carboxylase, which C. glutamicum and S. cerevisiae possess, and which carboxylates pyruvate rather than PEP — so it does not compete with the PTS or with aromatic biosynthesis for PEP.

Q. Why is growth on acetate impossible without the glyoxylate shunt?

A. Acetate enters metabolism only as acetyl-CoA, which in the ordinary cycle is fully oxidised to two CO₂ per turn, so no net carbon is available for biosynthesis — every carbon that enters leaves as CO₂. The glyoxylate shunt bypasses both decarboxylations, converting two acetyl-CoA into one C4 compound and so permitting net synthesis of the precursors the cell needs. Growth on fatty acids has the same requirement for the same reason.

Q. Why does an E. coli succinate strain need a carboxylating reaction, and which carboxylase would you pick in each host?

A. Because succinate is more oxidised than glucose, the electron budget allows more product than glucose's carbon alone supports — but only if the extra carbon comes from CO₂, raising the ceiling from about 1.0 to 1.71 mol/mol. In E. coli the available option is PEP carboxylase, usually overexpressed and often paired with pyruvate kinase attenuation so the carboxylase wins the competition for PEP; a heterologous pyruvate carboxylase is an alternative that avoids that competition. In C. glutamicum or yeast, use the native pyruvate carboxylase for exactly that reason.
