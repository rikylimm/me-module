Enzyme kinetics I

Week content

Your own reaction engineering, re-pointed. Michaelis-Menten is the quasi-steady-state approximation you already derive; the new material is knowing where it fails, fitting it honestly, and understanding which parameters a given experiment can and cannot identify. This week and Brief 1 together are where your quantitative credibility is established.

Core ideas

1. Derive it, do not memorise it

For E + S ⇌ ES → E + P with rate constants k₁, k₋₁ and kcat: set d[ES]/dt = k₁[E][S] − (k₋₁ + kcat)[ES] ≈ 0, with [E]T = [E] + [ES]. Substituting gives [ES] = [E]T[S]/(KM + [S]) where KM = (k₋₁ + kcat)/k₁, and v = kcat[ES] = Vmax[S]/(KM + [S]). Note what KM is and is not: it is a ratio of rate constants, equal to the dissociation constant only in the rapid-equilibrium limit where kcat ≪ k₋₁. Reporting KM as 'the affinity' is sloppy and occasionally wrong.

2. What each parameter means physically

Vmax = kcat[E]T, so it scales with how much enzyme you express and tells you nothing intrinsic. kcat is the turnover number, the rate at saturation per active site. KM is the substrate concentration at half-maximal rate, and tells you the regime the enzyme operates in: compare it with the intracellular concentration from week 27 and you know whether the step is substrate-sensitive. kcat/KM is the apparent second-order rate constant for free enzyme and free substrate, governs the rate at low substrate — which is the regime of most intracellular metabolites — and is the quantity under selection for substrate discrimination. Its ceiling is the diffusion limit, about 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹.

3. Where the QSSA fails inside a cell

Two conditions. First, enzyme concentration comparable to substrate concentration: for low-abundance metabolites this is common, the free-substrate assumption breaks, and you need the total QSSA. Second, substrate changing on the ES relaxation timescale — during a rapid transient, or in the instationary labelling experiments of week 29. A third issue is not a QSSA failure but matters more: the irreversible form is simply wrong near equilibrium, and most central-metabolic reactions operate near equilibrium. Use the reversible rate law with the Haldane relation, which ties the kinetic parameters to Keq and keeps the model thermodynamically consistent. This is a requirement in week 30, not an elegance.

4. Most metabolic enzymes have two substrates, and the mechanisms are distinguishable

Single-substrate kinetics is the exception; a dehydrogenase has a substrate and a cofactor, a kinase has a substrate and ATP. Three mechanism classes. Sequential ordered: both substrates bind before any product leaves, in a fixed order — typical of NAD-dependent dehydrogenases, where the cofactor binds first. Sequential random: both bind before catalysis but in either order. Ping-pong: the first substrate binds, a product leaves carrying a group onto the enzyme, then the second substrate binds — transaminases are the standard case, with the amino group parked on pyridoxal phosphate between half-reactions. Why you care: the mechanism determines whether one substrate's concentration affects the other's apparent KM, and therefore whether raising a cofactor pool will raise flux. For a kinetic model in week 30, assuming ordered kinetics for a ping-pong enzyme gives qualitatively wrong cofactor sensitivity.

5. The reversible rate law and the Haldane relation, in the form you will type

Write these at the top of every kinetic notebook, because prose descriptions of them are useless at the keyboard. For a one-substrate, one-product reversible step:

v = ( (Vf/K_S)·[S] − (Vr/K_P)·[P] ) / ( 1 + [S]/K_S + [P]/K_P )

where Vf and Vr are the forward and reverse maximal rates and K_S and K_P the respective Michaelis constants. The Haldane relation ties them to the equilibrium constant:

K'eq = (Vf · K_P) / (Vr · K_S)

The consequence is the reason this matters. Since ΔrG′° = −RT ln K′eq, the thermodynamics fixes K′eq, which fixes one combination of the four kinetic parameters — so only three are independent. Fit all four freely and you will produce a model that implies an equilibrium constant contradicting measured thermodynamics, which is unphysical however well it fits. In practice: fit three parameters and compute the fourth, or parameterise directly in terms of K′eq. At equilibrium, setting v = 0 recovers [P]/[S] = K′eq, which is the one-line check that your implementation is correct — run it before trusting any simulation.

6. The four inhibition mechanisms, read off the data

Competitive: inhibitor binds free enzyme, apparent KM rises by (1 + [I]/Ki), Vmax unchanged; raising substrate relieves it. Uncompetitive: inhibitor binds only ES, both apparent Vmax and KM fall by the same factor so Vmax/KM is unchanged; raising substrate makes it worse. Non-competitive: binds both equally, Vmax falls, KM unchanged. Mixed: binds both with different affinities, both parameters change by different factors. The engineering reading matters more than the taxonomy: a competitively inhibited step can be relieved by increasing its substrate pool, an intervention available to you; an uncompetitively inhibited one cannot, so it is the harder bottleneck.

7. Fit properly, and know what you cannot determine

The double-reciprocal plot distorts the error structure: it compresses high-substrate points and enormously inflates the leverage of the low-substrate points, which are the noisiest. Unweighted regression on transformed data therefore gives biased parameters and dishonest intervals. Fit the untransformed data by weighted non-linear least squares with an error model you have stated, and report confidence intervals. On identifiability: Vmax is well determined if your highest substrate concentrations approach saturation; KM is inferred from the curvature and is correlated with Vmax, so it needs points near KM itself. Design the titration by where the model is sensitive to the parameter you care about — a D-optimal argument you already know.

8. Reading a double-reciprocal plot — for diagnosis, never for fitting

The replot patterns are heavily examined and are worth knowing cold, provided you keep the distinction: read the pattern to identify a mechanism, then fit the untransformed data to get the numbers.

On a 1/v against 1/[S] plot, with lines at several inhibitor concentrations: • Competitive — lines intersect on the y-axis. Slope rises with [I], y-intercept (1/Vmax) unchanged, x-intercept moves toward the origin. • Uncompetitive — parallel lines. Slope unchanged, y-intercept rises with [I]. • Non-competitive (pure) — lines intersect on the x-axis. Slope and y-intercept both rise, x-intercept (−1/KM) unchanged. • Mixed — lines intersect to the left of the y-axis, above or below the x-axis depending on the relative affinities for E and ES.

Secondary replots, which is where the examiner usually goes next: plot slope against [I] and the x-intercept gives −Ki for competitive and mixed inhibition; plot y-intercept against [I] and the x-intercept gives −Ki′, the ES-binding constant, for uncompetitive and mixed. For pure non-competitive the two are equal. For multi-substrate mechanisms the same plot distinguishes classes: ping-pong gives parallel lines when one substrate is varied at fixed concentrations of the other, while both sequential mechanisms give intersecting lines — which is the single most common multi-substrate examination question.

9. Diffusion and immobilisation distort everything above

Shuler's immobilised-enzyme sections introduce what week 48 develops: when the catalyst is in a particle, the interior sees less substrate than the bulk, so a Michaelis-Menten fit to bulk concentration returns an apparent KM that is transport-contaminated and larger than the intrinsic value. The effectiveness factor quantifies the penalty, and the Thiele modulus tells you the regime. You already know this machinery from heterogeneous catalysis; the only new thing is that the kinetics inside is saturable rather than power-law.

Worked anchor

An enzyme has kcat 85 s⁻¹ and KM 40 µM; its substrate sits at 15 µM in vivo.

Fraction of Vmax: v/Vmax = S/(KM + S) = 15/55 = 0.273, so 27% of capacity. Per-enzyme rate: 85 × 0.273 = 23.2 s⁻¹. To carry 1.5 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹: flux per cell = 1.5 × 3×10⁻¹³ = 4.5×10⁻¹³ mmol/h = 7.5×10⁴ molecules/s, so copies = 7.5×10⁴/23.2 ≈ 3,200.

Now the interpretation, which is the part that matters. The enzyme is far from saturation, so its flux is strongly substrate-sensitive — a two-fold rise in substrate gives nearly a two-fold rise in rate. That makes it a candidate for control, and it also means this step's flux can be raised by pushing its substrate pool rather than by expressing more enzyme. Contrast with an enzyme at 94% saturation, where expressing more is the only lever. Week 31's control analysis formalises this intuition.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • irreversible Michaelis-Menten, no product or feedback inhibition
  • one substrate limiting, the rest saturating
  • in vivo kcat equals the in vitro value
  • 3×10⁻¹³ gDW per cell

Common misconceptions

  • That KM is a dissociation constant. It equals Kd only when kcat ≪ k₋₁; otherwise it is a kinetic quantity.
  • That a Lineweaver-Burk plot is just a presentational choice. Fitting on it biases the parameters, measurably, which Brief 1 asks you to quantify.
  • That irreversible Michaelis-Menten is adequate for metabolism. Most central reactions run near equilibrium, where the irreversible form overestimates flux and violates thermodynamics.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Derive Michaelis-Menten from the QSSA, then state the two in vivo conditions under which it fails.

A. Set d[ES]/dt = k₁[E][S] − (k₋₁+kcat)[ES] = 0 with [E]T = [E]+[ES]; substituting gives [ES] = [E]T[S]/(KM+[S]) with KM = (k₋₁+kcat)/k₁, and v = kcat[ES]. It fails when enzyme concentration is comparable to substrate concentration, common for low-abundance metabolites, requiring the total QSSA; and when substrate changes on the ES relaxation timescale, as in a fast transient or instationary labelling. Separately, the irreversible form is simply wrong near equilibrium, which is where most central-metabolic reactions sit.

Q. Apparent Vmax and KM both fall threefold at 50 µM inhibitor. Which mechanism, what is Ki, and why does more substrate not help?

A. Uncompetitive: both parameters fall by the same factor, so Vmax/KM is unchanged, and on a double-reciprocal plot the lines are parallel. The factor is (1 + [I]/Ki′) = 3, so Ki′ = 25 µM. Raising substrate increases the concentration of ES, which is precisely the species the inhibitor binds, so the inhibition deepens — the opposite of the competitive case and the reason this is the harder bottleneck to engineer around.

Q. You can add three data points to a titration. Where do you put them if KM is the parameter you need, and why?

A. Clustered around [S] ≈ KM, where the curvature carries the information about KM and where the model's sensitivity to it is greatest, with at least one point well above saturation retained to pin Vmax — since the two parameters are correlated, a poorly determined Vmax degrades KM. If the error is absolute rather than relative, shift the cluster upward because the low-substrate points are then relatively noisier. This is a D-optimal design argument: put points where the derivative of the response with respect to the uncertain parameter is largest.
