Respiration, electron transport and aeration

Week content

Where biology meets your mass transfer course. The oxygen demand you compute this week becomes the kLa calculation in week 49 and the vessel cost in week 52, and the chain's branching is why a single P/O number was never going to be enough.

Core ideas

1. E. coli's respiratory chain branches at both ends, and that is the point

Entry: NADH dehydrogenase I (Nuo), translocating roughly 4 H⁺ per 2 e⁻, or NADH dehydrogenase II (Ndh), translocating none and simply passing electrons to the quinone pool. Exit: cytochrome bo₃, low oxygen affinity, separating 4 charge equivalents per 2 e⁻ — two pumped and two scalar, which is why its BiGG reaction is named CYTBO3_4pp — or cytochrome bd, high oxygen affinity, separating 2 by the scalar route alone. Combine the options and the equivalents per NADH range from 8 down to 2, so with E. coli's c₁₀ ATP synthase at 4.3 H⁺ per ATP the effective P/O spans roughly 1.9 down to 0.5. The cell chooses by condition: high oxygen favours the efficient combination, low oxygen forces the high-affinity bd oxidase. A model with a fixed P/O therefore mispredicts systematically at low dissolved oxygen, and that is a real source of error in microaerobic process modelling.

2. Anaerobic respiration, ranked by what it is worth

With oxygen absent, many bacteria respire on alternative terminal acceptors, and the ATP yield follows the reduction potential. Nitrate is the best, close enough to oxygen to support substantial growth; then DMSO and TMAO; then fumarate, which is the weakest and which doubles as the reductive TCA arm from week 11. Note the regulatory hierarchy: cells express these in order of preference, so a medium containing nitrate will suppress fumarate respiration. For engineering, this matters in two ways — an unintended electron acceptor in your medium can break a growth-coupled design by giving the cell an escape route, and deliberately supplying one is a way to control redox state.

3. From electron balance to oxygen demand, which is the week's transferable skill

Oxygen uptake is not an independent parameter to be looked up; it follows from the electron balance. Electrons entering as substrate must leave as product, as biomass, or as water via the respiratory chain. So: available electrons in substrate consumed, minus those in biomass formed, minus those in products formed, divided by four, gives moles of oxygen. Do it this way and the number is consistent with your yields by construction, which is the whole point. Working in degrees of reduction per C-mol — glucose 4.00, biomass 4.20, acetate 4.00, ethanol 6.00, succinate 3.50 — makes the arithmetic routine, and week 17 formalises it.

4. Oxygen as a substrate with almost no solubility

Air-saturated water at 30 °C holds about 0.21 mM dissolved oxygen, roughly 7 mg/L. A dense culture consumes that in a few seconds, which is why oxygen must be supplied continuously and why it is the usual limiting nutrient in aerobic fermentation. At steady state OTR = kLa(C − C) must equal OUR. The critical dissolved oxygen concentration — below which the organism's respiration becomes oxygen-limited rather than substrate-limited — is typically 1–5% of saturation for bacteria, which is low enough that a setpoint of 20–30% gives comfortable margin. The consequence to carry forward: raising C by oxygen enrichment or head pressure is usually cheaper than raising kLa by agitation, because power input scales steeply and brings shear and heat penalties with it.

5. Microaerobic operation as a deliberate choice

Some products are made best at low dissolved oxygen, because the cell's product spectrum shifts with redox state: restricting oxygen raises NADH, which drives reductive routes that are unavailable to a fully respiring cell. This is used industrially for several reduced products, and it changes the process problem from supplying oxygen to controlling it precisely at a low setpoint, which is harder. The trade is that growth and ATP yield fall, so titre and rate suffer while yield on the reduced product rises — the titre-rate-yield triangle appearing in its most physical form.

6. Heat, which is the constraint people forget

Roughly 460 kJ is released per mole of oxygen consumed, nearly independent of substrate, which makes oxygen uptake a direct proxy for heat generation. At an OUR of 100 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ that is about 13 kW per cubic metre, so a 50 m³ vessel generates several hundred kilowatts of heat requiring removal through a jacket or coils whose area scales as volume to the two-thirds. That is why large aerobic fermenters are sometimes heat-limited rather than oxygen-limited, and why the energy balance from Doran Ch 5 belongs in a process design rather than only in an examination.

Worked anchor

Compute the oxygen demand of a producing strain from first principles, then the kLa it requires.

A strain grows at µ = 0.25 h⁻¹ with YX/S = 0.45 g/g on glucose and makes acetate at 0.20 g per g glucose. Biomass is CH₁.₈O₀.₅N₀.₂.

Per gram of glucose: available electrons in = 0.133 mol e⁻ (24 per mole, 180 g/mol). Biomass at γ = 4.2 per C-mol and 24.6 g per C-mol carries 0.171 mol e⁻ per gram, so 0.45 g carries 0.077. Acetate at γ = 4.0 and 30 g per C-mol carries 0.133 per gram, so 0.20 g carries 0.027. To oxygen: 0.133 − 0.077 − 0.027 = 0.029 mol e⁻, and oxygen accepts 4, so 0.0073 mol O₂ per g glucose.

Substrate uptake qS = µ/YX/S = 0.25/0.45 = 0.56 g gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹, so qO₂ = 0.0073 × 0.56 × 1000 = 4.1 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹.

At 40 gDW/L: OUR = 164 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹. Required kLa = OUR/(C* − C) = 164/(0.21 − 0.04) = 965 h⁻¹.

That is comfortable at bench scale and at or beyond the limit of a large production vessel — which is precisely the conflict week 49 designs around, and the reason the answer to 'can we scale this' is often decided in week 12 rather than in the fermentation hall.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • biomass CH₁.₈O₀.₅N₀.₂ at 24.6 g per C-mol and γ = 4.2
  • the electron balance closes on biomass, acetate and oxygen alone
  • steady state, no accumulation of intermediates
  • the kLa correlation applies at this scale and broth viscosity

Common misconceptions

  • That P/O is a constant. In E. coli it varies four-fold, from about 2.4 to 0.6, with which dehydrogenase and which oxidase are expressed, and those shift with dissolved oxygen.
  • That you look up qO₂. It follows from the electron balance given your yields, and computing it that way guarantees internal consistency — looking it up guarantees the opposite.
  • That oxygen limitation is only about supply. The critical dissolved oxygen concentration is strain-specific and often very low, so a culture at 2% saturation may be perfectly unlimited while one at 10% with poor mixing is not.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Why does E. coli switch terminal oxidase at low oxygen, and what does that do to ATP yield?

A. Cytochrome bo₃ has lower oxygen affinity and separates 4 charge equivalents per 2 e⁻; cytochrome bd has much higher affinity and separates 2. At low dissolved oxygen the cell switches to bd in order to keep respiring at all, accepting half the charge separation per electron pair and therefore a lower effective P/O and lower ATP yield per glucose. This is one reason microaerobic operation shifts the product spectrum, and one reason a fixed-P/O model mispredicts at low oxygen.

Q. A strain at 35 gDW/L has qO₂ = 5 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹. What kLa is needed at C* = 0.21 mM and a 0.04 mM setpoint, and is it feasible at 30 m³?

A. OUR = 5 × 35 = 175 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹; kLa = 175/0.17 = 1,029 h⁻¹. A large stirred tank typically delivers 200–500 h⁻¹ before power and shear become limiting, so this is not feasible as stated. Remedies: oxygen enrichment or head pressure to raise C, a lower setpoint if the critical oxygen concentration permits, or a lower specific demand by capping growth rate through the feed — and raising C is usually cheapest because power scales steeply.

Q. How much cooling does that fermentation need per cubic metre?

A. About 460 kJ per mole of oxygen consumed. At 175 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ = 175 mol m⁻³ h⁻¹, heat generation is 80.5 MJ m⁻³ h⁻¹ = 22 kW per cubic metre. For 30 m³ that is 670 kW of cooling duty, through a heat transfer area that scales only as volume to the two-thirds — which is why large aerobic fermenters are sometimes heat-limited before they are oxygen-limited.
