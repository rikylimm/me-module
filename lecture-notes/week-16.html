Biomass composition and the biomass equation

Week content

Biomass is a product with a formula. Until you can write that formula from composition data you cannot write an honest yield, a biomass objective function, or a defensible maximum titre. This week converts the biosynthesis of week 14 into coefficients, and it produces the single most reused artifact of phase 3.

Core ideas

1. The elemental formula and what it is for

E. coli biomass is conventionally written CH₁.₈O₀.₅N₀.₂, sometimes with phosphorus and sulfur appended, and a C-mol of it weighs about 24.6 g including ash. Its degree of reduction is γ = 4 + 1.8 − 2(0.5) − 3(0.2) = 4.2 per C-mol. Note how close that is to glucose at 4.00: aerobic growth on glucose is nearly redox-neutral before any product forms, which is why the oxygen demand in such a culture is modest and why making a reduced product changes the oxygen picture so sharply. The formula varies between organisms and with growth condition by perhaps ±10% in each coefficient, which propagates into every electron balance you compute — so quote it with its source and treat closures to better than a few per cent with suspicion.

2. From macromolecular composition to precursor demand

The conversion is mechanical and you should do it once by hand. Take a composition — for fast-growing E. coli roughly 55% protein, 20% RNA, 3% DNA, 9% lipid, 6% murein, 5% glycogen, 2% other by dry weight. For protein: 0.55 g per gDW divided by an average residue mass near 110 g/mol gives about 5.0 mmol of amino acid residues per gDW, which you then distribute across the twenty amino acids by their measured abundance and trace back to the twelve precursors using the week 14 tree. Repeat for RNA at about 330 g/mol per nucleotide, giving 0.6 mmol, and for lipid and murein similarly. The output is a precursor demand vector in mmol per gDW, and that vector is the biomass reaction's left-hand side.

3. The energy cost, and why the measured number exceeds the computed one

Polymerisation alone: 4 ATP-equivalents per peptide bond, roughly 2 per nucleotide added, plus activation costs for lipid. For the composition above that totals about 23–26 mmol ATP per gDW. Measured growth-associated maintenance in E. coli models is 50–60 mmol ATP per gDW — two to three times higher. The difference covers transcript turnover, protein folding and degradation, transport of precursors into the cell, maintenance of membrane potential during growth, futile cycling, and all the inefficiency the model does not represent explicitly. Say this plainly when asked: GAM is a fitted residual, not a computed cost, and that is a limitation of genome-scale models rather than a defect of your arithmetic.

4. GAM, NGAM and the Pirt relation

Growth-associated maintenance sits inside the biomass reaction, scaling with biomass formed. Non-growth-associated maintenance is a separate ATP hydrolysis reaction with a fixed lower bound, scaling with time rather than growth. The Pirt relation, 1/Y_apparent = 1/Y_true + m/µ, separates them from data: regress reciprocal apparent yield against reciprocal growth rate across a chemostat series, and the intercept gives the true yield while the slope gives the maintenance coefficient. Two warnings from week 18's problem set: two dilution rates cannot resolve this — you need four to six — and the regression is extremely sensitive to residual substrate measurements near the detection limit, which is where most published maintenance coefficients are weakest.

5. Composition changes with growth rate, and what that does to a model

RNA content rises steeply with growth rate — from perhaps 10% of dry weight at µ = 0.1 h⁻¹ to over 30% at µ = 1.5 h⁻¹ — because ribosomes are RNA-rich and ribosome content scales with growth rate. Protein content falls correspondingly. So the precursor demand vector is not a constant: a fast-growing cell needs proportionally more ribose-5-phosphate and nucleotide precursors, and less of the amino acid precursors, than a slow one. A genome-scale model with a single fixed biomass objective function is therefore exactly right at one growth rate and approximately wrong everywhere else. Good practice is to use a composition measured near your condition and to state the growth rate at which it was measured.

6. What a wrong biomass equation does, and how to check yours

Errors propagate asymmetrically. Getting the energy coefficients wrong changes predicted growth yield almost proportionally. Getting a minor precursor wrong changes little, unless that precursor becomes limiting in a knockout, in which case it changes an essentiality call. Getting the elemental formula wrong corrupts every electron balance downstream. The check is to benchmark: compute your own coefficients, then compare against a published model's and explain every discrepancy larger than about twenty per cent. Most differences will trace to composition source or to how ash, lipopolysaccharide and glycogen were handled — and understanding why is worth more than agreeing.

Worked anchor

Build the protein term of a biomass equation and compute its ATP cost.

Protein is 0.55 g per gDW. Average residue mass in a polymer is about 110 g/mol, so residues = 0.55/110 = 5.0×10⁻³ mol = 5.0 mmol per gDW.

ATP cost of polymerisation: 2 ATP-equivalents to charge each tRNA (ATP → AMP + 2Pi counts as two) plus 2 GTP per elongation cycle, so 4 per residue: 5.0 × 4 = 20 mmol ATP per gDW from protein alone.

RNA: 0.20 g per gDW at 330 g/mol gives 0.61 mmol nucleotides; at roughly 2 ATP each for polymerisation, 1.2 mmol. DNA at 0.03 g adds about 0.1 mmol. Lipid and murein add a few more. Total polymerisation ≈ 22–24 mmol ATP per gDW.

Against a measured GAM near 55 mmol per gDW, your computed figure accounts for roughly 40%. Do not treat that as an error in your arithmetic: it is the honest statement that more than half of the energetic cost of growth is not in polymerisation at all, and is captured empirically.

One more check worth doing: 5.0 mmol of amino acid residues per gDW at a typical amino acid mass means protein synthesis consumes the equivalent of about 0.5 g of amino acids per gram of biomass, which must all come from the twelve precursors — so when you trace those 5 mmol back through the week 14 tree, the dominant precursor demands will be pyruvate, oxaloacetate, 2-oxoglutarate and 3-phosphoglycerate. That ordering tells you which parts of central metabolism a growing cell is really pulling on.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • protein 0.55 g per gDW, mean residue 110 g/mol
  • 4 ATP-equivalents per residue polymerised
  • polymerisation only; precursor synthesis costed separately
  • composition measured in one condition and applied here

Common misconceptions

  • That the biomass equation is a measured reaction. It is a modelling construct assembled from composition data, with fitted energy terms — which is why two models of the same organism disagree on it.
  • That one biomass composition serves all conditions. RNA content more than doubles across the growth rate range, so the precursor vector shifts systematically.
  • That a close match to a published biomass equation validates yours. Both may inherit the same composition measurement from the 1970s; what validates yours is explaining the differences, not minimising them.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Compute the degree of reduction of CH₁.₈O₀.₅N₀.₂ and explain its closeness to glucose.

A. γ = 4(1) + 1(1.8) − 2(0.5) − 3(0.2) = 4 + 1.8 − 1 − 0.6 = 4.2 per C-mol, against glucose at exactly 4.00. The closeness means converting glucose into biomass is nearly redox-neutral, so aerobic growth without product formation has a modest oxygen demand — the electrons mostly stay in the biomass. It also means that any product more reduced than 4.2 creates an electron deficit that must be paid by oxidising additional substrate, and any product more oxidised creates a surplus that can be put towards CO₂ fixation.

Q. Why is measured GAM two to three times the computed polymerisation cost?

A. Polymerisation accounts for roughly 23–26 mmol ATP per gDW, while fitted GAM is 50–60. The gap covers transcript synthesis and turnover, protein folding and proteolysis, transport of precursors across the membrane, maintenance of the proton-motive force during growth, futile cycling, and the general inefficiency the model represents nowhere else. GAM is therefore a fitted residual rather than a computed cost, which is a stated limitation of genome-scale models and should be described as such rather than defended.

Q. Your biomass coefficients differ from a published model's by 40% on lipid. What do you check?

A. First, the composition source and the growth condition it was measured under, since lipid fraction varies with growth rate, temperature and medium. Second, whether lipopolysaccharide and membrane lipid were counted together or separately, which is a common bookkeeping difference in Gram-negatives. Third, the assumed average chain length and degree of unsaturation, which changes the acetyl-CoA and NADPH demand per gram substantially. Fourth, whether ash was subtracted consistently. Then test sensitivity: if the predicted growth yield barely moves across the two lipid values, record the discrepancy and proceed; if it moves materially, the composition becomes a measurement you need.
