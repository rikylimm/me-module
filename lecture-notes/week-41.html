Yeasts: Saccharomyces and the non-conventional species

Week content

The eukaryotic chassis, and the week where the host's advantages and its complications are the same fact. Compartments give yeast the membrane surface and the redox environment that cytochrome P450 chemistry needs, and they also mean every heterologous enzyme has an address that must be specified and can be wrong. The quantitative spine of the week is not in the assigned chapter: Lee Ch 18 contains no section on physiology or tools and does not mention the Crabtree effect once, so the respiratory-capacity arithmetic that governs every aerobic yeast process is taken from Shuler and derived here from first principles.

Core ideas

1. The Crabtree effect, stated as a capacity constraint rather than an effect

Shuler §5.1 gives it in one sentence: S. cerevisiae makes ethanol anaerobically and biomass aerobically, but even aerobically, at high glucose concentration, some ethanol forms — regulation by glucose as well as by oxygen. The engineering content is the consequence. Respiratory capacity per litre of broth is bounded by oxygen transfer, so sugar delivered faster than that capacity is fermented whatever the dissolved oxygen reads. Shuler's appendix on bakers' yeast draws the conclusion explicitly: production is run fed-batch to avoid the Crabtree effect, with feeding rates adjusted to maximise growth, and ethanol is reported as inhibitory to growth and as reducing biomass yield. The same appendix gives the numbers the calculation needs — about 0.5 g cells per g substrate, 1 g cells per g oxygen, µmax 0.6 h⁻¹, 30 °C, pH 6–7, dissolved oxygen above 2 mg L⁻¹, vessels of 50–350 m³ at a height-to-diameter ratio of 3.

2. Yeast against E. coli, on the properties that actually decide

Shuler's Table 14.2 is the only like-for-like comparison in the uploaded texts and it is blunt: E. coli excellent on growth rate, genetic systems, expression level and media cost; S. cerevisiae very good to excellent on the same, better on protein folding, capable of simple glycosylation and not complex, very good on secretion where E. coli is poor, and excellent on safety where E. coli is very good. Shuler §14.3.1 adds the decision rule: post-translational modification sends you to animal cells, simple glycosylation permits yeast or fungi, and food use favours a GRAS-listed organism such as S. cerevisiae because it simplifies regulatory approval. Neither claim is about elegance. The second is about calendar time and the first about whether the product works at all.

3. Compartments are addresses, and an address has a cost

Alberts Ch 12 — third edition, sections named rather than numbered — is the mechanism. Signal peptides are typically 15 to 60 residues and often cleaved; ER-destined proteins carry an N-terminal peptide including about 5 to 10 hydrophobic residues, mitochondrial ones a peptide alternating positive and hydrophobic residues, peroxisomal ones a three-residue signal at the carboxyl terminus, and nuclear ones an internal cluster of positive residues. Proteins without any of these stay in the cytosol by default. The chapter's import sections carry the cost that pathway engineering keeps rediscovering: translocation into the mitochondrial matrix requires both the electrochemical gradient across the inner membrane and ATP hydrolysis, and proteins enter in an unfolded state, drawn through by sequential binding to mitochondrial hsp70 and hsp60. An enzyme that will not unfold, or that needs a cofactor bound before it folds, is not a candidate for matrix targeting however good its signal sequence is.

4. Where to put a pathway, as a decision with four inputs

Lee Ch 18 shows the choice being made rather than theorised. Medium-chain fatty acyl-CoAs were hijacked from β-oxidation by compartmentalising the pathway in peroxisomes, setting a physical barrier against competing cytosolic alcohol and aldehyde reductase activity; elsewhere the chapter notes a pathway complicated by subcellular compartmentalisation because its upstream half sits in one compartment and its downstream half in another. The inputs are therefore: where the precursor already is, what competing enzymes are present there, whether the organelle's redox and pH suit the chemistry, and whether the enzymes can be delivered — which Alberts answers. Transport of intermediates across the membrane is the fifth input and the one most often assumed away.

5. Why yeast suits P450 chemistry, and what it costs in oxygen

Cytochromes P450 are membrane-bound and need a reductase partner and a supply of NADPH and molecular oxygen; the ER membrane provides the environment and S. cerevisiae provides the native machinery. Lee §18.2.5 is specific about the cost: when expressing heterologous P450s it is common practice to overexpress the reductase from the same organism, but poor coupling between reductase and P450 consumes NADPH and causes toxicity, as seen in artemisinic-acid-producing strains, which was improved by reducing reductase expression while overexpressing the plant cytochrome b5. Overexpressed P450s can also deplete heme and iron. So the advantage is real and the failure mode is cofactor drain, not catalysis — which makes it a week 31 control problem with an oxygen demand attached, since every P450 turnover consumes O₂ in a vessel whose oxygen transfer already caps the sugar feed.

6. The terpenoid numbers, and what the industrial one proves

Lee §18.2.5.3 gives the lineage: truncating HMG1 to its catalytic domain raised amorphadiene titres fivefold; an improved strain carrying three copies of tHMG1 reached over 40 g L⁻¹ in an ethanol pulse-feed process; Erg20 is the second rate-limiting step, and squalene synthase Erg9 is the competing drain, dynamically repressed by swapping its promoter for a copper-, methionine- or glucose-responsive one so the process runs growth-first and production-second. The β-farnesene strain is the one to study: a synthetic route to cytosolic acetyl-CoA with reduced ATP cost, balanced cofactor supply and increased flux to FPP raised production while requiring 75% less oxygen, reaching 130 g L⁻¹. That last clause is the lesson — at industrial scale the binding constraint was oxygen transfer, and the pathway was redesigned to need less of it.

7. The artemisinin case is about ten years, not about titre

The science in Ro et al., Nature 440:940–943 (2006) was artemisinic acid in engineered yeast; what followed was a decade of process and chemistry work before material reached the market, and then a market that did not need it at the price offered because agricultural supply recovered. Lee Ch 18 supplies the technical half of why: the CYP–CPR–cytochrome b5 coupling problem, heme and iron depletion, and the gap between a flask titre and the 40–130 g L⁻¹ class of strain that an industrial process needs. The non-technical half — semi-synthesis chemistry, regulatory qualification, and the price of the botanical route — is not in the assigned reading and should be cited from elsewhere when you write the case note, not inferred from the chapter.

8. The non-conventional yeasts, and where the week's reading runs out

Yarrowia lipolytica has a chapter of its own and it is the most quantitative in the book. Lee Ch 19 reports intracellular protein at about 50% of dry cell weight when lipid is below 10%, lipid bodies exceeding 50% of dry weight in engineered strains, and states a theoretical yield beside each product: citrate at 1.07 g g⁻¹ against 49 g L⁻¹ from 60 g L⁻¹ glucose under nitrogen limitation, 76% of theoretical; succinate at 110 g L⁻¹ and 0.53 g g⁻¹, 83% of theoretical; triacylglycerol at 61.7% of dry weight, an overall process yield of 0.195 g g⁻¹ and 0.143 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹. That is a storage organism with an electron sink, which is what a hydrophobic product wants. Pichia pastoris appears only in Shuler Table 14.2 — very good growth rate, excellent expression, low-cost media, simple glycosylation, very good secretion — and Kluyveromyces has no treatment anywhere in the uploaded texts beyond a line noting K. fragilis as an option when the substrate is lactose or pentose. The week's fourth objective cannot be met from the week's reading, and the matrix entry should say so rather than be filled from memory.

Worked anchor

The respiratory capacity limit, and the feed rate it implies.

Basis: Shuler's bakers' yeast appendix. Yield on oxygen 1 g cells per g O₂; yield on substrate about 0.5 g cells per g substrate; working volume in the 50–350 m³ class; dissolved oxygen held above 2 mg L⁻¹.

Step 1, the oxygen ceiling. The appendix reports kLa = 600 h⁻¹ for these vessels. With a driving force of about 7 mg L⁻¹ — air-saturated broth at 30 °C less the 2 mg L⁻¹ held in solution — the transfer rate is

OTR = kLa (C* − C) = 600 × 7 = 4,200 mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹ = 4.2 g O₂ L⁻¹ h⁻¹, which is 131 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹.

The same appendix also prints an oxygen transfer rate of 1 mole O₂ per litre per hour. That is 32 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹, about eight times what its own kLa and driving force give, and five to ten times the 100–250 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ that large stirred vessels are usually credited with. The two numbers in one paragraph cannot both be right; the kLa-derived one is the one consistent with everything else, and the printed molar figure should be treated as an error until checked against a second source.

Step 2, from oxygen to biomass. At 1 g cells per g O₂, 4.2 g O₂ L⁻¹ h⁻¹ supports 4.2 g cells L⁻¹ h⁻¹ of purely respiratory growth.

Step 3, the growth rate that permits. At X = 30 g L⁻¹, µ_max,resp = 4.2/30 = 0.14 h⁻¹ — against a µmax of 0.6 h⁻¹. The organism is capable of four times the growth rate the vessel can oxygenate, and the difference is exactly the room in which the Crabtree effect operates.

Step 4, the feed. At 0.5 g cells per g substrate, 4.2 g cells L⁻¹ h⁻¹ needs 8.4 g sugar L⁻¹ h⁻¹. In a 100 m³ working volume that is 840 kg h⁻¹ of sugar; as 50% w/w molasses, about 1.7 m³ h⁻¹ of feed. Any sugar delivered above that is fermented to ethanol, lost from the biomass yield and inhibitory at the titre it reaches.

Step 5, what it costs to get it wrong. Suppose 20% of the sugar overflows. At the respiratory yield of 0.5 g g⁻¹, that fifth would have made 0.1 g cells per g of total sugar; routed to ethanol at roughly 0.48 g g⁻¹ it makes ethanol that is not the product and that suppresses the yield of the rest. The biomass yield falls from 0.5 to about 0.4 g g⁻¹ — a 20% increase in sugar bill per tonne of yeast, from a feed pump setting.

The decision this supports: in any aerobic yeast process the feed rate is set by oxygen transfer, not by the strain's appetite, and the engineering options are to raise kLa, lower the biomass concentration, or lower the demand — which is what the β-farnesene strain did when it was rebuilt to need 75% less oxygen.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • Shuler's bakers' yeast appendix values: 1 g cells per g O₂, about 0.5 g cells per g substrate, μmax 0.6 h⁻¹, kLa 600 h⁻¹
  • an oxygen driving force of 7 mg L⁻¹, air-saturated broth at 30 °C less 2 mg L⁻¹ held in solution
  • oxygen transfer taken from kLa rather than from the 1 mol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ the same appendix prints, which its own kLa contradicts
  • biomass at 30 g L⁻¹ when the respiratory growth rate is computed
  • overflowed sugar routed to ethanol at about 0.48 g g⁻¹ with no further oxidation
  • molasses feed taken as 50% w/w fermentable sugar

Common misconceptions

  • That the Crabtree effect is a fermentation that happens when oxygen runs out. It happens with oxygen in excess and is triggered by glucose: the constraint is respiratory capacity per litre, set by oxygen transfer and by the cell's own respiratory proteome, so a culture at 30% dissolved oxygen can overflow to ethanol while the probe reads comfortable.
  • That compartmentalising a pathway is a matter of adding a targeting sequence. The enzyme must also reach the compartment in a form the import machinery accepts — mitochondrial import runs on the membrane potential plus ATP and takes proteins unfolded — and the intermediates must cross a membrane that may have no transporter for them.
  • That yeast's advantage over E. coli is genetic. Shuler rates E. coli higher or equal on growth rate, genetic systems, expression level and media cost. Yeast's advantages are folding, secretion, simple glycosylation, the membrane environment for P450s, GRAS status and a century of industrial familiarity — none of which is a genetic feature.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Why does yeast suit P450-dependent chemistry, and what does that cost you in oxygen demand?

A. P450s are membrane-bound heme enzymes needing a reductase partner, NADPH and molecular oxygen; the ER membrane and the native reductase and b5 machinery are present in S. cerevisiae and absent in E. coli, so the enzyme folds and couples where it would otherwise be inactive or inclusion-bodied. The cost is twofold. Each turnover consumes O₂ in a vessel whose oxygen transfer already caps the sugar feed — at kLa 600 h⁻¹ and a 7 mg L⁻¹ driving force that ceiling is about 4.2 g O₂ L⁻¹ h⁻¹ — and poor coupling between reductase and P450 burns NADPH without product and is toxic, which Lee reports for artemisinic acid strains and which was fixed by lowering reductase expression and raising cytochrome b5.

Q. Your feed pump is set from the strain's µmax. What happens, and what should it have been set from?

A. The strain will take the sugar and ferment what it cannot respire. At 30 g L⁻¹ biomass the vessel supports about 0.14 h⁻¹ of respiratory growth against a µmax of 0.6 h⁻¹, so a feed sized for the organism overshoots the vessel fourfold: ethanol accumulates, biomass yield falls from roughly 0.5 toward 0.4 g g⁻¹ at a fifth overflowing, and the ethanol inhibits what is left. The feed should be set from oxygen transfer — OTR divided by the yield on oxygen gives the respirable biomass rate, and dividing by the yield on substrate gives the sugar the vessel can actually oxidise, about 8.4 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹ here.

Q. You want a heterologous three-enzyme pathway in the mitochondrial matrix. What do you check before designing the constructs?

A. Whether each enzyme can be imported at all: matrix import requires the electrochemical gradient and ATP, and the chain enters unfolded through hsp70 and hsp60, so an enzyme that needs a bound cofactor to fold, or that assembles as an obligate multimer before import, will not arrive functional. Then whether the precursor is in the matrix or has a transporter into it, whether the product can leave, and what competing matrix enzymes will do to the intermediates. Then the redox and pH of the compartment against the chemistry. Only after those four does the choice of targeting peptide matter, and that part is the easy part.
