Metabolic control analysis and proteome-constrained models

Week content

The week that reshapes how you read every overexpression paper. Control over a flux is shared, sums to one, and redistributes the moment you act on it, so the question is never which enzyme is rate-limiting but where control will move next. The proteome constraint then supplies the budget control is spent from, and the two analyses meet. Paper 4 is sat and marked at the end of the week.

Core ideas

1. The three coefficients, with their definitions and their units

A flux control coefficient is C^J_i = (∂J/∂e_i)(e_i/J): the fractional change in steady-state flux per fractional change in enzyme i's activity, with the network re-equilibrating. A concentration control coefficient is the same with a concentration in place of the flux. An elasticity ε^i_S = (∂v_i/∂S)(S/v_i) is different in kind — a local property of one enzyme's isolated rate, everything else fixed. All three are dimensionless, which is why they compare across enzymes of different magnitudes. The distinction that matters is systemic against local: control coefficients belong to the network and are measurable only in situ, elasticities belong to one enzyme and are measurable in vitro.

2. The summation theorem, and the thing it does not say

Flux control coefficients sum to one across a pathway; concentration control coefficients sum to zero. The flux theorem follows from the fact that raising every enzyme by the same small factor raises the flux by that factor with all concentrations unchanged. So control is a shared, conserved quantity and one enzyme can have more only if others have less. What it does not say is that no enzyme has substantial control — 0.7 is entirely consistent with it. What it makes untenable is the strong rate-limiting-step picture, one enzyme at unity and the rest at zero, together with its corollary that acting on the controlling enzyme redistributes control away from it.

3. The connectivity theorem, and why a step near equilibrium has little control

For any metabolite S, Σ_i C^J_i·ε^i_S = 0 over the enzymes that see S. This links the systemic quantities to the local ones and is how control coefficients follow from network structure plus elasticities. The consequence worth holding: a step far from saturation and near equilibrium has a large elasticity, so the theorem forces its control coefficient small — the network absorbs a change in its activity through a small concentration adjustment. A saturated step far from equilibrium has a small elasticity and tends to carry control. That is the honest version of the intuition that an irreversible saturated step is rate-limiting, and it shows when the intuition fails.

4. Measuring control coefficients, and the caveat on every method

Four routes: titration with an inducible promoter series, taking the slope at the wild-type point, which assumes the promoter changes nothing else; genetic modulation by knockdown or heterozygote, assuming a small perturbation; inhibitor titration, reading the coefficient from the initial slope and assuming specificity; and double modulation, inferring elasticities from paired perturbations. All four estimate a derivative at a point, so the number holds for small perturbations only — an enzyme with a coefficient near zero may still be catastrophic to delete, because a deletion is not a small perturbation.

5. Why overexpression plateaus, with the arithmetic

A control coefficient is a logarithmic derivative, ∂lnJ/∂ln e, so read it carefully across a large perturbation. For an enzyme at C^J = 0.6 the fractional-change reading gives a 60% gain on doubling; holding the coefficient constant across the interval and integrating instead gives J₂/J₁ = 2^0.6 ≈ 1.52, about 52%. Both are sensitivity heuristics rather than predictions, and the second is the better one, since the first extrapolates a tangent to a change that is not small. Neither survives what happens next: intermediate concentrations shift, so the amplified enzyme moves towards saturation; downstream steps carry more flux and approach their capacities; and the summation theorem holds the total at one, so as its coefficient falls the others rise. The second doubling of the same gene therefore achieves much less than the first, and the first achieves well under its prediction. The correct response is coordinated amplification of the steps control has migrated to, which is why multi-gene balancing has outperformed single-gene work in many well-documented pathway optimisations.

6. The proteome as the budget: enzyme cost and allocation

An enzyme carrying flux v needs protein mass m = v·MW/kcat, so every flux has a price and total protein is finite. The budget is not an accounting convention: it is the same crowding that made week 30's parameters effective rather than measured, since a cytoplasm already at 300–400 g L⁻¹ has a finite solvent capacity for further enzyme, which is the constraint crowding-based FBA imposes directly. Coarse-grained models impose one constraint, Σ v_i·MW_i/kcat_i ≤ E, and reproduce what stoichiometric models cannot: overflow metabolism as a switch to a lower-yield but protein-cheaper route at high growth rate, a maximum growth rate at all, and the penalty of expressing anything useless. Fine-grained ME models represent transcription and translation explicitly, so the cost of the cost is accounted and the expression of the machinery itself is predicted. What they compute that an M model cannot is anything where the price of catalysis matters.

7. Where control analysis and proteome allocation meet

Maximise flux J over an allocation subject to Σe_i = E. At the optimum every enzyme's marginal flux per unit protein is equal, or protein would move to the better one, so ∂J/∂e_i = λ for all i. Substituting, C^J_i = λe_i/J, and summing with the summation theorem gives λ = J/E, hence C^J_i = e_i/E: at an optimal allocation each enzyme's control equals its share of the protein budget. That is an idealised optimal-allocation result rather than an identity that holds in a real cell. It needs a fixed total budget, activity proportional to enzyme amount, a smooth interior optimum with no expression bound active, no enzyme shared with another pathway or carrying a second function, and flux through this pathway as the only objective. Within those conditions it is a diagnostic — compute the coefficients, measure the shares by targeted proteomics, and compare. Control above share means under-allocated; below means over-allocated; and the comparison says whether the pathway is near its own optimum at all.

Worked anchor

A four-enzyme pathway, from control coefficients to a protein reallocation.

Measured flux control coefficients: 0.58, 0.27, 0.12, 0.03. They sum to 1.00, which is the first arithmetic check on the model — if they do not, the numerical derivatives are wrong or the steady state was not converged.

The naive prediction: reading C₁ as a fractional change gives 58% on doubling. Integrating instead, with the coefficient held constant across the interval, gives 2^0.58 = 1.49, about 49%. State which you are quoting and why — the first extrapolates a tangent to a perturbation that is not small, the second assumes a coefficient the summation theorem says will fall. Both are upper bounds rather than estimates.

Why the measurement comes in lower: enzyme 1's product accumulates and its substrate falls, moving it towards saturation and product inhibition; downstream steps approach their capacities; and the summation theorem forces redistribution. Recompute the steady state and the flux lands below both figures — 1.41-fold in the model behind this week's figure — while the coefficients come back as something like 0.31, 0.42, 0.21, 0.06, still summing to one, with control migrated to enzyme 2. The next intervention is therefore enzymes 1 and 2 together, not more of enzyme 1.

Now the budget. Suppose proteomics gives pathway protein shares of 0.25, 0.40, 0.25 and 0.10. At an optimal allocation C^J_i = e_i/E, so compare: enzyme 1 has control 0.58 against share 0.25, enzyme 2 has 0.27 against 0.40, enzyme 3 has 0.12 against 0.25, enzyme 4 has 0.03 against 0.10. Only enzyme 1 carries more control than protein; the other three carry more protein than control, by 0.13, 0.13 and 0.07, which is exactly the 0.33 enzyme 1 lacks — necessarily so, since both sets sum to one. The remedy is specific: take protein from enzymes 2, 3 and 4 and give it to enzyme 1. Control analysis alone says amplify enzyme 1; the budget comparison says you can do it without spending additional protein, which is a different and better experiment because it avoids the burden.

The burden itself, and it is larger than the protein share rather than equal to it. In the growth-law framework of Scott, Gunderson, Mateescu, Zhang and Hwa (Science 2010, 330:1099–1102), protein the cell cannot use takes its mass out of the adjustable part of the proteome — the ribosomal and metabolic sectors together, φmax, estimated for E. coli at roughly 0.45 to 0.55 — and growth falls in proportion, Δµ/µ ≈ φpathway/φmax. At 14% of cell protein that is a 25 to 31% penalty, about 29% at φmax = 0.48, from protein mass alone and before any carbon or cofactor drain. The tuning-library anchor in phase 6 runs the same argument against the metabolic sector alone at 40% and gets 37% for a 15% pathway, which is the more pessimistic divisor; quote whichever you use and say which sector you divided by, because that choice is most of the answer. What the penalty is not is the 10 to 20% a reader expects when they assume it tracks the protein share one for one. Measuring it needs the right control, and this is where most published burden claims fail — an empty vector conflates the cost of synthesising any protein at that level with the pathway's metabolic drain. The correct comparison is an equivalent mass of catalytically inert protein from the same promoter and plasmid, with the mass verified; the residual difference is the metabolic cost and nothing else.

Finally, close the loop with week 30. Every coefficient above came from a model whose parameters are unidentifiable in most directions, so propagate the ensemble: if the hundred sets agree that enzyme 1 carries the most control, the conclusion is a model result; if a third put it on enzyme 2, the honest output is that a titration is needed before any construct is built.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the coefficients come from the week 30 model and inherit its parameter uncertainty
  • a fixed total protein budget for the pathway
  • enzyme activity proportional to enzyme amount
  • a smooth interior optimum with no expression bound active
  • no enzyme shared with another pathway or carrying a second function
  • flux through this pathway as the only objective

Common misconceptions

  • That a flux control coefficient near zero means the enzyme does not matter. It means amplifying it slightly changes little. Deleting it may still be catastrophic, because a deletion is not a small perturbation.
  • That the summation theorem rules out a controlling step. It rules out the strong rate-limiting-step picture and forces redistribution after an intervention. A single coefficient of 0.7 is consistent with it.
  • That an empty-vector control measures burden. It conflates synthesis cost with metabolic drain. The control is an equivalent mass of inert protein from the same promoter and plasmid, with the mass verified.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. A flux control coefficient of 0.1: what does it predict for a two-fold overexpression, and why will the measured gain be smaller still?

A. Read as a fractional change, about 10%; integrated with the coefficient held constant, 2^0.1 = 1.07, about 7%. Both are local sensitivity heuristics rather than predictions, since the coefficient is a derivative at the wild-type point and a doubling is not small. The recomputed gain is smaller still, for three compounding reasons: the amplified enzyme moves towards saturation as its substrate is drawn down; downstream steps approach their capacities; and the summation theorem forces its coefficient down as the others rise. There is also a cost the analysis omits — the extra protein comes from the same budget as everything else, so at a coefficient of 0.1 the burden may exceed the benefit, which is the argument against amplifying a low-control step at all.

Q. Derive the relationship between control coefficients and protein shares at an optimal allocation, and say what you would do with it.

A. Maximise J subject to Σe_i = E. At the optimum the marginal flux per unit protein is equal across enzymes, ∂J/∂e_i = λ, since otherwise protein could be moved to a higher-return enzyme. Substituting into C^J_i = (∂J/∂e_i)(e_i/J) gives C^J_i = λe_i/J, and summing with ΣC^J_i = 1 gives λ = J/E, hence C^J_i = e_i/E, under the conditions listed with the worked anchor. Use it as a diagnostic: measure the shares by targeted proteomics, compute the coefficients from the model, and compare. Control above share means the enzyme should receive protein, below means it can give protein up, and that reallocation costs no additional protein so carries no additional burden — a better first experiment than amplification.

Q. What does a proteome-constrained model predict that a stoichiometric one cannot, with two examples?

A. Anything in which the protein price of catalysis matters rather than only the mass and electron balance. Overflow metabolism first: acetate secretion at high growth rate has no stoichiometric explanation, since respiration has the higher ATP yield and the network can perform it, but under a proteome constraint it follows directly, because fermentative ATP production is cheaper in protein per ATP, so above a threshold growth rate the cheaper route is optimal despite the lower yield. Second, the penalty of a useless protein: a stoichiometric model sees only the amino acids diverted, while a proteome-constrained one sees catalytic capacity displaced from the budget.
