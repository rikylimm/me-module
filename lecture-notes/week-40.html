Escherichia coli as a platform

Week content

The reference host, and the week where four decades of published strain engineering are available to check your understanding against. The content divides cleanly: what the knowledge base actually buys, what acetate overflow costs and why it happens, and how to read a published titre. The week's hardest habit is the last one — every number in Lee's Ch 11 tables is a titre, a yield or a productivity measured under conditions the table does not state, and the first thing to do with any of them is recompute it as a fraction of the theoretical maximum you derive yourself. One of them comes out above 100%, which is the most useful thing in the chapter.

Core ideas

1. What "reference host" buys, stated as capability rather than reputation

Shuler §14.3.2 is the honest inventory: E. coli is chosen first when post-translational modification is unnecessary, because the knowledge base, host backgrounds, vectors and promoters are deeper than for any other organism. The quantities behind that are high growth rate, growth to over 50 g dry weight per litre, expression from strong vector–promoter combinations at roughly 25–50% or more of total protein, and cheap defined media — which multiply into volumetric productivity. Table 14.2 rates five hosts across growth rate, genetic systems, expression level, media cost, folding, glycosylation, proteolysis, secretion and safety; it is the skeleton of Artifact 24 and the matrix should keep its columns. Modelling depth is part of the same inventory: Lee §2.4 traces the E. coli genome-scale model from iJE660 through iJR904, iAF1260 and iJO1366 to iML1515, whose reconstruction covers 1,515 genes. No other organism has that lineage, which is exactly why every model you have built in phases 4 and 5 was built here.

2. The same inventory read as liabilities

Shuler §14.3.2 continues into what E. coli does badly, and the list is specific. It does not normally secrete protein: about 20% of its protein is translocated across the inner membrane, and excretion into the medium is the exception. High-level cytoplasmic expression triggers the heat-shock regulon, one response of which is increased proteolysis, in some cases fast enough to degrade product as quickly as it is made. More often the product forms an inclusion body of misfolded, inactive protein, recoverable by resolubilisation at a cost that varies enormously between proteins — and advantageous when resolubilisation is easy, because it simplifies the first purification steps. The cytoplasm may not form disulfide bridges, the product usually retains an N-terminal methionine, and lysing the cells releases lipopolysaccharide endotoxin, which is a purification problem rather than a nuisance when the product is injectable.

3. Acetate overflow is four mechanisms, and the assigned chapter does not contain any of them

This has to be said plainly because the week's curriculum points at Lee Ch 11 for it: the chapter mentions acetate six times, all incidentally, and has no section on overflow. The mechanisms, from the primary literature, are (i) flux imbalance — glucose uptake exceeding what the acetyl-CoA node, TCA cycle and respiratory chain can process, the classical account of el-Mansi and Holms, J Gen Microbiol 135:2875–2883 (1989); (ii) proteome allocation — fermentation is a cheaper way to make ATP per gram of protein invested than respiration, so at high growth rate excreting acetate maximises growth, which is Basan et al., Nature 528(7580):99–104 (2015), doi:10.1038/nature15765, and which predicts the threshold-linear rise of acetate with growth rate; (iii) loss of the scavenging route — acetyl-CoA synthetase is under carbon catabolite repression, so the cell stops reassimilating what it excretes, per Valgepea et al., BMC Syst Biol 4:166 (2010), doi:10.1186/1752-0509-4-166; and (iv) redox state — a high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio inhibits TCA flux and shifts transcription, per Vemuri et al., Appl Environ Microbiol 72(5):3653–3661 (2006). They are not alternatives. A fast-growing fed-batch culture has all four acting at once.

4. One countermeasure per mechanism, and they are not interchangeable

Against flux imbalance: controlled glucose feeding so that glucose never accumulates, which Shuler §14.3.2 names as the engineering contribution that made high-cell-density E. coli possible, coupled to consumption rate where that can be estimated on-line; or weakening uptake itself by moving from the PTS to a permease-plus-kinase route. Against proteome allocation: grow slower in the production phase, because the threshold is in growth rate — which is why a two-phase process with induction late is both a burden and an overflow decision. Against the repressed scavenging route: de-repress or overexpress acs so the excreted acetate is taken back. Against redox: raise respiratory or NADH-oxidising capacity, or aerate harder. Deleting pta and ackA removes the main excretion route and appears in Lee Ch 11 doing exactly that, in the 3-hydroxypropionate strain that reached 72 g L⁻¹ from glycerol at 1.8 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹ — but it is a blunt instrument: it does not remove the cause, and the carbon reappears as pyruvate, lactate or formate unless something downstream takes it.

5. Ch 11 is organised by what the carbon becomes, and the tables are the chapter

§11.2 fuels, §11.3 chemicals, §11.4 materials, with no methods sections at all — tools, expression and genome engineering are elsewhere in the book, as week 33 to 35 already found. Tables 11.1 onward give product, titre, yield, productivity, carbon source, culture style, strategy and reference, and the spread within one product is as instructive as the differences between products: ethanol at 34.6 g L⁻¹ in flask from overexpressed Zymomonas mobilis pdc and adhII, then 54.4 g L⁻¹ at 0.57 g g⁻¹ in batch once the operon was integrated into pflB and frd deleted, then 63.2 g L⁻¹ from xylose in a tolerance-evolved derivative. 1-butanol runs from 0.552 g L⁻¹ in flask to 30 g L⁻¹ at 0.287 g g⁻¹ and 0.18 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹ in fed-batch with in situ product removal. Isopropanol reaches 143 g L⁻¹ — in 240 hours, so 0.60 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹. Caprolactam sits at 79.6 µg L⁻¹. A titre without its culture style and duration is not a number you can use.

6. Recompute every reported yield against a ceiling you derived

Take the degree of reduction of substrate and product, divide, and you have the electron-limited ceiling; compare with the carbon-limited one and the smaller binds. Ethanol from glucose: both limits give 0.511 g g⁻¹. Table 11.1 reports 0.57 g g⁻¹ for the integrated KO11 strain on glucose — 112% of the maximum. The number is not wrong, it is differently based: the extra carbon comes from somewhere the yield definition excludes, almost always the complex medium, and occasionally from a yield taken on sugar consumed for product rather than on sugar consumed. This is the single most transferable skill in the week. A yield above its own ceiling means the basis is undeclared, and a yield of 0.45 g g⁻¹ from xylose in the same table — 88% of theoretical, which is excellent and plausible — tells you the chapter's numbers are not systematically inflated, just inconsistently defined.

7. Yim et al. 2011, criticised with the tools you now have

The 1,4-butanediol paper (Nat Chem Biol 7(7):445–452, cited in Lee Ch 11 as reference 63 for the 18 g L⁻¹ fed-batch result) was read in week 26 as a strain-design success. Three criticisms are available now and were not then. First, the algorithm did the pathway enumeration and the growth coupling, but the enzyme assignment, the expression tuning and the host background were hand work, so the paper demonstrates less about algorithmic design than its framing suggests — week 26's own distinction between a pathway and a strain. Second, 18 g L⁻¹ is a titre against an electron-limited ceiling of about 1.09 mol BDO per mol glucose, 0.55 g g⁻¹: the gap to be closed is in yield and rate, which the paper does not report in a form that lets you close it. Third, growth coupling is a property of a model under an objective, and the week 25 and 26 caveats apply — a coupled design is only coupled while the constraints it was computed under hold, and a seed train is not that environment.

8. Three situations in which E. coli is the wrong choice

When the product needs post-translational modification: Shuler §14.3.1 makes this the first branch in the decision, and no amount of E. coli engineering moves it. When the product is a secreted enzyme sold by the kilogram: a host that secretes natively removes the lysis, the inclusion-body and the endotoxin problems at once, which is week 42's argument for Bacillus. When the process environment itself is hostile — a low-pH acid fermentation, a solvent-containing broth, a feedstock of lignin aromatics — the tolerance of the host matters more than its tool stack, which is weeks 42 and 43. A fourth, weaker case is regulatory: for a food product the question is not what E. coli can express but whether an engineered Gram-negative with an endotoxin problem is a sensible thing to put in the process at all.

Worked anchor

What acetate costs, in carbon and in money, at a stated flux.

Basis: aerobic fed-batch on glucose, product of molar mass 90.1 (take 1,4-butanediol), 15% of consumed carbon leaving as acetate. Glucose 180.16 g mol⁻¹, six carbons; acetic acid 60.05 g mol⁻¹, two.

Carbon first. Per 100 mol of substrate carbon — 16.67 mol glucose, 3,003 g — 15 mol C leaves as acetate, which is 7.5 mol of acetate, 450 g. Nothing has been made from it.

Yield. If the diverted carbon would otherwise have gone to product at the same efficiency, a reported yield of 0.30 g g⁻¹ becomes 0.30/0.85 = 0.353 g g⁻¹ once the leak is closed. Against the electron-limited ceiling of 0.55 g g⁻¹ that is a move from 55% to 64% of theoretical.

Money. Glucose at $400 per tonne: at 0.30 g g⁻¹ the substrate cost is 1/0.30 = 3.33 kg glucose per kg of product, $1.33 kg⁻¹. At 0.353 g g⁻¹ it is 2.83 kg, $1.13 kg⁻¹. The saving is $0.20 per kg, $200 per tonne. For a bulk chemical selling near $1,800 per tonne that is 11% of revenue, and plausibly the whole margin; for a $20,000-per-tonne specialty it is noise. Acetate is a bulk-product problem.

What the arithmetic leaves out, and why it understates the penalty: acetate also inhibits growth. Shuler §14.3.2 says so without giving a threshold, and no number in the assigned reading supplies one, so the honest statement is mechanistic rather than numeric — acetic acid has pKₐ 4.76, so at the pH of a controlled fermentation most of it is dissociated, and the undissociated fraction that crosses the membrane and acidifies the cytoplasm is the week 2 derivation applied to a different acid. The real cost is the carbon above plus a growth-rate term and a titre ceiling that have to be measured in your own strain.

So the rank order of countermeasures follows from where your flux sits, not from a list: if glucose is accumulating, feed control is free and immediate; if it is not, and the culture is fast, the overflow is the proteome-allocation kind and only a slower production phase or more respiratory capacity touches it.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • 15% of consumed carbon leaving as acetate, and that carbon available to product at the same efficiency if the leak were closed
  • product taken as 1,4-butanediol, 90.1 g mol⁻¹, with an electron-limited ceiling of 0.55 g g⁻¹
  • glucose at $400 per tonne and a bulk selling price near $1,800 per tonne
  • no growth-rate or titre penalty from acetate included in the money figure
  • yields compared on grams per gram of glucose consumed, which is the basis Lee's tables do not state

Common misconceptions

  • That acetate overflow means the cell has run out of oxygen. It happens in fully aerated cultures with dissolved oxygen in excess, because the limit is the processing capacity downstream of acetyl-CoA and the proteome cost of respiration, not oxygen availability. Oxygen limitation is a fifth, separate cause with a different signature.
  • That deleting pta and ackA solves it. It removes the main excretion route, not the imbalance that fills it: the carbon re-emerges as pyruvate, lactate or formate, and the strain often grows worse, because acetate excretion was doing something for it.
  • That a published yield can be compared with a theoretical one without checking its basis. Lee's Table 11.1 reports 0.57 g ethanol per g glucose against a ceiling of 0.511 — 112% of theoretical. The yield is on an undeclared basis, almost certainly complex medium carbon; find the basis before you use any number from a table.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Name the four causes of acetate overflow and the specific countermeasure for each.

A. Flux imbalance at the acetyl-CoA node, where uptake outruns TCA and respiratory capacity — countered by controlled glucose feeding, or by weakening uptake itself. Proteome allocation, where fermentation is the cheaper ATP route per gram of protein at high growth rate — countered only by lowering growth rate in the production phase or by buying more respiratory capacity. Carbon catabolite repression of acetyl-CoA synthetase, which switches off reassimilation — countered by de-repressing or overexpressing acs. A high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio inhibiting TCA flux — countered by raising NADH oxidation or aeration. Deleting pta and ackA is not a fifth cause's countermeasure; it blocks the exit, and only helps if something downstream can take the carbon.

Q. A table reports 0.57 g ethanol per g glucose. What do you do?

A. Compute the ceiling before anything else: glucose to ethanol is limited at 0.511 g g⁻¹ by both carbon and electrons, so 0.57 exceeds it and the basis must be undeclared. The usual explanation is carbon from a complex medium counted into product but not into substrate; a second is a yield taken only over the sugar routed to product. Neither makes the strain bad — KO11 is a real strain — but the number cannot enter a comparison with a yield computed on total sugar, and nothing in the matrix should be populated from it without the basis stated beside it.

Q. You are asked to justify E. coli for a secreted industrial enzyme. What is the strongest argument against you, and what would you need to win?

A. That the host does not secrete: about 20% of E. coli protein crosses the inner membrane and excretion to the medium is the exception, so the process acquires lysis, inclusion-body recovery and endotoxin clearance — three unit operations a natively secreting host does not need, for a product whose price cannot carry them. To win you would need either a demonstrated excretion titre in your own hands in the same vessel, or a product that folds only in the cytoplasm, or a downstream train already built and paid for. Expression level alone does not win it, because the 25–50% of total protein that E. coli can reach is intracellular protein.
