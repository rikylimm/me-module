Enzyme engineering: rational design and directed evolution

Week content

Sometimes the pathway is right and the catalyst is wrong. This week is library arithmetic, and the arithmetic is unusually unforgiving: the number of transformants needed to see 95% of a saturation library rises so fast with the number of randomised positions that the design decision is made before any enzymology is considered. The content is how positions are grouped and which codon alphabet is used, because those two choices move the requirement by six orders of magnitude, and they are made from structure and consensus data rather than from intuition.

Core ideas

1. Three strategies differ in what they require you to know, not in what they achieve

Rational design needs a structure, a mechanism and a hypothesis about which residue does what; it tests a handful of variants and is cheap when the hypothesis is right and worthless when it is wrong. Directed evolution needs only a screen and makes no claim about mechanism, which is why it works on properties nobody can predict — expression, solubility, tolerance of a cosolvent — and why it costs library-sized effort. Semi-rational design uses structure to choose where to randomise and evolution to decide what to put there, which is the dominant mode in practice because structural information is now cheap and screening still is not. Protein Engineering Ch 5 formalises this as CAST: residues lining the binding pocket are identified from a structure or a docked substrate, then saturated.

2. Oversampling for 95% coverage is the number that decides the campaign

A saturation library of L distinct members needs roughly 3L clones screened before 95% of it has been seen, because sampling with replacement leaves e⁻ᴺ/ᴸ unsampled. Protein Engineering Table 5.1 tabulates this through the Patrick–Firth algorithm for the two standard codon schemes. With NNK degeneracy — 32 codons covering all 20 amino acids — one position needs 94 transformants, two need 3,066, three need 98,163, four more than 3.1 × 10⁶, six more than 3.2 × 10⁹, and ten more than 3.4 × 10¹⁵. The growth is 32-fold per position at the DNA level, so each additional randomised residue costs a factor of about 30 in screening, and the fourth one is where the campaign stops being a plate experiment.

3. A reduced codon alphabet buys back orders of magnitude and pays in diversity

NDT degeneracy uses 12 codons encoding 12 amino acids — Phe, Leu, Ile, Val, Tyr, His, Asn, Cys, Arg, Ser, Asp and Gly — chosen so the set still spans hydrophobic and hydrophilic, polar and non-polar, aromatic and aliphatic, small and large. The same table gives 34 transformants for one position, 430 for two, 5,175 for three, 62,118 for four, 745,433 for five and more than 8.9 × 10⁶ for six. At six positions that is a 360-fold reduction against NNK; at four it is a 50-fold reduction. What is given up is eight amino acids, including alanine, methionine, tryptophan, proline, threonine, lysine, glutamate and glutamine — so no proline-driven backbone change and no new salt bridge from lysine or glutamate. The chapter's own conclusion is that high coverage of a reduced alphabet beats low coverage of NNK, and notes that this still needs generalising.

4. Grouping positions beats reducing their number

The limonene epoxide hydrolase campaign is the arithmetic made concrete. Ten residues were identified by docking cyclohexene oxide into the crystal structure. NNK saturation at all ten requires more than 3 × 10¹⁵ transformants for 95% coverage, which is not a number; a triple-code alphabet over all ten brings it to about 10⁶, which is still a year. The residues were instead grouped into three randomisation sites — four, three and three residues — and, because docking showed the pocket residues to be hydrophobic and a consensus analysis over 100 related epoxide hydrolases agreed, the building blocks were restricted to valine, phenylalanine and tyrosine. The three libraries then needed 576, 192 and 192 transformants for 95% coverage. Wild-type enantioselectivity of 4% ee became 97–99% ee for the (S,S) product, and one iterative step gave 97% ee for (R,R). The reduction from 10¹⁵ to 10³ came from two structural inferences, not from a better screen.

5. Iterative saturation multiplies calendar, not library size

Having grouped into sites, the order in which they are visited is itself a choice, and the number of upward pathways through the sites is factorial: two sites give two pathways, three give six, four give twenty-four. Each pathway is a sequence of rounds, each round is a library and a screen, and the best variant from one site becomes the parent for the next. This is where a campaign's duration actually lives. The practical consequence is that site count is capped by calendar rather than by screening capacity — four sites at even two weeks per round is two years if the pathways are explored exhaustively, so a real campaign commits to one pathway and justifies the choice, usually by taking the site with the largest single-site effect first.

6. Screening format is a throughput ladder with a resolution penalty at every rung

Agar plates with a colorimetric readout reach 10³–10⁴ per week and measure a crude proxy. Microtitre plates with a real assay reach 10³–10⁴ and measure the property you care about, but each well costs reagent. Flow cytometry reaches 10⁷–10⁸ per day and requires the property to be fluorescent and cell-associated, which is the condition week 36's biosensors exist to manufacture. Droplet microfluidics reaches comparable numbers while keeping the enzyme and its product in the same compartment, which is what lets a secreted or diffusible product be screened at all. Growth coupling is effectively unlimited and measures only whether the cell lived. Every rung up trades the directness of the measurement for throughput, and the campaign is designed from both ends at once: the library size the format can cover, and the property the format can see.

7. Continuous evolution is bounded by the error threshold, and its cost is targeting

Ch 1 derives why in-vivo continuous evolution is hard. Organisms sit near a mutational error threshold of order 1/L: with a genome of 5 × 10⁶ bases, E. coli's measured rate is about 5 × 10⁻⁸ substitutions per base, and yeast at 1.2 × 10⁷ bases sits near 10⁻¹⁰. A 1 kb gene of interest, being three to four orders of magnitude shorter, can tolerate about 10⁻³ per base and needs that much to generate comparable total diversity. So the whole engineering problem is targeting: mutating the gene at 10⁻³ while leaving the genome at 10⁻⁸ demands on-target against off-target discrimination of about 10⁶-fold in E. coli and 10⁷-fold in yeast and human cells, far beyond the 10- to 1,000-fold selectivity typical of molecular recognition problems. The reported systems land at various points — orthogonal replication near 10⁻⁵ per base, error-prone polymerase I systems at 10⁻³ and up to 10⁻² adjacent to a nicked site, a 7.7-million-fold local elevation, with off-target rates rising too, in one case 40-fold over a 10 kb window.

8. Stability is spent, not preserved, and has to be bought in advance

Activity-improving substitutions are disproportionately destabilising, because the active site is already strained and the residues that tune it are the ones holding the fold together. A campaign that gains four-fold activity over four rounds and loses 8 kJ mol⁻¹ of folding free energy has produced an enzyme that is better on the bench and worse in a 48-hour fermentation at 37 °C. The buffers are known and cheap relative to the campaign: consensus substitutions, where the most common residue across a large homologue alignment is adopted; ancestral sequence reconstruction, which tends to yield more thermostable parents; and B-FIT, which saturates the positions with the highest crystallographic B-factors on the argument that the most mobile residues are where rigidification pays. All three are applied before the activity campaign, not after it, because the stability margin is what the activity campaign then spends.

Worked anchor

Design the campaign for the weakest enzyme in the pathway. Week 31's control analysis put a flux control coefficient of 0.58 on one step; the enzyme is characterised, there is a crystal structure, and docking identifies six first-shell residues. The screen available is a microtitre assay at 10⁴ wells per week, and a biosensor-based sort is possible at 10⁷ per day if the week 36 sensor can be made to work.

Option A, NNK at all six. Table 5.1 gives more than 3.2 × 10⁹ transformants for 95% coverage. At 10⁴ per week that is 6,100 years, and even at 10⁷ per day by sorting it is 320 days of continuous sorting. Excluded.

Option B, NDT at all six. More than 8.9 × 10⁶ transformants. At 10⁴ per week, 17 years — excluded for the plate assay. At 10⁷ per day it is a single day's sort, so this option exists if and only if the sensor exists. That is the decision the two weeks are linked by: the sensor is not a convenience, it is what makes a six-position library legal.

Option C, two sites of three with NDT. Each site needs 5,175 transformants for 95% coverage, so one round is half a week of microtitre screening and both sites are one week. Two sites give two iterative pathways; committing to one, the campaign is two rounds, with build and assay turnaround putting each round at about three weeks. Total nine weeks including the confirmation assay.

Option D, three sites of two with a reduced three-amino-acid code, if docking and a consensus alignment support restricting the alphabet as the limonene epoxide hydrolase case did. Each site then needs a few hundred transformants, so a round is a day of screening, but three sites give six pathways and the calendar, not the screening, becomes the constraint.

Choose C. The arithmetic says so: it is the largest search that the available screen can actually cover without depending on a sensor that does not yet exist, and it keeps the pathway count at two so the commitment to one ordering costs at most a factor of two in explored space.

Expected gain and stopping. Published semi-rational campaigns on selectivity report wild-type values moving most of the way to the achievable limit in one to two rounds — 4% to 97–99% ee in the limonene case. For activity rather than selectivity the usual observation is two- to five-fold per round for the first two rounds, flattening after. So the campaign is specified as: two rounds, target a four-fold improvement in kcat/KM against the wild type, measured by the confirmation assay on purified protein rather than by the screen, with a stability check of ΔTm no worse than −3 °C. Stopping criterion as a result and a date: if round one does not deliver two-fold by week six, stop and reconsider the target, because the second round's expected gain is conditional on the first. The alternative if it is not met is to spend the remaining budget on consensus and B-FIT stabilisation of the wild type and accept the wild-type activity, on the grounds that an enzyme that survives the run is worth more than one that is faster for six hours.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • transformant counts for 95% coverage taken from Protein Engineering Table 5.1 as printed, computed there by the Patrick–Firth algorithm
  • sampling treated as with replacement, so coverage is 1 − e⁻ᴺᐟL and N ≈ 3L at 95%
  • microtitre screening capacity 10⁴ wells per week and sorting capacity 10⁷ per day, both taken as sustained rather than peak
  • transformation efficiency not limiting, so the number of transformants needed equals the number obtainable
  • each campaign round taken as three weeks from library build to confirmed hit, including assay turnaround
  • one iterative pathway committed to, so the explored space is a factor of two below the two-site exhaustive set
  • expected gain of two- to five-fold per round taken from reported semi-rational campaigns on other enzymes, not from this target
  • the week 31 flux control coefficient of 0.58 assumed still to hold at the improved activity, which is a tangent and not a prediction

Common misconceptions

  • That more randomised positions means a better search. Each position multiplies the library by about 32 under NNK and 12 under NDT, so the sixth position costs three orders of magnitude and buys coverage of a space you cannot screen. Real campaigns search less space more completely: the limonene case went from 3 × 10¹⁵ to 576 transformants by grouping and alphabet reduction and got 99% ee out of it.
  • That NNK is the safe default because it covers all 20 amino acids. It covers them at a coverage you cannot afford past three positions. The chapter's own position is that high coverage of a reduced alphabet beats low coverage of the full one — and a library screened at 5% coverage is a random sample dressed as a saturation experiment, with no basis for concluding that an absent substitution was tested.
  • That raising the mutation rate speeds up evolution. Above about 1/L it destroys the population: the genome is near its error threshold already, which is why E. coli sits at 5 × 10⁻⁸ per base. Continuous evolution systems do not raise the mutation rate, they relocate it, and their whole engineering difficulty is the 10⁶- to 10⁷-fold on-target to off-target discrimination that relocation requires.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Saturation at six positions needs how many transformants for 95% coverage, and which screening formats can deliver that?

A. Under NNK degeneracy, more than 3.2 × 10⁹ — no format reaches that at 95% coverage except growth coupling, which only reports survival. Under NDT, more than 8.9 × 10⁶, which flow cytometry at 10⁷–10⁸ per day or droplet microfluidics can cover in about a day, provided the property is fluorescent and cell-associated; plate and microtitre formats at 10³–10⁴ per week cannot. The correct answer to the question as posed in a campaign is to not randomise six positions at once: two NDT sites of three need 5,175 transformants each, which is half a week of microtitre screening and keeps a direct assay instead of a proxy.

Q. A collaborator proposes continuous evolution instead of your two-round campaign. What do you ask before agreeing?

A. Three things. What is the selection, since continuous evolution selects continuously and therefore needs the phenotype linked to host fitness at every generation — it cannot select for a property measured in a plate reader, nor for anything requiring purified protein, nor for stability at a temperature the host will not grow at. What is the off-target rate, because a system driving the target at 10⁻³ per base while raising the genome 40-fold over a 10 kb window accumulates host mutations that confound every subsequent measurement, so the endpoint needs resequencing. And what happens to the parent: continuous systems have no round structure, so there is no intermediate to retreat to and no record of the trajectory unless frozen stocks are banked deliberately. If the property is growth-coupled and the campaign would otherwise take a year, it is the right tool; if the property needs an assay, it is not.

Q. Your campaign gains four-fold in kcat/KM and the enzyme now denatures in eight hours at the process temperature. What went wrong, and at what point in the plan should it have been prevented?

A. Nothing went wrong in the screen; the screen measured what it was asked to. Activity-improving substitutions are disproportionately destabilising because the residues tuning the active site are the ones holding the fold, so a campaign that does not measure stability spends the stability margin without recording it. The prevention is upstream of round one, not after it: consensus substitutions from a large homologue alignment, ancestral reconstruction, or B-FIT saturation at the highest-B-factor positions, applied to the wild type to build margin that the activity campaign can then consume. Retrofitting afterwards means re-screening every stabilising candidate against the new active site, since the mutations now interact. The procedural fix is a stability gate in the round criteria — ΔTm no worse than some stated figure — so the trade is visible at each round instead of discovered at the end.
