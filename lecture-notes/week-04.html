Enzyme catalysis

Week content

Mechanism before kinetics. Knowing which catalytic strategy an enzyme uses tells you what its rate is sensitive to, which cofactor it will demand from your pathway, and what engineering it will tolerate. The week also teaches you to read a kinetic record sceptically, which is the skill Brief 1 grades.

Core ideas

1. Catalysis is transition-state stabilisation, and the arithmetic is exponential

Rate ∝ exp(−ΔG‡/RT). At 310 K, RT = 2.577 kJ/mol, so every 5.9 kJ/mol of barrier reduction buys a factor of ten. A 30 kJ/mol reduction buys about 10⁵; real enzymes achieve 10⁸ to 10¹⁷, which means barrier reductions of 45–100 kJ/mol. The enzyme achieves this by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate — binding energy is paid back as catalysis. Crucially, the barrier falls equally in both directions, so the equilibrium constant is unchanged. State this crisply when asked; confusing catalysis with equilibrium shifting is the commonest conceptual error in interviews.

2. Four catalytic strategies and their canonical examples

Covalent catalysis: a nucleophilic residue forms a transient covalent intermediate — serine proteases, with the Ser-His-Asp triad in which the histidine deprotonates the serine and the aspartate orients the histidine. General acid-base catalysis: a residue donates or accepts a proton in the transition state, which is why histidine at pKa ≈ 6 is so common. Metal ion catalysis: a bound metal polarises a substrate or lowers the pKa of water, as zinc does in carbonic anhydrase to generate hydroxide at neutral pH. Proximity and orientation: holding two substrates in the correct geometry, which converts an unfavourable entropy of association into a binding event paid for once.

3. Cofactors are a pathway-level constraint, not an enzyme detail

Learn which chemistry each cofactor enables, because that is how you predict a pathway's demand. NAD⁺/NADH: two-electron hydride transfer, catabolic, held oxidised. NADP⁺/NADPH: the same chemistry, biosynthetic, held reduced — the separation is enforced by enzyme specificity and is the central cofactor problem of the course. FAD: one- or two-electron, handles radicals and oxygen. Coenzyme A: acyl group activation as a thioester, which is energetically rich. Thiamine pyrophosphate: decarboxylation and two-carbon transfer. Pyridoxal phosphate: essentially all amino acid chemistry — transamination, decarboxylation, racemisation. Biotin: carboxylation, which is how CO₂ gets fixed at the anaplerotic node. Tetrahydrofolate: one-carbon units. When you look at a new pathway, total its cofactor demand before anything else; that total often decides the host.

4. EC numbers as a prediction tool

The first digit gives the reaction class: 1 oxidoreductase, 2 transferase, 3 hydrolase, 4 lyase, 5 isomerase, 6 ligase, 7 translocase. From the class alone you can usually predict the cofactor requirement — a 1.x.x.x needs a redox cofactor, a 6.x.x.x needs ATP, a 4.1.1.x is a decarboxylase and so releases CO₂ and probably costs you a carbon. Use this to sanity-check a pathway you are reading for the first time, before you look anything up.

5. Why assay conditions make published parameters incomparable

Enzyme rate follows Arrhenius over the stable range, with activation energies typically 40–60 kJ/mol, which corresponds to a Q₁₀ of roughly 2 — so a measurement at 25 °C rather than 37 °C is about half as fast before anything else differs. Above the stability limit the rate collapses as the protein unfolds, so the apparent optimum is a competition between catalysis and denaturation and is not a thermodynamic property. pH dependence is usually bell-shaped: two ionisable groups, one that must be protonated and one deprotonated, give a plateau between their pKa values, and the apparent kcat and KM can have different pH profiles because they report on different ionisation states. Ionic strength and buffer identity shift both, and some buffers coordinate metals or inhibit outright. Put these together and a sevenfold spread in reported kcat for one enzyme needs no further explanation — which is the quantitative basis for the week 4 scepticism and for Brief 1.

6. Why published kinetic parameters disagree, and what to do

Four mechanistically distinct causes. Assay conditions — pH, temperature, ionic strength, buffer identity — easily span sevenfold. Enzyme preparation: tags, partial misfolding, proteolysis, or an inactive fraction, all of which depress apparent kcat unless active-site titration was done. Cofactor or metal saturation differing between preparations. And a different substrate, isoform or orthologue reported under the same EC number. The correct response is never to average: tabulate values with their conditions, exclude the non-comparable ones with the exclusion stated, and carry a range into any model. If a conclusion flips across that range, the study's output is that the parameter must be measured.

Worked anchor

How much rate does a 32 kJ/mol barrier reduction buy at 310 K, and what does that mean practically?

Enhancement = exp(ΔΔG‡/RT) = exp(32,000/(8.314 × 310)) = exp(12.42) = 2.5 × 10⁵.

If the uncatalysed reaction has a half-life of 100 years, the catalysed half-life is 100 × 365 × 24 / 2.5×10⁵ hours ≈ 3.5 hours. Still far too slow for a metabolic step, which tells you that 32 kJ/mol is a modest enzyme and that real central-metabolic enzymes must be doing considerably more — typically 60–90 kJ/mol of barrier reduction.

Use this both ways: it calibrates your sense of what catalysis costs, and it tells you that an engineered enzyme improved by 10-fold has gained only 5.9 kJ/mol of transition-state stabilisation, which is one hydrogen bond. That is why directed evolution works in small increments.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • 310 K, transition-state theory with an unchanged prefactor
  • barrier reduction is the only change between catalysed and uncatalysed
  • first-order decay, so the half-life scales inversely with rate

Common misconceptions

  • That enzymes shift equilibria. They lower the barrier in both directions; ΔG° is a state function and is untouched.
  • That kcat is a property of the enzyme. It is a property of the enzyme under stated conditions with a stated substrate and preparation, which is why the same EC number spans orders of magnitude.
  • That a high kcat means a fast step in vivo. Flux depends on kcat, enzyme abundance and the substrate saturation, and an enzyme at 5% saturation is slow however high its kcat.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. What rate enhancement does 24 kJ/mol of barrier reduction give at 310 K?

A. exp(24,000/(8.314 × 310)) = exp(9.31) ≈ 1.1×10⁴, roughly ten thousand fold. Each 5.9 kJ/mol is a factor of ten at this temperature, so 24 kJ/mol is about four decades.

Q. From its EC number alone, 4.1.1.31 — what does this enzyme do, what cofactor is likely, and what does it cost in carbon?

A. Class 4 is a lyase; 4.1 cleaves carbon-carbon bonds; 4.1.1 is a carboxy-lyase, meaning a decarboxylase or, run in reverse, a carboxylase. 4.1.1.31 is PEP carboxylase. Since it fixes CO₂ onto PEP to give oxaloacetate it gains a carbon rather than losing one, and unlike pyruvate carboxylase it needs no biotin and no ATP — it uses the phosphate transfer potential of PEP instead. The general lesson is that the class alone predicts both the chemistry and whether carbon is gained or lost.

Q. Two laboratories report kcat differing fivefold. Give four distinct causes and say which value you would model.

A. Assay temperature and pH, worth about two-fold for a 12 °C difference alone; enzyme preparation differences including tags, misfolding and an inactive fraction that depresses apparent kcat unless active-site titration was done; cofactor or metal saturation differing between preparations; and a different substrate, isoform or orthologue reported under the same EC number. Model neither value alone: tabulate both with conditions, exclude the non-comparable one if there is a reason to, and carry the remaining range into an ensemble. If the conclusion flips across that range, the output of the study is that the parameter must be measured.
