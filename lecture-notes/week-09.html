Week 9 of 55 · Metabolism in depth · Core

Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis

Week content

The chassis. Every product you design draws from twelve precursors, most made here, and every yield calculation starts from this stoichiometry. Learn the pathway as an accounting object rather than a sequence of names: what goes in, what comes out, what it costs in ATP and redox, and where the regulation sits. By Friday you should be able to write all ten reactions with cofactors from memory and build the stoichiometric matrix by hand.

Core ideas

1. The two phases, and why the investment phase exists

The preparatory phase spends 2 ATP to phosphorylate and cleave glucose into two triose phosphates; the payoff phase recovers 4 ATP and 2 NADH. Net: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate per glucose. The investment looks wasteful until you see its three purposes: phosphorylation traps the sugar inside the cell, since the phosphorylated form cannot cross the membrane; it lowers the activation barrier to cleavage by destabilising the hexose; and it creates the handle for the substrate-level phosphorylation that follows. Carry the ten reactions as a sequence of chemical transformations — phosphorylation, isomerisation, phosphorylation, cleavage, isomerisation, oxidative phosphorylation, transfer, mutation, dehydration, transfer — rather than as ten names.

2. The three irreversible steps and what they regulate

Hexokinase or glucokinase, phosphofructokinase, and pyruvate kinase are the thermodynamically irreversible steps, and they are the regulated ones because reversible steps near equilibrium cannot control flux. PFK is inhibited by ATP and PEP and activated by AMP and ADP, making it an energy sensor; in many organisms fructose-2,6-bisphosphate is a potent activator. Pyruvate kinase is activated by fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, which is feedforward activation — upstream signal anticipating downstream demand. Note the distinction you settled in week 6: these enzymes regulate glycolysis strongly and control it weakly, which is why overexpressing PFK does little and why the 'pacemaker' language in older texts is wrong.

3. Gluconeogenesis and the cost of futile cycling

Reversing glycolysis requires bypassing the three irreversible steps: pyruvate carboxylase plus PEP carboxykinase replaces pyruvate kinase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase replaces PFK, and glucose-6-phosphatase replaces hexokinase. If both enzymes of a pair are active at once the net result is ATP hydrolysis with no chemical change — a futile cycle. Cells suppress this by reciprocal regulation, but it is never complete, and a deliberate futile cycle is occasionally used to create ATP demand. Quantitatively: a cycle running at 1 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ burns 1 mmol ATP gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹, which is about 5% of maintenance — detectable but rarely decisive unless the cycle is fast.

4. Three routes from glucose to pyruvate, compared properly

Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas: 2 ATP, 2 NADH per glucose. Entner-Doudoroff: 1 ATP, 1 NADH and 1 NADPH, using fewer enzyme steps and substantially less protein per unit flux. Phosphoketolase: 1 ATP per glucose with acetyl phosphate as a product, and no net NADH if the acetyl phosphate goes to acetate. The comparison that matters is not ATP per glucose but ATP per gram of enzyme invested: ED yields half the ATP but needs far less protein, which is why it dominates in Pseudomonas and Zymomonas, organisms operating where carbon is cheap and proteome is the binding constraint. This is the week-1 growth laws showing up in pathway choice, and it is the same logic that explains overflow metabolism in week 13.

5. The twelve precursors, which you should now commit to memory

Glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate, ribose-5-phosphate, erythrose-4-phosphate, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, 3-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenolpyruvate, pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, 2-oxoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, oxaloacetate. Six of these come from glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway, six from the TCA cycle and its node. Every biomass component and nearly every product derives from them, which is why the biomass objective function in week 16 is written in their terms and why a yield calculation begins by asking which precursors a product needs and how many carbons are lost reaching them.

6. The matrix, and the first thing it tells you

Write the reactions as columns and the metabolites as rows, with stoichiometric coefficients as entries, and you have S. Two immediate checks: every column must balance elementally and in charge, which catches most transcription errors; and the null space of S contains every steady-state flux distribution, so its dimension tells you how many fluxes must be fixed by measurement or assumption before a unique solution exists. For glycolysis alone with its exchanges the null space is small and interpretable by inspection, which is exactly why Palsson teaches it on this pathway. Build it by hand this week, and build it in a notebook as you read rather than afterwards: print the matrix as a labelled DataFrame and assert that every column sums to zero for C, H, O, N, P and charge before you trust anything downstream. Reading about a carbon rearrangement in prose is weaker than watching its column balance in Python. In week 20 you will compare yours against the published core model, and every difference will teach you something.

Worked anchor

How much carbon does the cell lose on the way to acetyl-CoA, and why does that bound every acetyl-CoA-derived product?

Glucose (6 C) → 2 pyruvate (3 C each), no loss. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂, so one carbon of three is released at each of two pyruvates. Carbon reaching acetyl-CoA: 4 of 6, or 67%.

Therefore any product built purely from acetyl-CoA has a hard carbon ceiling of 2/3 C-mol per C-mol of glucose through this route, before any consideration of redox or ATP. For a C4 product from 2 acetyl-CoA, that is 1 mol product per mol glucose maximum; for a C₁₆ fatty acid needing 8 acetyl-CoA, 0.5 mol per mol glucose.

Now the engineering response, which is why this calculation appears in every acetyl-CoA paper: either fix the lost CO₂ back in with a carboxylation, or use a route that avoids the decarboxylation entirely — a phosphoketolase route to acetyl phosphate, or a reversed glyoxylate shunt. Each recovers carbon and costs something else. Doing this arithmetic before reading the paper tells you what problem the authors were solving.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • carbon accounting only; redox and ATP ignored
  • pyruvate dehydrogenase is the only route to acetyl-CoA
  • no anaplerotic return of carbon from the TCA cycle

Common misconceptions

  • That the irreversible steps are irreversible because the enzymes are one-directional. They are irreversible because ΔrG′ is strongly negative under cellular conditions; a different enzyme with a different mechanism runs the reverse reaction.
  • That ED is simply worse than EMP. It yields less ATP per glucose and more ATP per gram of protein, and which matters depends on whether carbon or proteome is limiting.
  • That phosphorylating glucose is purely about trapping it. It also destabilises the hexose for cleavage and creates the handle for substrate-level phosphorylation.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Write the net stoichiometry of glycolysis including cofactors and protons, and say where each ATP is made and spent.

A. Glucose + 2 NAD⁺ + 2 ADP + 2 Pi → 2 pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 ATP + 2 H⁺ + 2 H₂O. Spent: 1 ATP at hexokinase, 1 at phosphofructokinase. Made: 2 at phosphoglycerate kinase and 2 at pyruvate kinase, one each per triose, so 4 gross and 2 net. NADH is produced at glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, which is also where inorganic phosphate enters.

Q. Why does Entner-Doudoroff persist despite yielding half the ATP?

A. It requires substantially less enzyme protein per unit of catabolic flux, so under proteome-limited aerobic conditions it delivers more ATP per gram of protein invested even though it delivers less per glucose. Organisms occupying niches where carbon is plentiful and growth rate is set by protein allocation — Pseudomonas, Zymomonas — favour it. It also yields NADPH rather than only NADH, which suits oxidative-stress tolerance and reductive biotransformations.

Q. A product is built from 3 acetyl-CoA. What is its carbon ceiling from glucose through pyruvate dehydrogenase, and how would you raise it?

A. Each acetyl-CoA costs a decarboxylation, so from 1.5 glucose (9 C) you get 3 acetyl-CoA (6 C) having lost 3 as CO₂: a ceiling of 2/3 C-mol per C-mol, giving 0.67 mol product per mol glucose for a C6 product. Raise it by fixing the CO₂ back with a carboxylating reaction, or by avoiding the decarboxylation entirely with a phosphoketolase route to acetyl phosphate or a reversed glyoxylate shunt. Each recovers carbon at a cost in ATP or redox, and which is preferable depends on the host's energetics.
