Curation, GPRs and gap filling

Week content

The unglamorous ninety per cent of modelling. Curation is where a draft becomes trustworthy, and knowing what it involves is what lets you judge whether a published model deserves the weight you are about to put on it. The week's practical output is your own gene-deletion implementation, which forces you to understand GPR logic rather than call a function.

Core ideas

1. The four-step process, and what each consumes

Draft reconstruction from the annotated genome, which is automatable and produces something incomplete and partly wrong. Curation against literature, which is manual, slow and the bulk of the work: checking stoichiometry, charge balance, directionality, localisation and evidence for every reaction. Conversion to a computational model by adding bounds, a medium and an objective. Validation against experimental data, which sends you back to curation. Published genome-scale reconstructions have taken person-years; the 96-step standard operating procedure exists because the work is too detailed to carry in your head.

2. GPR logic, and why AND versus OR decides essentiality

A gene-protein-reaction association maps genes to a reaction with Boolean logic. AND denotes a complex: all subunits required, so deleting any one gene removes the reaction. OR denotes isozymes or alternative complexes: either suffices, so only the double deletion removes the reaction. Real GPRs nest both — (a AND b) OR (c AND d) is a reaction catalysed by either of two heterodimers. Getting this right is what lets a model predict gene essentiality rather than merely reaction essentiality, and writing AND where the truth is OR is a leading cause of false positives. Implement the deletion logic yourself from the GPR strings this week and validate it against the library's function; the exercise takes an afternoon and removes any mystery from week 25.

3. Dead ends, blocked reactions and what they signify

A dead-end metabolite is produced but never consumed, or vice versa. At steady state it forces every reaction touching it to zero flux, so whole pathway segments go silent without complaint. Blocked reactions are those that cannot carry flux in any steady state. In a typical draft, a substantial fraction of reactions are blocked, and each blockage is a knowledge gap: a missing transporter, a missing biosynthetic step, or a reaction that genuinely should not be there. Finding them is mechanical — compute flux variability on an open medium and collect the reactions with zero range — and interpreting them is the curation work.

4. Gap filling is hypothesis generation, not evidence

Algorithmic gap filling searches a universal reaction database for the smallest set of additions that restores flux. That is a parsimony argument, and parsimony is not evidence. Before accepting a fill, look for a homologue of the candidate enzyme in the genome; if there is none, the fill is a claim that the organism does something with no known gene. Record every fill in the provenance log at the lowest confidence class, and then check whether any conclusion you draw depends on one. The specific trap to avoid is filling gaps until the model grows: that guarantees growth and destroys the model's evidential value.

5. Thermodynamic directionality, which is curation not computation

Every reaction needs bounds, and the choice of reversible or irreversible is a thermodynamic judgement made at curation time. Set too many reactions reversible and the model finds unphysical routes, including loops; set too many irreversible and you exclude flux the cell genuinely carries. Good practice is to assign directionality from ΔrG′° with physiological concentration ranges, flagging the marginal cases rather than deciding them silently. This is where week 8's conventions earn their keep, and it is the main reason two models of the same organism give different answers.

6. Quality control as a test suite

Treat a model like code. Standardised test suites check mass and charge balance on every reaction, metabolite and reaction identifiers against databases, the presence of gene associations, absence of duplicated reactions, correct exchange formulation, blocked reaction fraction, and whether the model produces energy from nothing — a stoichiometrically balanced cycle generating ATP with no substrate, which is the most embarrassing failure and still appears in published models. Run such a suite on any model before you use it, including your own.

Worked anchor

Implement gene deletion from a GPR, and find out why it matters.

Take the GPR '(b0727 and b0726) or b2910'. Two subunits forming a complex, or a single alternative gene.

Delete b2910 alone: the complex remains, so the reaction stays active. Delete b0727 alone: the complex is broken, but b2910 still provides the activity, so the reaction stays active. Delete b0727 and b2910 together: no route remains, so the reaction is knocked out.

The implementation is a Boolean evaluation with deleted genes set false. Write it, run it over every gene in the core model, and compare with the library's built-in function. They should agree exactly; where they do not, you have either a parsing bug or a GPR with syntax you did not anticipate, and both are worth finding now.

Then do the instructive variant: change the GPR to '(b0727 and b0726) and b2910' — the same genes with the wrong connective — and rerun. Every one of the three genes now reads as essential. That single character is the difference between three correct predictions and three false positives, and it is why week 25's confusion matrix is dominated by false positives rather than false negatives.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the curated GPR is correct
  • deletion means complete loss of function
  • no regulatory or expression-level compensation

Common misconceptions

  • That gap filling completes a model. It generates hypotheses at the lowest evidence class, and a model with dozens of unflagged fills is not a reconstruction.
  • That directionality comes from the solver. It is a curation decision made from thermodynamics, and it is the main reason two models of one organism disagree.
  • That a published model is validated. Some produce ATP from nothing; run a test suite before you trust one, including your own.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Why does writing AND where the truth is OR produce a false essentiality prediction?

A. AND models a protein complex, so deleting any one listed gene removes the reaction and the model calls that gene essential. If the genes are in fact isozymes, the organism retains the activity after a single deletion and the gene is experimentally non-essential. The error produces a false positive for every gene in the mis-written rule, and because isozymes are common it is a leading contributor to the precision deficit typically seen in deletion screens.

Q. What is a blocked reaction, how do you find them, and what does a high fraction mean?

A. A reaction that cannot carry flux in any steady state, usually because it touches a dead-end metabolite. Find them by running flux variability analysis on a permissive medium and collecting reactions whose minimum and maximum flux are both zero. A high blocked fraction means the reconstruction has substantial knowledge gaps — missing transporters, missing biosynthetic steps, or reactions wrongly included — and each blockage is a curation task rather than a computational one.

Q. A model produces ATP with no substrate uptake. What has gone wrong and why does it matter?

A. A stoichiometrically balanced cycle exists that generates ATP from nothing, usually created by a reaction with the wrong directionality or an unbalanced proton or water stoichiometry. It matters because every energetic prediction the model makes is then meaningless: growth yields, maintenance fitting and any ATP-limited design all rest on the energy balance. It is a standard test in quality-control suites, and the fact that it still appears in published models is the reason you run the suite yourself.
