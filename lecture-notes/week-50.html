Instrumentation, control and feeding strategies

Week content

Control applied to a catalyst that adapts to the control action, which is the one sentence that makes this week different from a process-control course. The content divides into what can be measured, what has to be inferred, and what the feed rate is actually allowed to be — and the third of those is where the arithmetic lives. The result that organises the week is that the feed profile is not set by the strain's maximum growth rate, nor by the overflow threshold, but by the vessel's oxygen transfer capacity from week 49: once cell density is high enough, the exponential feed has to stop being exponential, and the correct policy becomes constant oxygen uptake rate with a growth rate that falls as the reciprocal of biomass. Every feeding strategy in the textbooks is a way of implementing that without saying so.

Core ideas

1. What is measured, and what each measurement's failure mode is

Stanbury Ch 8 is organised as a catalogue and it should be read as one, with a failure mode written beside each entry, because that column is what the control philosophy document needs and no book supplies it. The chapter's named sections: methods of measuring process variables, covering temperature (mercury-in-glass, electrical resistance, thermistors, and temperature control), flow measurement and control for gases and liquids, pressure measurement and control, safety valves, agitator shaft power, rate of stirring, foam sensing and control, weight, microbial biomass, measurement and control of dissolved oxygen, inlet and exit-gas analysis, pH measurement and control, redox, carbon dioxide electrodes; then on-line analysis of other chemical factors — ion-specific sensors, enzyme and microbial electrodes, near infra-red spectroscopy, mass spectrometers; then control systems, from manual through two-position, proportional, integral and derivative and their combinations; then computer applications, with data logging, data analysis and process control. The failure modes worth writing in: a dissolved-oxygen probe drifts and fouls and reads a bulk average in a vessel with gradients; a pH probe drifts and cannot be recalibrated in situ; a weight signal is the most reliable measurement on the vessel and is usually ignored; exit-gas analysis is the most informative and has the longest dead time, because it includes the headspace residence time. That last one is the reason the off-gas soft sensor is a diagnostic rather than a control input.

2. The DO cascade, and why it is a cascade rather than a loop

Dissolved oxygen has four actuators and they must be ordered, because they differ by two orders of magnitude in cost per unit of transfer. Agitation first: cheapest, fastest, and bounded above by shear tolerance and by the motor. Aeration rate second: cheap, but bounded by flooding of the impeller at high gas rate and by foam, and with a side effect — raising the gas rate strips CO₂, which changes pH control. Head pressure third: raises C* in proportion to absolute pressure, which is the week 49 design decision reappearing as a control action, and bounded by the vessel rating and by dissolved CO₂ rising in the same proportion. Oxygen enrichment last: unbounded in effect and the only one with a significant per-kilogram operating cost. The reason for the order is that each step should be exhausted before the next begins, and the reason it is a cascade rather than four independent loops is that they interact — more air both raises k_L a and strips CO₂ and increases foam, so a controller allowed to use them in parallel will hunt. Write the setpoints and the handover points explicitly, including what happens on reverse: coming down from enrichment should be the first thing to go, not the last.

3. The exponential feed, derived rather than quoted

Fed-batch at constant specific growth rate is the one control strategy in biology that has a closed-form answer, and it is worth deriving because every term in it is a decision. If the feed is the only source of the limiting substrate and the substrate concentration in the vessel is negligible — the defining assumption of a fed-batch, and the one that fails first — then the substrate consumed equals the substrate fed, so F·S_F = (µ/Y_XS)·X·V. With X·V = X₀V₀e^(µt) the feed rate is F(t) = (µ/Y_XS)·(X₀V₀/S_F)·e^(µt), which is an exponential ramp with the same exponent as the growth it is driving. The cumulative volume added is F(0)(e^(µt) − 1)/µ, which is why the vessel's headspace sets the run length as firmly as the strain does. Stanbury Ch 2's named section on fed-batch culture gives the taxonomy around it — variable volume, fixed volume, cyclic fed-batch, applications and examples — and the distinction that matters for control is variable against fixed volume, because a fixed-volume fed-batch with a concentrated feed keeps the vessel constant and therefore keeps k_L a constant, which the variable-volume version does not. Shuler §9.3.3 is the quantitative treatment of fed-batch operation and §9.3.1 the chemostat-with-recycle case that is its continuous cousin.

4. The setpoint comes from physiology, and there are two candidate ceilings

Having derived the profile, the only free parameter is µ, and there are two different arguments for where it goes. The overflow argument, from week 40: acetate excretion is threshold-linear in growth rate, so the setpoint should sit below the threshold with a margin, and the threshold is a strain measurement from phase 3 rather than a literature value. The oxygen argument, from week 49: q_O2 = µ/Y_XO + m_O, so the oxygen demand per gram rises linearly with µ and the total demand rises as X·q_O2, which means the vessel's transfer capacity sets a combined ceiling on the product of biomass and growth rate rather than on either alone. These two ceilings are not the same and they bind at different times in the run. Early, when X is low, oxygen is abundant and the overflow threshold binds. Later, when X is high, the oxygen ceiling binds at a growth rate well below the overflow threshold, so the overflow constraint stops being active and the strain's own metabolic preference stops being the issue. The anchor computes where the crossover is. The practical consequence is that a feed profile set once, at the start, from the overflow threshold, will violate the oxygen ceiling in the second half of every high-density run — which is exactly what a DO crash at hour ten is.

5. DO-stat and pH-stat: feedback on a consequence rather than on a model

The exponential profile is open-loop: it assumes Y_XS, X₀ and µ and corrects for none of them, so an error in any of the three compounds over the run. The alternative is to feed on a signal that the substrate's exhaustion produces. DO-stat exploits the fact that when the carbon source runs out, respiration stops and dissolved oxygen spikes; the controller adds feed on a rising-DO event. Its advantage is that it is self-correcting with respect to every parameter in the open-loop model. Its instability risk is specific and worth stating: the loop has a dead time set by the circulation time plus the probe response, and the plant gain changes through the run as biomass rises, so a controller tuned at 10 g L⁻¹ oscillates at 50 g L⁻¹, and the oscillation is in substrate concentration, which means the culture is cycling between starvation and excess — the exact condition the strategy was adopted to avoid. pH-stat works the same way on a different consequence: with ammonia as both nitrogen source and base, consumption of the carbon source acidifies and the base addition tracks growth, so base flow is a feed signal. Its failure mode is that anything else which changes pH — acetate excretion above all — corrupts the signal, so pH-stat is least reliable exactly in the overflow condition it would be most useful for.

6. The off-gas soft sensor, which is the best instrument on the vessel

From inlet and exit gas composition and the gas flow rate, a nitrogen balance gives the exit flow, and the oxygen and carbon dioxide balances then give OUR and CER directly, with no assumption about the culture at all. That makes OUR and CER the only two rates on a production fermenter that are measured rather than inferred, and their ratio RQ = CER/OUR is the single most informative number available in real time. RQ has a value for each metabolic state and the table is the deliverable: on glucose with full respiration, RQ ≈ 1.0; growing on glycerol or another more-reduced substrate, below 1; producing a reduced product such as a lipid or an alcohol, below 1; oxidising a partially-oxidised substrate such as an organic acid, above 1; fermenting — ethanol or acetate overflow — distinctly above 1, because CO₂ is produced without a matching oxygen consumption; and consuming a previously-excreted acid after the carbon source is exhausted, below 1 and falling with OUR. The diagnostic value is in the deviations rather than the value: RQ rising from 1.0 to 1.3 mid-fermentation means fermentative metabolism has started, which means either the feed has outrun the oxygen supply or the oxygen supply has fallen, and the two are distinguished by whether OUR rose or fell at the same moment. Doran §13.4's monitoring-and-control section covers this under state estimation and indirect metabolic control, which is where the soft-sensor idea is treated as a control architecture rather than an instrument.

7. The mass balance as an observer, and what it can and cannot reconstruct

Doran §13.4.5 state estimation and §13.4.4 process modelling are the formal version of what the off-gas sensor does informally: with a stoichiometric model and a subset of measured rates, the unmeasured rates can be reconstructed, and the reconciliation machinery from week 17 is exactly the tool. The useful framing is degrees of freedom. A carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and electron balance over the vessel gives four equations; the measured quantities on a production fermenter are typically OUR, CER, base addition, feed rate and weight, which is five; the unknowns are biomass, product, each by-product and the residual substrate. Count them and the system is usually underdetermined by one or two, which is why on-line biomass estimation by capacitance or optical probe is worth its drift problems — it closes the system. When the system is overdetermined, the redundancy is a consistency check rather than a bonus, and the week 17 habit applies: an inconsistency is information about which measurement is wrong, and the usual answer is the one nobody calibrated. What the observer cannot reconstruct is anything with no elemental signature distinct from biomass, which includes most intracellular product — so for an intracellular product the soft sensor tracks the process but not the thing you are selling.

8. The scale-down experiment, which is the only honest test of a scale-up decision

Shuler §10.2.5 is the scale-down section and its logic is the inverse of §10.2.4's: since the large vessel cannot reproduce the small one, reproduce the large vessel's heterogeneity in a small one and see what the strain does. The standard construction is two compartments — a stirred vessel representing the well-mixed bulk, connected to a plug-flow loop representing the poorly-mixed zone, with the loop's residence time set to the large vessel's circulation time from week 49 and the feed introduced into the loop so that the cells see the same concentration excursion they would see at the feed point. The design parameters are therefore not free: the cycling timescale comes from the circulation time, the amplitude from the feed concentration and the loop's residence time, and the only genuine choices are what to measure and for how long. What to measure is the thing the gradients are supposed to cost — by-product formation, yield, and the plasmid or construct stability from week 33 — and the comparison is against the same strain in a single well-mixed vessel at the same average conditions. A strain that performs identically in both is a strain whose scale-up is a transport problem only. A strain that loses 20% of yield in the two-compartment system has told you the number to put in week 52's model, and that number cannot be obtained any other way.

9. The control philosophy document, which is the week's actual deliverable

Every loop, setpoint, alarm and the response to each alarm, which sounds like documentation and is actually a design review. Three things make it worth writing. It forces the handover points in the DO cascade to be numbers, which they otherwise never are. It forces an alarm to have a response, which exposes the alarms that exist because the signal exists rather than because anything would be done — and an alarm with no response trains operators to ignore alarms, which is the mechanism by which real failures are missed. And it forces the distinction between a control variable and a diagnostic: RQ is a diagnostic, because closing a loop on a quantity with the off-gas dead time produces oscillation, whereas dissolved oxygen is a control variable despite being the less informative of the two. Writing which is which, and why, is the part of this week that transfers directly into the capstone dossier, because it is the part a reviewer can check without rerunning anything.

Worked anchor

Where the exponential feed has to stop, and what replaces it.

Basis. The week 49 vessel: 50 m³ working volume, starting the feed at V₀ = 30 m³ and X₀ = 20 g L⁻¹, so X₀V₀ = 600 kg dry weight. Feed of 600 g L⁻¹ glucose. Biomass yield Y_XS = 0.45 g g⁻¹. Oxygen demand q_O2 = µ/Y_XO + m_O with Y_XO = 1.0 g DW per g O₂ and m_O = 0.01 g O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹.

Step 1 — the open-loop profile at the overflow-based setpoint. Phase 3 put the acetate threshold at about 0.2 h⁻¹; take µ = 0.12 h⁻¹, a 40% margin below it. F(0) = (µ/Y_XS)(X₀V₀/S_F) = (0.12/0.45)(600,000 g)/(600 g L⁻¹) = 267 L h⁻¹.

The profile: at 4 h, 431 L h⁻¹ with 1.37 m³ added and 970 kg of biomass; at 8 h, 696 L h⁻¹ with 3.58 m³ added and 1,567 kg; at 12 h, 1,126 L h⁻¹ with 7.16 m³ added and 2,532 kg. At 12 h the volume is 37.2 m³ and the cell density 68 g L⁻¹. The vessel has headspace for about 20 more hours at this rate before it reaches 50 m³, so volume is not the binding constraint.

Step 2 — the oxygen ceiling, which is. At 12 h, q_O2 = 0.12 + 0.01 = 0.13 g O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ and X = 68 g L⁻¹, so OUR = 8.8 kg O₂ m⁻³ h⁻¹.

Week 49's vessel delivers, at 2 bar absolute and k_L a = 500 h⁻¹ with C_L held at 2.1 g m⁻³, an OTR of 500 × (14.0 − 2.1) = 5,950 g m⁻³ h⁻¹ = 5.95 kg m⁻³ h⁻¹.

So the demand at 12 h is 1.5 times the supply. The run does not reach 12 hours at µ = 0.12.

Step 3 — where it actually stops. The ceiling is X(µ + 0.01) ≤ 5,950 g m⁻³ h⁻¹ with X in g m⁻³, i.e. X ≤ 5,950/(µ + m_O) in g m⁻³. At µ = 0.12 that is X ≤ 45.8 g L⁻¹. Working back through X·V = 600e^(0.12t) and V = 30 + 0.267(e^(0.12t) − 1)/0.12: solving X(t) = 45.8 gives t ≈ 8.9 h, with V = 34.2 m³ and X·V = 1,750 kg. So the exponential phase lasts just under nine hours and delivers 46 g L⁻¹, not the 68 g L⁻¹ the profile would reach at twelve.

Step 4 — what replaces it. Past the ceiling the correct policy is constant OUR: feed at whatever rate holds OTR = OUR, which means the total oxygen consumption is pinned at 5.95 kg m⁻³ h⁻¹ × V and the growth rate falls as biomass rises. Explicitly, µ(t) = OTR_max/X(t) − m_O, so at 60 g L⁻¹, µ = 5,950/60,000 − 0.01 = 0.089 h⁻¹; at 80 g L⁻¹, 0.064 h⁻¹; at 100 g L⁻¹, 0.0495 h⁻¹. Biomass then grows almost linearly rather than exponentially, because X·µ is constant, which is the signature of an oxygen-transfer-limited fed-batch and is what the biomass curve of nearly every high-density process actually looks like.

Time to 80 g L⁻¹: integrating dX·V/dt = (OTR_max/Y_XO)·V with V rising slowly, the biomass increment from 46 to 80 g L⁻¹ in roughly 36 m³ is about 1,200 kg, at a production rate of about 5.95 × 36 = 214 kg O₂ h⁻¹ and therefore about 214 kg DW h⁻¹, giving about 5.6 h. So 80 g L⁻¹ is reached at roughly 14.5 h against the 11 h the pure exponential would have predicted — a 30% longer run for the same endpoint, and the difference is entirely oxygen.

Step 5 — the three ways to implement constant OUR, costed. (i) Compute it open-loop from the model above and program the pump. Free, and wrong by however much Y_XO, m_O and X₀ are wrong, with the errors compounding. (ii) Close the loop on dissolved oxygen: feed down when DO falls below setpoint. Self-correcting with respect to every parameter, and this is the only version that is actually robust, because DO is the variable the ceiling is expressed in. Its instability risk is the dead time — circulation time plus probe response, tens of seconds at this scale — against a plant gain that rises with biomass, so the loop must be retuned or gain-scheduled through the run. (iii) Close the loop on off-gas OUR directly, which is the physically correct variable. Rejected: the off-gas dead time includes headspace residence, which at this scale is minutes, so a feed loop closed on OUR will oscillate. Use OUR as the diagnostic that tells you whether the DO loop is doing what you think.

Step 6 — the arithmetic that chooses the setpoint, stated once. Before the ceiling, µ is chosen from overflow: as high as the acetate threshold allows, because a higher µ gets to the ceiling sooner and the time before the ceiling is the only time the vessel is not oxygen-limited. After the ceiling, µ is not chosen at all — it is the quotient of a fixed oxygen supply and a rising biomass, and the only lever is the oxygen supply. Which means the single most valuable control action in a high-density fed-batch is not in the feed loop. It is the head pressure and the enrichment valve, which is to say it is week 49's design decision, exercised. A control philosophy that treats the feed as the primary manipulated variable and enrichment as a last resort has the hierarchy upside down for the second half of the run.

What this leaves out, and it matters: the whole calculation treats the vessel as well mixed, and week 49 put the circulation time at 20-60 s. The cells near the feed point see glucose at far above the vessel average for a few seconds per pass, and that is precisely the condition that triggers overflow regardless of the average growth rate. So the margin below the acetate threshold in step 1 is not conservatism about µ, it is an allowance for a gradient the model does not contain, and the only way to size it is the two-compartment scale-down experiment.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • the week 49 vessel and its oxygen supply carried forward unchanged, including the 2 bar absolute decision
  • feed is the only source of the limiting substrate and its concentration in the vessel is negligible, which is what makes the exponential profile exact
  • Y_XS = 0.45 g g⁻¹ and Y_XO = 1.0 g DW per g O₂ both constant through the run, when maintenance makes both fall at low μ
  • feed glucose at 600 g L⁻¹ and no volume change on mixing
  • the acetate overflow threshold at about 0.2 h⁻¹, from phase 3 for this strain, with μ set 40% below it
  • k_L a held at 500 h⁻¹ through the run, which a variable-volume fed-batch does not do as the liquid height rises
  • the vessel treated as well mixed, so the computed μ ceiling ignores the local excursion at the feed point — the margin below the overflow threshold is the allowance for it and is not derived
  • product formation neglected in the biomass and oxygen balances, so the figures are for a growth phase rather than a production phase
  • no evaporative loss and no change in broth density as cell density rises

Common misconceptions

  • That the feed profile is set by the strain. It is set by the strain only until the oxygen ceiling binds, which in a high-density aerobic process happens in the first half of the run. After that, growth rate is the quotient of a fixed oxygen supply and a rising biomass, falling as 1/X, and no feed-controller tuning changes it — only pressure or enrichment does. A DO crash at hour ten is this arithmetic arriving on schedule, not a control fault.
  • That RQ is a control variable. It is the most informative number on the vessel and the worst one to close a loop on, because the off-gas measurement carries a dead time that includes headspace residence — minutes at production scale. Close the feed loop on dissolved oxygen, which has a dead time of tens of seconds, and use RQ to check that the DO loop is doing what you intended.
  • That DO-stat is self-correcting and therefore safe. It is self-correcting with respect to the parameters of the open-loop model, which is its real advantage, and it is not safe, because its plant gain rises with biomass through the run while its dead time stays fixed. A loop tuned at 10 g L⁻¹ oscillates at 50, and the oscillation is in substrate concentration — cycling the culture between starvation and excess, which is the condition the strategy was adopted to avoid.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. Your RQ rises from 1.0 to 1.3 mid-fermentation. What happened, and what is your control response?

A. CO₂ is being produced without a matching oxygen consumption, which means fermentative metabolism has started — on glucose that is acetate or ethanol overflow. Two causes produce the same RQ signature and the discriminator is what OUR did at the same moment. If OUR fell while CER held or rose, the oxygen supply failed: a blocked sparger, impeller flooding, a foam-over onto the exhaust filter, or a drop in head pressure. If OUR held at its ceiling while CER rose, the demand outran the supply: the feed is now driving a growth rate the vessel cannot oxygenate, which is the anchor's step 3 arriving. Response differs accordingly. For a supply failure, restore the supply and do not touch the feed until you know why it fell, because cutting the feed masks the fault. For a demand overrun, cut the feed to the rate that holds OUR at the transfer ceiling and, if the product phase needs the biomass, buy supply with head pressure and then enrichment in that order. In both cases check the dissolved oxygen trace against the RQ trace: if DO fell before RQ rose, it is supply; if RQ rose while DO was already at setpoint and the controller was saturated, it is demand. And record the acetate, because overflow carbon does not come back and the yield loss goes into week 52's model as a number rather than a worry.

Q. Derive the exponential feed rate for a target specific growth rate, and state the assumption that fails first.

A. Take the limiting substrate as supplied only by the feed and consumed with a constant biomass yield, and take its concentration in the vessel as negligible, which is what makes the culture substrate-limited rather than substrate-sufficient. Then the substrate fed equals the substrate consumed: F·S_F = (µ/Y_XS)·X·V. Biomass at constant µ is X·V = X₀V₀e^(µt), so F(t) = (µ/Y_XS)(X₀V₀/S_F)e^(µt), with cumulative volume F(0)(e^(µt) − 1)/µ. For the worked basis — X₀V₀ = 600 kg, S_F = 600 g L⁻¹, Y_XS = 0.45, µ = 0.12 h⁻¹ — F(0) = 267 L h⁻¹, doubling every 5.8 h. The assumption that fails first is not the yield and not the negligible residual substrate; it is that the vessel is well mixed. The derivation prices the feed against the vessel average, while the cells passing the feed point see a concentration far above it for a few seconds per circulation, and overflow responds to the local concentration rather than the average. That is why the µ setpoint carries a margin below the measured acetate threshold and why the margin can only be sized by a two-compartment scale-down experiment. The second assumption to fail is constancy of Y_XS, which falls as maintenance becomes a larger fraction at low µ — so the open-loop profile systematically over-feeds late in the run, which is a second reason to close the loop on dissolved oxygen rather than trust the ramp.

Q. Design the two-compartment scale-down experiment for this process, and say what result would change your scale-up decision.

A. A well-mixed stirred vessel representing the bulk, connected to a plug-flow loop representing the feed zone, with the feed introduced into the loop. Set the loop residence time to the large vessel's circulation time from week 49 — 20 to 60 s for 50 m³ — and set the loop's glucose concentration from the feed rate and loop volume so the excursion amplitude matches what a cell passing the feed point would see. Run the same strain in a single well-mixed vessel at the same average dilution, temperature, pH and dissolved oxygen as the control. Measure three things over a full fed-batch: acetate or by-product accumulation, biomass and product yield on substrate, and construct stability by the week 33 assay. The result that changes the decision is a yield or stability gap between the two vessels. If the two-compartment system matches the control, the scale-up is a transport problem only and constant P/V with its degraded mixing time is acceptable. If it loses yield, the choice between Shuler's four criteria changes — constant impeller speed protects mixing time at a 25-fold cost in P/V, which then has to be weighed against the measured yield loss rather than guessed at — and the feed point itself becomes a design variable, since multiple feed points or a feed into the impeller discharge reduce the amplitude of the excursion without touching the criterion. If it loses construct stability, the answer is upstream of the vessel entirely and is week 33's: integrate the construct, because no mixing regime fixes segregational loss.
