The cell as a chemical plant

Week content

Case thread: Map the moving-tray reactor onto the cell. Which quantities are process variables, and which would be intracellular? Open the case thread.

This week is a translation exercise, not a biology lesson. You already think in reactors, holdups, residence times and recycle loops; the cell is all of those at a scale where the numbers are unfamiliar. The aim is that by Friday you can state the size, composition and timescales of a cell from memory, say which compartment a pathway could live in and what that costs, and defend a host choice with numbers rather than with habit.

Core ideas

1. The cell as a reactor, with numbers you should memorise

An E. coli cell is roughly a cylinder 2 µm long and 0.8 µm across, so about 1 µm³ = 1 femtolitre. It is 70% water; the dry mass is around 3×10⁻¹³ g, of which 55% is protein, 20% RNA, 3% DNA, and the rest lipid, murein and small molecules. That gives about 2.5 million protein molecules and 10,000–70,000 ribosomes per cell depending on growth rate. Doubling time on glucose minimal medium is 40–60 minutes; on rich medium, 20. A yeast cell is 30–50 times larger by volume and divides in 90–140 minutes. The single most useful derived number: one molecule per E. coli cell is about 1.7 nM, because 1/(6.02×10²³ × 10⁻¹⁵ L) = 1.66×10⁻⁹ M. Carry that conversion; you will use it weekly.

2. Compartments are the engineering decision, not the biology trivia

E. coli has cytoplasm and, because it is Gram-negative, a periplasm between inner and outer membranes. The periplasm is oxidising, so it is where disulfide-bonded proteins fold correctly, and it is a secretion destination industrially. Yeast adds mitochondrion (acetyl-CoA, TCA, iron-sulfur clusters, and its own genome), endoplasmic reticulum (membrane-bound cytochrome P450s and their reductase — the reason terpenoid and alkaloid routes go to yeast), peroxisome (beta-oxidation, and a useful isolated compartment for toxic intermediates), vacuole (storage and degradation), and the nucleus. Each compartment you put a pathway into creates a transport problem for every substrate, product and cofactor the pathway needs, and the cofactor pools differ between compartments. That is the trade: compartmentalisation gives you isolation and the right chemical environment, and charges you transport.

3. Every molecule crossing a membrane is paid for, in one of two currencies

Transport against a gradient costs either ATP directly — the ABC transporters hydrolyse it — or the proton-motive force, which symporters and antiporters spend and which respiration paid ATP-equivalents to build. The PMF is an electrochemical gradient with two components, the pH difference and the membrane potential, together worth roughly 150–200 mV in a respiring bacterium. The group translocation route is a third option: the phosphotransferase system phosphorylates glucose during transit at the cost of one PEP, which is why PTS uptake halves the ceiling on any PEP-derived product. Carry one sentence from this week: nothing enters or leaves a cell for free, and the bill is paid in ATP, in PMF, or in a glycolytic intermediate. Week 8 quantifies the PMF, week 15 works the transport stoichiometries, and week 12 connects it back to oxygen.

4. What the cell is made of, and what making it costs

Four macromolecule classes. Proteins: 20 amino acids, peptide bonds, about 4 ATP-equivalents per residue to polymerise. Nucleic acids: 4 bases each, phosphodiester bonds, expensive per monomer because the bases themselves are costly to synthesise. Lipids: fatty acids on a glycerol or sphingoid backbone, built from acetyl-CoA and reduced with NADPH, so they are the most reduced and therefore most expensive cell component per gram. Polysaccharides: murein in the bacterial wall, glycogen as storage. The orders matter: protein dominates the mass and therefore dominates the biosynthetic demand, which is why amino acid supply and the twelve precursors govern growth yield. You will build this into a biomass equation in week 16.

5. The bacterial growth laws, which almost everything later rests on

Ribosome content scales linearly with growth rate. These are E. coli figures and the organism matters: a cell at µ = 0.2 h⁻¹ devotes perhaps 10% of protein to ribosomes, and one at µ = 1.5 h⁻¹ nearly 40%, from the classical composition tables of Bremer and Dennis and the growth laws of Scott et al. (Science 2010, 330:1099–1102). Take the numbers for any other organism, or for E. coli under stress or in a non-minimal medium, from a source for those conditions. The proteome behaves as a budget partitioned between a ribosomal sector, a metabolic sector that supplies precursors, and an unused or housekeeping remainder — and the sectors sum to one. Three consequences you will meet repeatedly. Overflow metabolism in week 13 is the cell choosing a pathway that yields less ATP per glucose but more ATP per gram of protein invested. Proteome-constrained models in week 31 formalise exactly this budget. And metabolic burden in week 34 is quantitatively just the fraction of that budget your pathway takes, which is why the week 1 copy-number calculation is the foundation of the burden argument rather than an arithmetic curiosity. Learn the linear ribosome relation as a number now; it explains more of what follows than any single fact in the course.

6. Timescales, and why 'steady state' is defensible

Enzyme turnover: 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵ s per catalytic cycle. Metabolite pool turnover: ATP about 1 s, glycolytic intermediates seconds, amino acid pools minutes. Transcript half-life: 2–8 minutes in bacteria. Protein half-life: hours, or dilution by growth if the protein is stable. Generation time: tens of minutes to hours. This hierarchy is the entire justification for flux balance analysis in week 23: metabolite pools equilibrate in seconds while the growth state changes over hours, so treating metabolism as quasi-steady is sound. It is also why metabolomics in week 27 is brutally hard — you must stop metabolism faster than ATP turns over.

7. Host selection as a design decision

Five questions decide a host, in roughly this order. Does it natively supply the precursor and cofactor your pathway needs, or can it be made to? Does it tolerate the product at the titre your economics requires? Is the genetic toolkit mature enough that a build cycle takes weeks rather than months? Is there a fermentation and regulatory record at scale? And can it use the feedstock you can actually buy? Note that tool maturity ranks third, not first — which is why C. glutamicum beats E. coli for amino acids despite weaker tools, and why food applications go to lactic acid bacteria despite far weaker tools. You will build the full comparison matrix in weeks 40–46.

8. The central dogma as a set of engineering handles

DNA → RNA → protein, with each arrow a place you will intervene. Gene copy number and promoter strength set transcription; ribosome binding site and mRNA secondary structure set translation initiation, which is usually the real bottleneck; codon usage sets elongation; folding and proteolysis set how much active enzyme results. The practical consequence to internalise now: transcript abundance is a poor predictor of flux, because four independent steps sit between the transcript and a working catalyst. This is why the omics integration in week 39 is harder than it sounds, and why the week 34 tuning problem is quantitative rather than a matter of 'more promoter'.

9. The vocabulary that the whole course runs on

Titre: product concentration, g/L, which sets downstream cost. Rate or productivity: g L⁻¹ h⁻¹, which sets how much plant you need. Yield: g product per g substrate, which sets raw material cost and is usually expressed as a fraction of the theoretical maximum. Specific rates carry a q and are per gram dry weight per hour: qS for uptake, qP for production, qO₂ for oxygen. µ is specific growth rate in h⁻¹. Y with subscripts is a yield coefficient. Learn to say which of titre, rate and yield a given intervention improves — most interventions improve one and damage another, and week 51 is where you find out which one the economics punishes.

Worked anchor

How many copies of an enzyme does a cell need to carry a given flux? Take 2 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ and an enzyme running at 50 s⁻¹.

Flux per cell = 2 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ × 3×10⁻¹³ gDW = 6×10⁻¹³ mmol/h = 6×10⁻¹⁶ mol/h. Per second: 1.67×10⁻¹⁹ mol/s × 6.02×10²³ = 1.0×10⁵ molecules/s. Copies needed = 1.0×10⁵ / 50 = 2,000. Mass = 2,000 × 45,000 g/mol / 6.02×10²³ = 1.5×10⁻¹⁶ g = 0.15 fg, against roughly 180 fg of protein per cell — so 0.08% of the proteome.

The lesson is scale: one enzyme step is cheap, but a twelve-enzyme pathway at ten times this flux needs about 10% of the cell's protein, and that is where burden starts to bite. Keep this calculation; it recurs in weeks 31 and 34.

Assumptions behind this calculation

  • single cell dry mass 3×10⁻¹³ g and 180 fg protein per cell
  • the enzyme runs at its full kcat in vivo, one catalytic site per molecule
  • subunit mass 45 kDa
  • flux carried by this one step alone

Common misconceptions

  • That a bigger cell is a better factory. Yeast's larger volume comes with a slower doubling time and a lower surface-to-volume ratio for transport; neither is obviously better and the choice depends on the chemistry.
  • That tool maturity picks the host. It ranks behind precursor supply, tolerance and industrial record — a fast build cycle is worth little if the strain dies at 30 g/L.
  • That the periplasm is wasted space. It is an oxidising compartment with no equivalent in the yeast cytosol, and for disulfide-bonded products it is the reason to choose E. coli at all.

Check yourself before moving on

Q. From memory: cell volume, dry mass, protein fraction, and the nanomolar concentration of a single molecule per E. coli cell.

A. About 1 µm³ = 1 fL; dry mass roughly 3×10⁻¹³ g; protein about 55% of dry mass, so ~1.65×10⁻¹³ g, giving around 2.5 million protein molecules; and one molecule per cell ≈ 1.7 nM, from 1/(6.02×10²³ × 10⁻¹⁵ L).

Q. Name four yeast compartments and one product class you would deliberately target to each, with the transport problem that creates.

A. Cytosol: most pathways by default; no transport problem, but competition with native metabolism. Mitochondrion: acetyl-CoA-derived products and anything needing the TCA intermediates directly; the problem is importing substrates and exporting product across two membranes, and the cofactor pools differ from the cytosol. Endoplasmic reticulum: membrane-bound P450 chemistry, so terpenoids and alkaloids; the problem is that soluble substrates must reach a membrane-embedded active site and NADPH must be supplied at the cytosolic face. Peroxisome: isolation of toxic or volatile intermediates, and beta-oxidation chemistry; the problem is that peroxisomal import is restricted to folded proteins via PTS signals and small-molecule transport is poorly characterised.

Q. A pathway runs at 5 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ through an enzyme with kcat 20 s⁻¹ at half saturation. What fraction of the proteome does that one step need?

A. Effective rate 10 s⁻¹. Flux per cell = 5 × 3×10⁻¹³ mmol/h = 1.5×10⁻¹² mmol/h = 2.5×10⁵ molecules/s. Copies = 2.5×10⁴. At 45 kDa that is 1.9×10⁻¹⁵ g ≈ 1.9 fg, about 1% of the 180 fg protein complement. One step at 1%; a ten-step pathway at this flux approaches 10%, which is where growth rate starts to fall measurably.
