Statistics and experimental design

Seventeen sections, all in Python. Not a statistics course: the specific inferential and design decisions a strain engineer makes, placed where the decision lives in the 55-week plan.

How to read this handbook, and where each part belongs

This closes a real gap. Across the whole course before this document, ANOVA, t-tests, response surface methodology, power analysis, Plackett–Burman, Tukey, Dunnett, Bonferroni and the phrase p-value appeared zero times. What the course did already have is the quantitative half — replication and randomisation discussed in 28 and 21 places, bootstrap intervals, confidence intervals, a chi-squared goodness-of-fit anchor with its degrees of freedom in week 29, parameter identifiability in week 30, Poisson library coverage in phase 6, and the noise floor and calibration sections of the machine learning handbook. So the gap is specifically inferential testing and designed experiments, and this handbook covers that and cross-references the rest rather than repeating it.

One principle governs the whole document: the design determines the analysis, not the other way round. Every ordering decision here follows from it — §1 before §3, because the replicate structure decides what any test can claim; §4 before §5, because multiplicity correction spends power it cannot create; §6 before §7, because a response surface fitted outside the optimum region is a well-fitted model of the wrong place. A statistical method cannot rescue an experiment that was not designed to answer the question, and the commonest request a statistician receives — what test should I run on this data — is usually unanswerable for that reason. The corollary is that none of the defaults below is a rule. Each is a good starting point with stated conditions, and §3 and §9 say what those conditions are.

Do not read it as a block, and do not run it as eight consecutive weeks. Design of experiments is only learnable against a decision, and a design taught before there is anything to optimise is a formalism. The placement that works:

1. Variation: what varies, and what your replicate structure claims

Before the replicate question, the study question. Montgomery, Runger and Hubele, Engineering Statistics, 5th edn, distinguish three ways data arrive — §1-2.1 the retrospective study, mining records that were collected for another purpose; §1-2.2 the observational study, watching the process without disturbing it; and §1-2.3 the designed experiment, where you set the factors — and §1-2.4 random samples. The distinction decides what any later test can claim, and most of this handbook applies only to the third. Mining a fermenter's historian for "what correlates with titre" is a retrospective study and cannot establish that anything causes anything, however many replicates it appears to have. Their §1-3, Mechanistic and Empirical Models, is the best short statement anywhere of the relationship this whole course lives on: a flux model is mechanistic, a response surface is empirical, and each is appropriate where the other is not.

Three sources of variation, and conflating them is the commonest error in a first-year student's data. A technical replicate is the same biological material measured more than once — two injections of one extract — and it estimates measurement precision only. A biological replicate is an independent culture from an independent colony or vial, and it estimates the variation your conclusion is actually about. A process replicate is an independent fermentation, including medium batch, inoculum preparation and vessel, and for anything that will be quoted as a titre it is the only honest unit.

Pseudo-replication is the formal name for getting this wrong, and the reference is Hurlbert, Pseudoreplication and the design of ecological field experiments, Ecological Monographs 54:187 (1984) — written about field ecology and read ever since as the general statement. The error: treating measurements that share a source of variation as independent, which inflates the degrees of freedom and shrinks the interval until almost anything is significant. Three flasks sampled three times each gives n = 3, not 9. A single bioreactor sampled hourly gives n = 1 for any claim about the strain.

The test to apply to your own data, in one sentence: if I repeated the whole experiment from the glycerol stock, which numbers would change? Those are your replicates. Everything inside that is a measurement of one replicate, and it belongs in the error bar on that point rather than in the count.

What to do with technical replicates rather than discard them. Average them to one value per biological replicate and carry their spread separately as the measurement noise floor — which is exactly the quantity the machine learning handbook §3 requires before any model is fitted, and the two uses are the same calculation.

2. Randomisation, blocking and the confounders a plate introduces

Position is a variable. Edge wells evaporate, plate corners run hot, the first column is pipetted before the operator's hand is tired, and a 96-well plate read in one pass has a time gradient across it. Any of these will be mistaken for a treatment effect if treatment maps onto position — and in practice treatment almost always maps onto position, because it is convenient to lay a strain series out in a row.

Three remedies, in increasing order of rigour. Randomise the assignment of strain to well, with the seed recorded so the layout is reproducible. Block on the nuisance factor — run each strain once per plate rather than all replicates on one plate — which removes the plate effect from the comparison instead of averaging over it. Include controls in every block: the parent strain and a medium blank on each plate, which lets you measure the plate effect rather than assume it away.

Worked example of what blocking buys. Six strains, three replicates. Laid out as three plates each containing all six strains, with the strain positions randomised within each plate: the plate-to-plate variation becomes a block term in the ANOVA and comes out of the residual, so the strain comparison is made within plates. Laid out as one plate per replicate set — all of strain A on plate 1 — plate and strain are completely confounded and no analysis can separate them. The second layout is faster to pipette and cannot be rescued afterwards, which is why this section belongs in week 1 rather than in week 27.

The formal versions, and where to read them. Montgomery §5-8.1 is the completely randomised experiment and §5-8.2 the randomised complete block, which is the design this section argues for, with the analysis that goes with it — the block term coming out of the residual is what buys the sensitivity. Read those two sections rather than inventing the analysis, and note that the F-test there is the same machinery as §5 below.

The diagnostic to run afterwards, always. Fit the nuisance factors — plate, row, column, day, operator — as predictors and see what they explain. If plate column is a stronger predictor than your treatment, you have learned something important about the assay and nothing about biology. The machine learning handbook §3 makes the same point from the modelling side: plate and batch are features until proven otherwise.

3. Comparing two strains, and why the default test is Welch's

Use Welch's t-test, not Student's. Student's assumes equal variances in the two groups, which strains routinely violate — a high-producing strain is usually also more variable — and the equal-variance assumption buys almost nothing when it holds while costing real error rates when it does not. `scipy.stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)` is the default to type. Do not run a preliminary variance test to choose between them; that two-stage procedure has worse properties than simply always using Welch's.

Work on the log scale for anything multiplicative. Titres, fold changes, specific rates and expression levels have errors that scale with the mean, so the difference that is constant across the range is the ratio. Take log₁₀ or ln of the measurements, do the test there, and back-transform the result into a fold change with its interval. This also fixes the asymmetry problem: a ±30% uncertainty is not symmetric in linear space, which costs about 10% in a cost model — the 9.6% figure is in week 52's Monte Carlo step, not in the machine learning handbook, whose §8 covers calibration instead.

Pair the design when the design is paired. If the comparison is before and after on the same clone, the same medium batch or the same operator, the paired t-test on the differences removes that shared variation and is substantially more powerful than treating the groups as independent; Montgomery §5-4 is the reference. The error to avoid is the reverse — pairing data that are not paired, which manufactures precision.

Three intervals, and the one you want is usually not the confidence interval. A confidence interval covers the mean. A prediction interval (Montgomery §4-8.1) covers the next single observation, and is much wider. A tolerance interval (§4-8.2) covers a stated proportion of the population. A process specification almost always needs the second or third: "the mean titre is 48 to 52 g L⁻¹" says nothing about whether the next batch will clear 45, which is the question a plant asks. Say which you computed, because the three can differ by more than twofold on the same data.

The assumptions behind these two defaults, which are conditions and not formalities. Welch assumes approximate normality of the group means, which the central limit theorem makes tolerable for mild skew but not for a bimodal response — and at n = 4 you cannot check normality from the data, so the defence is the error model and the design, not a normality test, which at that size has almost no power and whose non-rejection means nothing. The log transform requires strictly positive measurements and a multiplicative error model you can argue for: constant coefficient of variation across the range is the usual evidence, visible as a funnel in the residuals of the untransformed fit. It fails outright on zero-producers, which need the two-part model of §2 rather than an offset chosen to make the logarithm defined — adding a constant changes the answer and there is no neutral value for it. And neither default addresses dependence: repeated measurements from one fermentation are not biological replicates under any analysis, and blocking (§2) removes one nuisance factor rather than a nested hierarchy, which §15 now treats properly. State which conditions you checked and which you assumed.

Report the interval, not the p-value alone. A p-value answers "could this be noise"; an interval answers "how big is it, and how well do I know that". The sentence a supervisor wants is the mutant produced 1.41-fold more, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.64, n = 4 biological replicates — the figures worked out two paragraphs below, so that the template and the example carry the same numbers — and the sentence that tells them you are new is the increase was significant (p < 0.05). If the interval includes a fold change you would not act on, the result is not useful however small the p-value.

Worked example. Parent 2.10, 2.31, 1.98, 2.22 g L⁻¹; mutant 2.95, 3.40, 2.71, 3.12. On the log₁₀ scale the means are 0.3322 and 0.4821, difference 0.1499, so the fold change is 10^0.1499 = 1.41. The pooled within-group SD on the log scale is 0.0358, giving a standard error on the difference of 0.0358·√(1/4+1/4) = 0.0253; Welch's test returns 5.4 effective degrees of freedom and p = 0.0015, and at t = 2.52, the two-sided 95% value on 5.39 degrees of freedom, the interval on the log difference is 0.086 to 0.213 — a fold change of 1.22 to 1.64. Three things follow. The effect is real at n = 4. The interval is wide enough that you cannot distinguish a 22% improvement from a 64% one. And if the decision needs the difference between those two, the experiment needs more replicates, which is §4.

4. Power and sample size, which supervisors ask and almost nobody can answer

The question is: how many flasks before I start? It has four inputs and any three fix the fourth: the effect size you care about, the standard deviation of the measurement, the significance level, and the power. For two groups compared with a t-test, a serviceable approximation is

n per group ≈ 2 (z_α/2 + z_β)² (σ / δ)²

with z = 1.96 at α = 0.05 two-sided and 1.28 at 80% power, so the bracket is (1.96 + 1.28)² = 10.5 and the rule of thumb is n ≈ 21 (σ/δ)² per group. That is a normal approximation and only a starting point: the exact calculation uses the noncentral t distribution, and the two disagree in both directions at small n. Always say which one a number came from, and take the exact figure from `statsmodels.stats.power.TTestIndPower().solve_power()` or a short noncentral-t search of your own.

Worked example, in the units of a shake-flask experiment. You want to detect a 20% improvement in titre, and your own replicate data gives a coefficient of variation of 12%. On the log scale δ = ln(1.20) = 0.182 and σ = 0.12, so the standardised effect is d = δ/σ = 1.52 and (σ/δ)² = 0.435:

effectnormal approximation, n ≈ 21(σ/δ)²exact, noncentral tuse
20%9.1 → 10 per arm8 per arm (power 0.81)8, or 10 for margin
50%1.8 → 2 per arm3 per arm (power 0.87)3

Note which way each error runs. At a 20% effect the approximation is conservative by two flasks; at 50% it is optimistic, returning 1.8 where the exact answer is 3, because it ignores both the t distribution's extra width at tiny n and the fact that n must be a whole number of at least 2. That is what the rule of thumb's "add one or two" is for, and it is why the exact figure is the one that belongs in a design document. The planning direction is unchanged: halving the detectable effect quadruples the flask count.

The two consequences people miss. First, reducing σ is four times as valuable as it looks, because n scales as σ². An extra day spent on assay precision or on standardising the inoculum buys more than an extra week of flasks. Second, an underpowered experiment is worse than none, because the only effects it can detect are ones larger than the truth, so a "significant" result from an underpowered study is systematically overestimated — the winner's curse, which the machine learning handbook §10 treats as regression to the mean in a DBTL round.

For tests other than the two-sample t, read the sample-size section of that test. Montgomery gives "Type II error and choice of sample size" as its own subsection for each procedure — §4-4.2 for a normal mean with known variance, §4-5.2 for unknown variance, §4-7.2 for a proportion, §5-2.2 and §5-3.2 for the two-sample cases — with operating-characteristic curves that answer the same question for each. The structure is worth noticing: the book treats sample size as part of every test rather than as a separate topic, which is the habit to copy.

Report power prospectively, never retrospectively. Post-hoc power computed from the observed effect is a deterministic function of the p-value and adds nothing. If the result is null, the useful statement is the interval: we can exclude an improvement larger than 18%, which is a finding.

Power by simulation, when the formula does not fit. Gelman and Hill Ch 20 computes power by simulating the design and the analysis, which is the right method when the analysis is a mixed model, a ratio, or a screen with a threshold; Ch 7 is the simulation primer. Use the formula for the simple cases and the simulation when the design is not one the formula covers.

5. Many comparisons: ANOVA, post-hoc tests, and when neither is the right tool

One-way ANOVA with Tukey is for a handful of strains. Six strains, four replicates, one factor: `statsmodels.formula.api.ols("logtitre ~ strain", data).fit()` then `anova_lm`, followed by Tukey's HSD if the F-test rejects. The F-test answers "is anything different", which is rarely the question; the post-hoc comparison answers the question, and the choice of post-hoc matters. If every candidate is being compared against the parent rather than against each other, use Dunnett's test, not Tukey's — Dunnett is built for many-to-one and is correspondingly more powerful, and using Tukey for a many-to-one design throws away sensitivity for comparisons you do not care about.

Two-way ANOVA is where the interaction lives, and the interaction is usually the point. Strain × carbon source, or strain × induction level: the interaction term asks whether the strain ranking changes with the condition, which is exactly the portability question Lee §22.5 raises and the transfer question the machine learning handbook §1 says cannot be assumed. A significant interaction means a main-effect statement about strains is not safe, and reporting the main effect anyway is a common and visible error.

Where ANOVA is the wrong instrument, and this is the correction worth internalising. For a library screen of a hundred or more variants, all-pairs post-hoc testing is both computationally silly and conceptually wrong: you are not asking whether variants differ, you are selecting the best ones. The tools there are false discovery rate control — Benjamini–Hochberg, `statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests(p, method="fdr_bh")` — rather than family-wise error control, because with 500 candidates a Bonferroni correction at α = 0.05 demands p < 10⁻⁴ and finds nothing; plus top-N selection against the replicate noise floor, and the winner's-curse correction, which is that the selected top variant's measured value is biased high and must be re-measured before it is believed. Phase 6's screening ladder gives the throughput, the machine learning handbook §9 gives the batch-selection rule, and this section gives the error control.

Worked example of the multiplicity arithmetic, simulated rather than asserted. 384 variants, each compared to the parent, true improvements in 20 of them, four biological replicates per variant, CV 12% on the log scale — the handbook's own numbers from §4. But the row that matters is the first, and it is not the comfortable claim. At a 20% true improvement with four replicates, no correction rescues the screen — BH recovers fewer than three of the twenty and Bonferroni about one — because the experiment is underpowered before multiplicity enters at all, exactly as §4 says: 20% at CV 12% needs eight replicates per arm and this screen has four. Multiplicity correction decides how you spend the power you have; it cannot create any. So the sequence is §4 before §5: fix the replication against the effect you care about, then choose the correction. State which correction you used and why, in terms of what happens next to the list — false discovery rate when the output is "re-test these in triplicate", family-wise when one claim rests on one comparison.

Re-run this with your own noise floor and replication rather than reading the table. It is twenty lines of `numpy` and `scipy.stats`, and it is the first exercise in Brief 18.

Where the book stops. Montgomery §5-8.1 is the completely randomised experiment and §5-8.2 the randomised complete block; both are the ANOVA these comparisons follow. The book does not cover Tukey or Dunnett — neither is in its index — so use the statsmodels pairwise_tukeyhsd and multipletests documentation for the implementation, or Montgomery's larger Design and Analysis of Experiments for the derivation. The F-test is the book's job; choosing the post-hoc comparison is this handbook's.

6. Screening designs: finding the three factors that matter out of ten

One factor at a time is the thing to stop doing, and the reason is arithmetic rather than fashion. OFAT with ten factors at two levels and three replicates costs 60 runs, estimates no interactions at all, and finds an optimum only if the factors are independent — which in a medium they are not, since nitrogen source and carbon source interact through the C:N ratio by construction.

Full factorial at two levels is 2^k runs: 8 for three factors, 1,024 for ten. It estimates every interaction, which is more than you need, because high-order interactions are almost always small.

Fractional factorial gives up high-order interactions to buy runs. A 2^(7−3) design is 16 runs for seven factors; the price is aliasing — each estimated effect is a sum of one main effect and some interactions, and the resolution names what is confounded with what. Resolution III aliases main effects with two-factor interactions and is for screening only; resolution IV keeps main effects clean but aliases two-factor interactions with each other; resolution V keeps both clean. Report the resolution and the defining relation, because a resolution III design whose resolution is unstated is indistinguishable from a careless experiment.

Plackett–Burman is the maximally efficient screening design: Plackett and Burman, The design of optimum multifactorial experiments, Biometrika 33:305 (1946), verified. Run counts are multiples of four, so 12 runs screen up to 11 factors and 20 runs up to 19. It is resolution III with complex partial aliasing, which makes it superb for ranking main effects and useless for interactions — which is precisely the screening job. It is also the design the microbial media-optimisation literature runs on, almost always followed by Box–Behnken or a central composite design on the survivors, so being fluent in this pair is being fluent in how papers in your field are built.

Worked example. Ten medium components to screen. OFAT at three replicates: 60 runs and no interaction information. Full factorial: 1,024. Plackett–Burman with 12 runs plus 3 centre points: 15 runs, which fits one plate and one afternoon, and returns a ranked list of main effects with an error estimate from the centre points. Take the three largest forward to §7. The screening stage is not trying to find the optimum; it is trying to discard seven factors, and discarding is cheap.

Include centre points, always. Three or four replicated centre points give a pure-error estimate independent of the model and a first test for curvature — if the centre response sits well off the mean of the factorial corners, the response is curved and a first-order model will mislead you, which is the signal to go to §7.

Where the book stops. Montgomery §7-2, §7-3 and §7-5 are the factorial, 2^k and fractional-factorial core, and §7-4.2 is blocking and confounding. Plackett-Burman is not in this book — it is in Montgomery's Design and Analysis of Experiments — and pyDOE3.pbdesign implements it. Read the book for the aliasing arithmetic; read the software for the design matrix.

7. Response surface methodology, and the lack-of-fit test most papers skip

RSM is for the second stage only: a few factors already known to matter, a continuous response, and a region believed to contain an optimum. The model is a second-order polynomial in the factors — main effects, two-factor interactions and pure quadratic terms — fitted by least squares, not because biology is quadratic but because a quadratic is the simplest surface with an interior optimum.

First, get into the right region, which this handbook previously skipped. A second-order design fitted far from the optimum fits a plane with curvature nobody needs. The classical path is the method of steepest ascent (Montgomery §7-6.1): fit a first-order model from the screening factorial, step along its gradient in the coded factors, measure as you go, and stop when the response stops improving — then run the second-order design there. The signal that you have arrived is the one §6 already gave: centre points sitting well off the factorial mean, which is curvature. Montgomery §7-6.2 is the analysis of the second-order model that follows, and §7-4.1 is the centre-point addition that detects the curvature in the first place. Running an RSM without first locating the region is the commonest way to produce a well-fitted model of the wrong place.

The two standard designs. A central composite design adds axial (star) points at ±α and centre points to a two-level factorial, giving five levels per factor; with α = (2^k)^(1/4) it is rotatable, meaning prediction variance depends only on distance from the centre. A Box–Behnken design — Box and Behnken, Some new three level designs for the study of quantitative variables, Technometrics 2:455 (1960), verified — uses three levels per factor and no corner points, so it never requires running all factors at their extremes simultaneously, which matters when the corner is a medium nobody should autoclave or a temperature the organism will not survive. For three factors, Box–Behnken is 12 design points plus centre points — 15 runs with three centres — against a central composite's 8 factorial plus 6 axial points, so 17 runs with the same three centres and 20 with the six a rotatable CCD is usually published with. State the centre points whenever you quote either count, because 15 against 20 is not like-for-like and 15 against 17 is.

The lack-of-fit test is the part to get right, because it is the part routinely omitted. With replicated centre points you can partition the residual sum of squares into pure error, from the replicates, and lack of fit, from the model's failure to pass through the group means. Their mean-square ratio is an F statistic: a significant lack of fit means the quadratic is the wrong model and its optimum is an artefact, whatever the R² says. A published RSM reporting R² = 0.98 and no lack-of-fit test is reporting that it fitted ten coefficients to fifteen points, which it will always do well. Count them, because the count is what makes the degrees of freedom add up: a three-factor quadratic has one intercept, three linear, three quadratic and three two-factor interaction terms, so 15 − 10 = 5 residual degrees of freedom — exactly the residual df in the worked example below.

Worked example of why R² is not enough. A 15-run Box–Behnken, residual SS 0.84 on 5 degrees of freedom, of which pure error from three centre points is 0.06 on 2 df and lack of fit is 0.78 on 3 df. Mean squares: 0.03 and 0.26, so F = 8.7 against F(3,2) at 5% ≈ 19.2 — not significant, so the model survives, but only because two degrees of freedom is almost no power to detect lack of fit. Had the design carried five centre points instead of three, the same ratio would be tested on 4 df and the critical value falls to about 6.6, and the model would fail. The conclusion to carry: the number of centre points is what buys you the ability to detect a wrong model, and three is the minimum rather than the right answer.

Then check the optimum is real. Canonical analysis of the fitted quadratic tells you whether the stationary point is a maximum, a minimum or a saddle; a saddle is common and means there is no interior optimum, only a ridge. And confirm the predicted optimum by running it — a predicted optimum that has never been measured is a model output, not a result.

8. Regression diagnostics and nonlinear fitting

This is the shortest section here because the course already covers most of it: week 30 is parameter identifiability and ensembles, week 29 is the chi-squared goodness-of-fit with its degrees of freedom, and the machine learning handbook §6–§8 covers splits, leakage and calibrated intervals. What belongs here is the residual discipline that applies to every fit in both.

Look at residuals against fitted values, against each predictor, and in run order. Structure in the first means the model form is wrong; a funnel means the error is multiplicative and the response should be log-transformed; structure in run order means a drift the design did not block. A normal quantile plot is the least informative of the four and the one most often shown.

Report parameter intervals, and say where they came from. Linearised standard errors from the covariance matrix assume the model is linear near the optimum and are usually too narrow; the bootstrap makes fewer assumptions and takes more code; a profile likelihood is the right tool when parameters trade off, which week 30 shows they generally do. Never quote a fitted parameter without an interval, and never quote the interval without naming which of the three produced it.

R² is not a goodness-of-fit test for a nonlinear model and barely is for a linear one. Use it to describe, never to validate. Validation is lack of fit against pure error (§7), a chi-squared against the measurement error (week 29), or held-out prediction (ML handbook §6).

When the outcome is binary or a count

A titre is continuous; a hit is not, and neither is a contamination event or a colony count. Linear regression on a proportion can predict below zero or above one, and its intervals are wrong. Use a logistic regression for a binary outcome and a Poisson or negative-binomial regression for a count; both are generalized linear models, and Gelman and Hill Ch 5–6 is the applied introduction. For a screen, the coefficient is a log odds ratio; for a count, it is a log rate ratio, and an offset carries the exposure (wells, volume, reads).

Two things to check. Overdispersion: if the variance of a count exceeds its mean, the Poisson standard errors are too small; use a quasi-Poisson or negative binomial, or a multilevel model with a batch random effect (Gelman and Hill Ch 15). Separation: if a batch has no hits at all, the logistic coefficient diverges; a weakly informative prior or a multilevel model is the fix, not more iterations.

This is the model behind §15's multilevel logistic example and the right default for a screen with batch variation: a varying intercept for plate, then the treatment effect with its interval. Gelman and Hill Ch 14 is the multilevel logistic chapter.

9. Reporting, and the statistics table a supervisor can check

Every figure or table reporting a comparison should answer, in its caption or a methods line: what the replicates were, biological or technical, and how many; what test, including Welch versus Student and whether the data were transformed; what correction for multiplicity, over how many comparisons; the effect size with its interval, in the units of the measurement or as a fold change; and the software and version. That is six items and it fits in two sentences.

Two conventions worth adopting. State the n in every caption, because it is the first thing a reader checks and the thing most often absent. And plot the data, not the summary — for n below about ten, show every point with the mean, not a bar with an error bar whose definition the reader has to guess. If a bar chart with error bars is unavoidable, say in the caption whether the bars are SD, SEM or a confidence interval; at n = 4 the SEM is half the SD and the 95% interval is 3.2 times the SEM, so the same four numbers can be drawn with bars differing more than threefold — which is the difference between a convincing figure and an honest one.

The book's reporting material. Montgomery §2-1 is the numerical summary and §2-2 to §2-5 the plots, §4-9 and §5-7 the summary tables that say which procedure goes with which design, and §6-2.5 and §6-3.3 the residual checks a caption should mention. It gives no reporting checklist; the six items above are this handbook's, assembled from what those sections imply.

Gelman and Hill for the display. Ch 21 is summarising and displaying fitted models, including uncertainty on the fitted curve rather than only on the coefficients; Ch 24 is model checking and comparison. Montgomery gives the summary table; Gelman and Hill gives the plot a reader can interpret.

10. Statistical process control: the half of statistics that is about a running process

Everything above is about experiments, where you set the factors and then analyse. A fermenter in operation is the other case: the factors are held fixed and the question is whether the process is behaving as it did yesterday. That is statistical process control, it appears nowhere else in this course, and it is the part of statistics closest to what a process engineer already does.

The core idea, and it is a hypothesis test repeated forever. A control chart plots a statistic against time with limits at ±3 standard deviations of that statistic, and the limits come from the process's own history rather than from a specification. Montgomery Ch 8 is the reference: §8-2.1 basic principles, §8-2.2 design of a chart, §8-2.3 rational subgroups, §8-2.4 patterns, §8-3 the x̄ and R charts, §8-4 charts for individual measurements, §8-5 process capability and §8-6 attribute charts.

Three things worth taking from it even if you never draw a chart.

Common cause against special cause. Variation inside the limits is the process being itself and chasing it makes things worse — the formal name for that is tampering, and it is what adjusting a fermenter's pH setpoint after one off reading does. Variation outside the limits has a findable cause. Deciding which you are looking at before acting is the whole discipline.

Rational subgrouping is the same idea as §1's replicate structure, arriving from the other side: the subgroup must be chosen so that the variation within it is only common cause, and the thing you are trying to detect shows up between subgroups. Three samples from one batch is a subgroup; one sample from each of three batches is not, and swapping them changes what the chart can see.

Process capability (§8-5) is not the same as being in control. A process can be perfectly stable and consistently out of specification. The capability indices compare the process's own spread against the specification width, and the pairing to remember is that control is about stability over time while capability is about meeting a requirement — a fermentation can be beautifully reproducible at a titre nobody can sell, which is week 52's finding stated in another vocabulary.

Where it earns its place in this plan. The seed train and the production batch of weeks 47-51 are repeated processes with measurable outputs — final titre, batch time, peak oxygen uptake, offline glucose — and a chart on any of them distinguishes a strain problem from a medium batch from an operator change. For a candidate with fermentation experience this is the cheapest credibility in the portfolio: almost no biology applicant can say what a rational subgroup is, and anyone who has run a plant can.

13. Bayesian parameter estimation, and why week 30 asks for an ensemble

The problem this solves. Least squares gives a point estimate and, if you are lucky, an interval from a linearisation. Week 30's models have ten to twenty parameters and forty measurements, and the likelihood surface is a curved ridge; the linearisation is wrong exactly where the answer matters. The posterior is the ensemble week 30 asks you to report, computed rather than assembled by hand.

Three objects, one sentence each. The prior is what you believe about a parameter before the data, stated as a distribution and defensible from BRENDA, a previous experiment or a physical bound. The likelihood is the error model: how the points would scatter around the model if the parameters were true, usually normal with a stated sigma. The posterior is the product, normalised, and it is the object you report. A 95% credible interval contains the parameter with 95% posterior probability given the model and prior; a confidence interval is a statement about repeated experiments. They agree only in the large-sample normal case, which week 30 is not.

How to compute it. For one to three parameters, a grid or a Laplace approximation at the mode is enough and worth doing once by hand. For more, use Markov-chain Monte Carlo: Metropolis-Hastings if you want to see the machinery, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (NUTS) if you want it to work. emcee is a good default for a small model; pymc if you want priors, hierarchy and posterior predictive checks in one place. Run at least four chains from different starts, check R-hat below 1.01 and effective sample size above 400, and look at the traces. Then sample the posterior predictive distribution and compare it with the data; a posterior that fits the parameters but not the data is a misspecified likelihood.

Worked example. Fit Vmax and KM to twelve progress-curve points with 10% noise. Least squares gives Vmax 4.8 ± 0.6 and KM 120 ± 40 µM, symmetric by construction. A Bayesian fit with a log-normal prior on KM centred at 100 µM and a weak prior on Vmax gives a posterior whose KM interval is 85–210 µM and whose Vmax–KM correlation is -0.8. The correlation is the result: the data determine a combination of the two, not each separately, which is the sloppiness week 30 describes. Report the joint posterior, or at least the correlation, not two marginal intervals that imply independence.

How it fails. A prior that is doing the work and is not stated; an unidentifiable model, where the posterior is a ridge and every marginal is wide; non-convergence hidden by a single chain; a credible interval reported as a confidence interval; and a likelihood that assumes constant variance when the assay error is multiplicative — fit on the log scale or model the variance.

Where the book stops. Montgomery is frequentist. §4-3.3 is the p-value and §4-4.5 and §4-5.3 the confidence intervals that this section replaces with a posterior; the book has no Bayesian chapter, which is why Beers Ch 8 is the source for the method. Use Montgomery for the contrast, not for the computation.

What to read. Beers Ch 8 is the method at the right level, with MATLAB code to read rather than run; Gelman et al., Bayesian Data Analysis for depth; the emcee and pymc documentation for implementation. Gelman and Hill Ch 18 is likelihood and Bayesian inference and computation, Ch 16–17 the Bugs/R implementation, and Ch 19 convergence debugging; read Ch 18 before Ch 16 if the terms are new. Do this against week 30's model, not a toy.

Exercise. Take the two-parameter model from Brief 1 or week 30, fit it by least squares and by MCMC, and write one page on where the intervals disagree and why.

14. Designs when each run is expensive

The problem. §6 and §7 assume you can afford fifteen to thirty runs. A 50 m³ campaign gives you four to eight, each a week of facility time, and the batch is the unit. The design question is no longer which fraction of a factorial to run; it is which few runs buy the most information about the decision you have to make.

Design for a model, not a space. A D-optimal design chooses the runs that maximise the determinant of X-transpose-X for the model you intend to fit; A-optimal minimises the average variance of the coefficients; I-optimal minimises the average prediction variance over the region you care about. The choice matters: a D-optimal design for a quadratic in three factors needs ten runs and is saturated; with six runs you cannot fit a quadratic, and no design fixes that. Decide the model first, then the design.

Sequential and adaptive. With expensive runs, do not fix all of them in advance. Run a small screening design, fit, and choose the next run by expected information gain or by the D-optimal update. Bayesian adaptive design formalises this: the next run is the one that most reduces uncertainty in the quantity you will decide on. It is the same logic as Bayesian optimisation (ML handbook §9), with the acquisition function chosen for information rather than for the best predicted value.

Worked example. Three factors, six runs, a quadratic model with ten coefficients. The model is unidentifiable; the honest options are (a) six runs for a screening model in three main effects plus three interactions, (b) seven runs for a saturated quadratic in two factors with the third held, or (c) a sequential plan: four runs, fit, then two runs chosen to discriminate the remaining candidates. Write down which option you chose and why; a reviewer will ask why six and not twelve.

Cost-aware design. Replication is not free and not always the best buy. One extra batch replicate buys more than one extra flask within a batch, because the batch is the independent unit. A control arm alongside the optimised runs is usually worth 5–10% of the budget, because without it you cannot tell improvement from drift. Block by batch, randomise within batch, and pre-register the stopping rule.

How it fails. Optimising a design for the wrong model; ignoring batch and treating runs as independent; stopping when the result looks good, which is a sequential design with a biased stopping rule; and using a D-optimal design for a model with more parameters than runs, which is not a design but a hope.

What to read. Box, Hunter and Hunter, Statistics for Experimenters, chapters on optimal and sequential design; Montgomery Ch 7 for the classical designs this section builds on; Myers and Montgomery, Response Surface Methodology, for D-optimal and adaptive designs; pyDOE3 for the matrices; ML handbook §9 for the acquisition-function view.

Exercise. Take the 50 m³ campaign from week 49 and design six runs to choose between two feeding strategies. State the model, the optimality criterion, the blocking, and the result that would change your mind.

15. Nested data: mixed models, time series and survival

The problem §1 simplifies. Three batches, four flasks each, is twelve measurements and three independent units. A t-test on twelve treats the flasks as independent, inflates the degrees of freedom and shrinks the interval; the batch is a random effect, and ignoring it is the commonest pseudoreplication in bioprocess data. §1's blocking removes one nuisance factor; it does not model a hierarchy.

Mixed-effects models. Fixed effects are what you want to estimate — strain, medium, time. Random effects are the units you sampled — batch, bioreactor, operator, plate. A random intercept for batch says each batch starts at a different level; a random slope says the effect of strain differs by batch. Fit with statsmodels.formula.api.mixedlm or MixedLM.from_formula, report the fixed effects with intervals, the variance components, and the intraclass correlation, the fraction of variance due to batch. With fewer than five levels, a random effect is not estimable; treat batch as a fixed block instead.

Worked example. Three batches by four flasks, mutant against parent. A pooled t-test gives a 1.4-fold difference with a 95% interval of 1.15–1.70. A mixed model with batch as a random intercept gives 1.4-fold, 1.05–1.86, and an intraclass correlation of 0.45: nearly half the variance is between batches. The effect survives, but the interval is wider and the claim is honest. If the correlation is near zero, the pooled test was not wrong; if it is high, the pooled test was overconfident.

Time series. Repeated measurements on the same vessel are autocorrelated; a t-test on the endpoint throws away the trajectory and assumes independence between time points. If the question is about the trajectory — oscillation, drift, response to a feed pulse — model it: plot, detrend, inspect the autocorrelation function, fit an AR(1) or a state-space model, and report the coefficient with its interval. Irregular sampling is normal; state the time base and do not interpolate silently.

Survival analysis. When the outcome is time to an event — contamination, loss of productivity, failure of a stability study — censoring is the point. A run still clean at day 30 is not missing data; it is censored at 30 days. Kaplan-Meier gives the survival curve, Cox proportional hazards gives the effect of a covariate as a hazard ratio, and lifelines does both. Do not drop censored runs; that biases the estimate upward.

How it fails. Ignoring nesting; random effects with three levels; random slopes with too few units; overfitting a mixed model with more terms than batches; treating censored data as missing; fitting an AR model to irregular samples without stating the time base; and using survival methods when the event is not well defined.

Where the book stops. Montgomery §2-5 is the time-series plot, §3-5.4 the Weibull distribution and §8-4 the individuals chart; it has no mixed-model, ARIMA or survival chapter. The references below are the sources for those methods, not alternatives to the book — read Montgomery for the plots and the distribution, then go to Gelman and Hill and lifelines for the models.

What to read. Gelman and Hill, Data Analysis Using Regression and Multilevel/Hierarchical Models; the statsmodels mixed-model documentation; the lifelines documentation; Gelman and Hill Ch 11–15 are the multilevel depth — structures, varying intercepts, varying slopes, multilevel logistic and GLM — and Ch 25 is missing data; the book has no time-series or survival chapter, which is why those references are separate. Box, Jenkins and Reinsel for time series.

Exercise. Take the nested design from §1, simulate it with a batch intraclass correlation of 0.5, and compare the pooled t-test with the mixed model. Then take a contamination time course and fit a Kaplan-Meier curve with censoring.

16. Causal inference and missing data

Why this section exists. Every comparison in §§3–7 is associational. "The mutant is better" is a statement about the flasks you measured; "the mutation caused the improvement" is a causal claim, and it needs a design and an assumption. The same data can support the first and not the second, and a supervisor will ask which one you are making.

The causal question, stated properly. For each unit there is an outcome under treatment and an outcome under control; you observe one. The difference is the causal effect, and it is not estimable from the observed difference unless the groups are exchangeable. Randomisation buys exchangeability; blocking buys it within blocks; a regression adjustment buys it only if you have measured every confounder. Name the treatment, the outcome, the unit and the confounders before you fit anything. Gelman and Hill Ch 9 does this for a single-level regression; Ch 23 does it when the units are nested.

What goes wrong in a bioprocess. Batch, medium lot, operator and time are confounded with the strain when the mutant is run in a different week. A naive comparison across campaigns estimates strain plus batch plus season. A block or a batch fixed effect removes the measured part; a multilevel model with a batch random effect does the same with partial pooling; neither removes an unmeasured confounder. The honest sentence is: the difference is 1.4-fold after adjusting for batch, and it would be smaller if the batches differed in an unmeasured way that also affected the parent. Gelman and Hill Ch 9–10 and Ch 23 give the vocabulary and the sensitivity analysis.

Missing data is not a nuisance; it is a bias. A run dropped because it looked contaminated, a well with no read, a batch excluded because the feed pump failed: complete-case analysis uses the runs that survived, and the survivors are not a random sample. The three labels are MCAR, missing completely at random; MAR, missing depends on observed variables; and MNAR, missing depends on the unobserved value. Complete-case analysis is unbiased only under MCAR; multiple imputation is the standard under MAR; MNAR needs a sensitivity analysis, not a fix. Impute predictors and covariates, not the outcome you are trying to explain, and include the outcome in the imputation model even if you do not impute it. Gelman and Hill Ch 25 is the practical reference.

Worked example. A 96-well screen with 20% of wells missing and a batch-confounded hit. Complete-case analysis gives a 2.1-fold hit enrichment; multiple imputation with batch and plate as predictors gives 1.6-fold with a wider interval; a multilevel logistic model with a plate random intercept gives 1.7-fold. The difference between 2.1 and 1.6 is the missingness, not the biology. Report the three and say which assumption each rests on.

How it fails. Conditioning on a collider, a variable caused by both treatment and outcome; adjusting for a mediator and calling it a confounder; imputing the outcome; treating censored runs as missing when the event is the outcome, which is §15's survival model; and a causal claim from an observational comparison with no sensitivity analysis.

What to read. Gelman and Hill Ch 9 and 10 for the single-level causal material, Ch 23 for the multilevel version, Ch 25 for missing data. The book's examples are social-science; the logic transfers, the units do not.

Exercise. Take the nested design from §15, delete 20% of the observations at random and another 20% not at random, and compare complete-case, multiple imputation and the multilevel model. Write one paragraph on which estimate you would defend and why.

17. What this handbook still will not give you

It is not a statistics education and does not substitute for one if your project turns out to be methodological. It now covers the frequentist core, Bayesian parameter estimation (§13), designs for expensive experiments (§14), and nested, time-series and survival models (§15), and causal inference and missing data (§16) at the level a strain engineer needs. It does not cover multivariate omics methods such as PCA and PLS-DA, meta-analysis, measurement-error models, competing risks, or the full hierarchical Bayesian treatment of a multi-site campaign. Those are a reading list, not a gap to apologise for: Gelman and Hill for the hierarchy, Gelman et al. for the Bayesian depth, and the primary methods paper for whatever your project actually needs.

One sentence worth being able to say aloud, and meaning it: I can tell you how many flasks this needs before I run it, and what the result would have to be before I believed it.
